The Social Brain Dispatch

The Number That Was Too Good to Trust

In 2015 a Swedish team predicted, with ninety two percent accuracy from twenty three patients, who would still be well a year after internet therapy for social anxiety, using nothing but the coupling between two regions on a criticism task. In 2024 a team that includes the scientist who first proved neuroscience routinely manufactures numbers exactly this good built a computational account of the same disorder in twelve hundred fifty seven people, and left the brain out of it entirely.

Edition No. 12 · Neural Methods and Computational Accounts · August 27, 2026

Computational psychiatry sells itself on a promise that sounds almost administrative: give the field a formal account of the latent process that generates a symptom, and both halves of psychiatry’s oldest problem, prediction and explanation, come free together. Predict who will respond to a given treatment and you can stop wasting nine weeks of a frightened person’s life on an intervention that was never going to work for them. Explain the process that generates the prediction and you can, in principle, intervene on it directly rather than waiting to see whether therapy happens to move it. What the field has instead, twenty years into taking that promise seriously, is a stubborn split between studies that are good at one half and studies that are good at the other, almost never both, and almost never for reasons anyone advertises. Two papers on social anxiety disorder, a decade apart, sit on opposite sides of that split with unusual clarity. One is a small, elegant, single-site neuroimaging study that used a multivariate classifier to predict, with an accuracy figure so high it should make any experienced reader suspicious on sight, which patients would still be well a year after guided internet therapy. The other is a pair of enormous, preregistered behavioral studies, more than twelve hundred participants combined, that built a formal computational model of exactly the belief-updating process social anxiety is supposed to distort, got a genuinely interesting mechanistic dissociation out of it, and never once put anyone in a scanner. Read only the first and you get a biomarker with no theory behind it strong enough to explain why it works. Read only the second and you get a theory with no anchor in the tissue it claims to describe. Read them together, with the second paper’s own co-author having spent the intervening decade telling the field exactly why the first paper’s number should not be trusted at face value, and something considerably more interesting than either paper alone emerges, a portrait of a subfield still deciding whether it would rather be right or be reliable.

Tomas Furmark has been doing this particular kind of work long enough that he shows up twice in this publication’s own archive under two different research identities. In 2001, as a co-author on the PET study of cerebral blood flow during stressful public speaking that this Dispatch covered in its third edition, Furmark belonged to the generation of Swedish affective neuroscientists, working out of Karolinska and Uppsala under Mats Fredrikson, who established with univariate, group-level contrasts that the amygdala and related limbic structures light up disproportionately in social phobics asked to perform in front of an audience. A year later Furmark co-authored what became one of the field’s most cited convergence findings, that citalopram and cognitive behavioral therapy, chemically and procedurally about as different as two treatments for the same disorder can be, produce strikingly overlapping reductions in that same regional blood flow, a result that did more than almost any single paper to establish the premise that talk therapy and medication for social phobia are not two different repair jobs but two different routes to the same neural endpoint. That premise is the quiet foundation the 2015 paper under discussion here is built on, and by 2015 Furmark’s own research program had shifted from asking what changes in the brain after successful treatment to asking whether the pre-treatment brain can tell you, in advance, who successful treatment is even going to happen to. The shift tracks a broader move in his group toward internet-delivered therapy, developed over the 2000s in close partnership with Gerhard Andersson and Per Carlbring, two of the field’s principal architects of guided self-help delivered almost entirely online, a treatment modality that scales in a way conventional face-to-face CBT for a disorder this common never could, but whose scalability creates its own new problem, that a clinician has far less ongoing contact with a given patient and correspondingly less clinical feel for who is likely to actually benefit. A predictive biomarker is not, for this group, an academic curiosity. It is a triage instrument for a treatment delivery system explicitly designed to have fewer humans in the loop.

Kristoffer Månsson, the paper’s first author, represents the half of the collaboration that made this particular study possible rather than merely another PET convergence paper, the multivariate half. Where Furmark’s earlier work asked whether a region, averaged across a group, differs between conditions, Månsson’s later career, which has gone on to include leadership roles in the ENIGMA-Anxiety mega-analytic consortium and work on moment-to-moment brain signal variability as a treatment biomarker, is organized around a different question entirely, whether the pattern of activity within an individual brain, not the group mean, carries enough information to say something useful about that one person. The 2015 study sits at exactly the hinge between those two research identities, using a task Furmark’s tradition would recognize instantly, participants read two hundred sixteen sentences over roughly seventeen minutes, varying in valence, positive, negative, or neutral, and in referential target, directed at the self, at a specific named other woman, or at a specific named other man, a design meant to isolate self-referential emotional processing from emotional processing more generally, and then analyzing the resulting BOLD patterns with a technique Furmark’s generation would not have used, a support vector machine trained to classify individual patients rather than to test for a group difference.

The cohort itself is small even by the standards of a field that runs chronically underpowered, twenty six patients with a confirmed DSM-IV diagnosis of social anxiety disorder, mean age just over thirty two, eighty five percent of the generalized subtype, entering a thirteen week protocol that combined guided internet-delivered CBT with attention bias modification in a crossover design, twenty three of whom were followed all the way to a one year outcome assessment. Just over half, twelve of the twenty three, met criteria as long-term responders. The classifier's job was to take each patient's baseline pattern of BOLD response to the self-referential criticism task, restricted a priori to two theoretically motivated regions, the dorsal anterior cingulate cortex and the amygdala, and predict, using leave one out cross validation, which of those twenty three would end up in the responder group a full year later. It got the classification right often enough to reach ninety two percent accuracy, with a confidence interval running from seventy three to just under ninety eight percent and a permutation-derived significance of p equals .001, and the specific pattern driving that prediction was informative in its own right, lower coupling between the dorsal ACC and the amygdala at baseline predicted a better long-term outcome, while none of the standard clinical or demographic variables collected alongside the scan, symptom severity, age, subtype, predicted response at all. Read at face value, this is a genuinely striking result, that the strength of the functional link between a threat-detection structure and a region implicated in cognitive and affective conflict monitoring, measured once before treatment even begins, outperforms everything a clinician can observe or ask about in a first session.

Read with the skepticism the field itself has spent the past decade cultivating, the number deserves to be treated as a hypothesis rather than a fact. Multivariate classifiers trained and cross-validated on samples this small are exactly the design the neuroscience methodology literature has spent years flagging as prone to wildly optimistic, frequently non-replicating accuracy estimates, not because leave one out cross validation is invalid in principle but because with twenty three cases split across the leave-out folds, a handful of atypical individuals can swing the reported accuracy by many percentage points in either direction, and because the specific ROI pair, dorsal ACC and amygdala, though chosen for defensible theoretical reasons rather than mined from the whole brain, was still one analytic choice among several plausible ones the authors could have made, with no independent replication cohort available to show the same two-region signature recurring. None of this means the finding is wrong. It means a reader has no principled way, from this study alone, to know whether ninety two percent reflects a real and clinically usable signal or an artifact of exactly the kind of small, underpowered design that the second paper in this edition exists specifically to correct for.

Katherine Button did not set out to build a computational model of social anxiety. Her name belongs to the field for a different reason entirely, as the lead author of a 2013 Nature Reviews Neuroscience paper, written with John Ioannidis, Marcus Munafò, and colleagues, that estimated the median statistical power of studies across neuroscience at somewhere between eight and thirty one percent, and demonstrated with unusual rigor a fact the field had mostly preferred not to look at directly, that underpowered studies do not merely fail to detect true effects at an acceptable rate, they also systematically inflate the size of the effects they do report, because only the inflated, lucky draws from a noisy small sample clear the bar for statistical significance in the first place. That paper became, within a few years, one of the most cited methodological critiques in the history of the discipline, precisely because it gave a name and a mechanism to a suspicion many researchers already held privately about their own field's most celebrated small-sample findings. It is not an accident that a scientist who built her reputation on that critique would, a decade later, co-author a study of social anxiety and depression built around two datasets totaling more than twelve hundred participants, a mega-analysis pooling four hundred fifty individuals from prior studies and a fully preregistered replication sample of eight hundred seven more. The design is close to a direct rebuke, whether intended as one or not, of exactly the kind of small, high-variance, cross-validated classifier that made Månsson and colleagues' number possible.

The theoretical machinery Button's co-authors brought to the collaboration has its own distinct lineage, running not through neuroimaging at all but through the computational modeling of self-esteem developed largely at University College London's Max Planck Centre for Computational Psychiatry and Ageing Research. Michael Moutoussis, the paper's third author, has spent much of the past decade extending a line of work originating with Robb Rutledge, Peter Dayan, and Raymond Dolan, which formalized subjective well-being and self-esteem as the output of a continuously updated belief, tracked trial by trial as a function of the prediction errors generated when expected social or material outcomes turn out better or worse than expected. Moutoussis's own contribution to that lineage has been to push it toward psychopathology specifically, modeling how a person's beliefs about their own social standing, and about how quickly that standing is changing, get built and revised from moment to moment, and asking what happens to that updating process when the person carries elevated depression or social anxiety. Janina Hoffmann, the paper's first author, arrived at the collaboration from a third direction again, a background in the cognitive modeling of memory-based and information-based decision making built at Basel and Konstanz before she joined Button as a colleague in decision science at Bath, bringing a methodologist's insistence on rigorous model comparison, testing not just whether a single preferred model fits the data but whether it fits reliably better than a defined set of plausible competitors, to a question the self-esteem literature had mostly approached with a single model already in hand.

What that combination of expertise produced is a computational account built from a genuinely large behavioral dataset rather than a single small scan. Participants across both samples completed a forced-choice probabilistic learning task in which they chose between two evaluative words, selecting the one more likely to indicate that a partner, sometimes a real algorithmic partner, sometimes a fictional other, liked them, under three feedback rules, a positive rule paying out correct feedback sixty to eighty percent of the time, a negative rule paying out only twenty to forty percent of the time, and a neutral rule at fifty percent, run in both self-referential and other-referential versions so that the researchers could separate how a person learns about their own standing from how the same person learns about someone else's. The best-fitting computational model to emerge from formal comparison against alternatives was a trait belief-updating architecture, carrying a stable prior belief about the self and a separate prior about others, an updating rate governing how strongly a given trial's prediction error moved that belief, and a decay parameter pulling the belief back toward its trait baseline between updates, which let the authors define, cleanly, an optimism bias score for each participant, the degree to which positive social feedback was learned more readily than negative social feedback of matched informational value.

The results split depression and social anxiety apart in a way neither disorder's existing cognitive-behavioral literature predicts especially cleanly. Both symptom dimensions were associated with reduced optimism bias overall, depression the more strongly of the two, beta of negative point two five, ninety five percent confidence interval running from negative point three four to negative point one five, social anxiety somewhat less strongly at beta negative point one nine, interval negative point two eight to negative point one, both comfortably below p equals .001 given the sample sizes involved. But the route to that shared endpoint diverged sharply once the authors looked past the single summary statistic. Social anxiety was specifically associated with fewer errors on the negative feedback rule in the self-referential condition, beta negative point one seven, p equals .001, meaning socially anxious participants were not worse at learning negative information about themselves, they were better at it, unusually efficient, high-fidelity learners of exactly the feedback that told them they were disliked. Depression showed the mirror-image signature instead, more errors specifically on the positive feedback rule, beta point two eight, p less than .001, a genuine blunting of the ability to extract and use good news, and one that showed up regardless of whether the good news concerned the self or someone else, a general dampening of positive learning rather than a self-specific one. Positive trait beliefs about the self, the model's stable prior term, independently predicted lower symptoms of both conditions, beta negative point one eight for social anxiety, negative point two two for depression, but through what the authors argue are functionally distinct mechanisms, a reduced baseline of self-regard sitting downstream of hypervigilant negative-feedback learning in social anxiety, and sitting downstream of a general positivity deficit in depression.

Set beside each other, the two studies describe the same disorder from opposite ends of a chain that neither has walked all the way through. Månsson and colleagues found that a specific pattern of coupling between a threat structure and an appraisal structure, measured while a socially anxious patient sat with sentences criticizing them, predicts with striking apparent precision who will still be well a year later, but their design offers no account of what, computationally, that coupling is doing, no process model of what information is moving between amygdala and dorsal ACC or what asymmetry in that exchange might explain why weaker coupling favors a better outcome. Hoffmann, Hobbs, Moutoussis, and Button found a strikingly specific and well-powered computational answer to almost exactly that question, that social anxiety's signature is not a general learning deficit but an efficient, high-fidelity negative-learning system paired with an eroded positive baseline, but their design offers no bridge back to the brain at all, no test of whether the trait belief-updating parameters their model estimates from choice data correspond to anything measurable in cortical or subcortical activity, let alone to the specific dACC-amygdala coupling Månsson's group had already flagged as clinically predictive nine years earlier. The natural hypothesis the two studies jointly suggest, and that neither one tests, is that dACC-amygdala coupling during self-referential criticism is the neural implementation of exactly the asymmetric error-weighting Hoffmann's model estimates behaviorally, an appraisal system staying too tightly locked to a threat detector because it is, at the algorithmic level, assigning too much weight to negative self-relevant prediction errors relative to positive ones, and that patients whose coupling loosens over the course of therapy are patients whose negative-learning gain has come back down toward a level a positive prior can compete with. Nobody has run that study, a belief-updating task of Hoffmann's design administered inside a scanner to a clinically diagnosed sample large enough to trust, testing whether the negative-rule learning advantage that marks social anxiety in behavior maps onto the specific coupling reduction that marks good long-term outcome in Månsson's cohort. Until someone does, the field holds a highly reliable model of a process it cannot yet locate in the brain, and a highly localized, clinically compelling brain signature it cannot yet explain, and has no way of knowing whether the two are actually describing the same mechanism at all, or whether the appearance of convergence is simply what two well-executed studies of the same disorder are always going to look like from a comfortable distance.
