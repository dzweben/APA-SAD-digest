The Social Brain Dispatch

The Signature That Cannot Do Both Jobs at Once

In 2014 an Uppsala group trained a machine to separate fourteen socially phobic men from twelve healthy ones and got its best answer from gray matter volume, nowhere near the amygdala. The same year, a Chicago lab asked a simpler question of twenty one patients already carrying the diagnosis, which of them would get better on cognitive behavioral therapy, and found its answer sitting exactly in the wiring between the amygdala and the prefrontal cortex.

Edition No. 12 · Neural Methods and Computational Accounts · August 31, 2026

Two decades of group-average neuroimaging have produced a long, increasingly confident inventory of where the socially anxious brain differs from the unafraid one, an overactive amygdala, an under-modulating prefrontal cortex, an insula that will not stop signaling interoceptive alarm. This publication has now walked through several of those findings in detail, from the amygdala studies that founded the field to the insula-anterior-cingulate connectivity work that complicated them. What none of that inventory has ever, by itself, been able to answer is the question a clinician actually needs answered at the point of contact with a single patient, not whether social phobics as a group show more amygdala reactivity than controls as a group, but whether the scan in front of you belongs to someone with the disorder at all, and, if it does, whether the treatment you are about to offer is going to work. Those are two different questions, diagnosis and prognosis, and the assumption embedded in decades of biomarker rhetoric, that a good enough map of where the disorder lives in the brain will eventually answer both, is itself an empirical claim nobody has been forced to test directly. In 2014, two groups, working independently on opposite sides of the Atlantic, built the first real single-subject tests of that assumption for social anxiety disorder, one asking a machine to tell patients from controls using nothing but an individual brain, the other asking a much simpler statistical model to tell future responders from future non-responders using nothing but a single resting scan taken before treatment began. Read separately, each is a modest, methodologically careful paper reporting encouraging numbers. Read together, they expose something the field’s biomarker rhetoric tends to elide, that the brain signal best suited to answering one of these questions is not obviously the same signal best suited to answering the other, and that neither paper, on its own, had any way of noticing this.

The diagnostic paper came out of a research program this Dispatch has already met once before, in different form. Mats Fredrikson’s laboratory at Uppsala University spent much of the 1990s and 2000s establishing itself as one of the few groups in the world willing to put social phobia patients into a PET scanner and ask, at the level of regional cerebral blood flow, what a public speaking task actually does to a frightened brain, work that included the 2001 study already covered in these pages showing elevated blood flow in the amygdala and periamygdaloid cortex during anticipated public speaking. Tomas Furmark, Fredrikson’s longtime collaborator and by the 2000s a full professor in his own right, pushed that program in a direction few imaging labs of the era attempted, using neuroimaging not merely to describe pathology but to compare treatments, most famously in a study that found the same regional blood flow changes in the amygdala complex whether patients had improved on an SSRI or on cognitive behavioral therapy, a result that briefly made the Uppsala group's biomarker plausible as a shared final common pathway for otherwise very different interventions. By 2014, when this edition’s first paper appeared, the group's roster had absorbed a very different kind of expertise. Andrew Marquand, a methodologist trained in statistical pattern recognition rather than clinical psychology, joined as a coauthor, and his presence in the byline marks the moment this specific research lineage stopped merely describing where the group-average difference sits and started asking whether that difference carries any information at the level of one person. Marquand would go on, in the years after this paper, to become one of computational psychiatry’s more influential methodologists, building normative modeling approaches that map an individual patient’s brain against a population distribution rather than against a single control group mean, and this 2014 paper, with Andreas Frick as first author and Furmark and Fredrikson anchoring the senior end of the byline, reads in retrospect as an early, unglamorous rehearsal for that later, more ambitious program, the first time this particular clinical group let a genuine machine-learning method loose on its own data rather than simply subtracting one group's average activation map from another's.

What Frick, Marquand, and colleagues actually did was straightforward enough to describe and considerably harder to get right. Support vector machine classification, the workhorse of that era's neuroimaging pattern analysis, does not ask whether two groups differ on average, the question a standard second-level general linear model answers. It asks whether a decision boundary exists in a high-dimensional feature space, built from voxel-wise brain measurements, that correctly assigns each individual scan to its group, and it reports that performance the way any diagnostic test is reported, as balanced accuracy across a cross-validation scheme in which the model is trained on all but one subject and then tested on the subject it has never seen. That leave-one-out procedure is both the method's strength and its most exploitable weakness, an honest attempt to simulate seeing a new patient, but one that in small samples is well known to produce optimistic, unstable accuracy estimates that swing considerably depending on exactly which subjects happen to fall into the training set on any given fold. Fourteen men with social anxiety disorder, diagnosed by structured clinical interview, and twelve male controls, all scanned at 1.5 tesla while alternately viewing blocks of fearful and neutral faces, is precisely that kind of small sample, and it is worth being direct about what the size and composition of this cohort costs the study before crediting its results, an entirely male sample recruited at a single site, with two patients carrying subthreshold obsessive-compulsive symptoms and one carrying a snake phobia, a group small enough that a single unusual individual could plausibly move the reported accuracy by several percentage points in either direction.

Within those limits, the pattern the classifier actually found is more interesting, and more disruptive to a simple amygdala-centric story, than the paper's framing quite emphasizes. Restricting the functional classifier to a theoretically motivated fear network, amygdala, anterior cingulate, hippocampus, and insula, using the fearful-versus-baseline contrast, produced 72.6 percent balanced accuracy, comfortably above chance and modestly better than the 68.5 percent achieved letting the same functional contrast search the whole brain unconstrained. That is the result a reader steeped in this Dispatch's earlier editions would expect, theory-guided anatomy outperforming an unconstrained search. But the study's single best number, 84.5 percent balanced accuracy with an area under the curve of 0.91, came not from function at all but from regional gray matter volume searched across the whole brain, a structural measure with no task, no emotional content, and no amygdala-specific anatomical constraint built in, and restricting that same structural analysis to the theoretically motivated fear network actually collapsed its performance to chance, fifty percent. The fearful-versus-neutral contrast, arguably the theoretically purest test of an amygdala-specific threat response, failed to classify anyone above chance in any configuration the authors tried. Put plainly, the feature that best distinguished these particular patients from these particular controls was not a measure of fear circuit reactivity at all, but a distributed structural signature the fear-network hypothesis gave the classifier no special reason to expect, and the feature the fear-network hypothesis most directly motivates, task-evoked amygdala-complex activation to threat relative to safety, was the one measure that produced no diagnostic signal whatsoever.

The prognostic paper belongs to a different lineage this Dispatch has also already met, though from its other end. K. Luan Phan built his career, first at the University of Michigan and then for most of the 2000s and 2010s at the University of Illinois at Chicago, around a single sustained project, mapping how prefrontal cortex regulates, or fails to regulate, amygdala-driven threat responses across the anxiety and mood disorders, and Heide Klumpp spent much of that period as his closest and most consistent collaborator, first author on a long run of the lab's papers connecting specific circuit signatures to specific clinical phenotypes. This Dispatch has already sat with one product of that partnership, the 2012 study connecting insula reactivity and insula-anterior-cingulate connectivity to generalized social anxiety disorder, a paper squarely in the tradition of asking where, cross-sectionally, the disordered brain differs from the healthy one. The paper taken up here, published two years later with Klumpp again in the first-author position and Phan anchoring the senior end of the byline, keeps the same amygdala-centered circuitry the lab had spent a decade mapping but turns the entire question ninety degrees, away from diagnosis and toward outcome, asking not what distinguishes twenty one patients with generalized social anxiety disorder from anyone else, since the study enrolled no healthy comparison group at all, but what, measured in their brains before treatment began, distinguishes the patients among them who were about to get much better from the ones who were not.

The design is in some ways more modest than the diagnostic study and in one respect considerably more ambitious. Twenty one patients with generalized social anxiety disorder, diagnosed by structured clinical interview, with a mean baseline Liebowitz Social Anxiety Scale score of 71.6, solidly in the severe range, underwent an eight-minute resting-state scan, eyes open on a fixation cross, doing nothing at all, before beginning twelve weeks of manualized individual cognitive behavioral therapy built around psychoeducation, cognitive restructuring, and in vivo exposure. By the end of treatment the group's mean Liebowitz score had fallen to 51.5, a highly reliable drop, and seventy percent of patients met criteria for clinical response, figures broadly in line with what this literature generally reports for CBT in generalized social anxiety disorder. The analytic move that matters is what Klumpp and colleagues did with that single pre-treatment resting scan, seeding a whole-brain connectivity analysis at the bilateral amygdala and asking, across all twenty one patients, whether the strength of amygdala coupling to any other region at baseline, measured before a single session of therapy had occurred, predicted how much a given patient's symptoms would go on to fall over the following twelve weeks, with baseline severity statistically controlled so the model could not simply be recovering the trivial fact that more severe patients have more room to improve. The result was a small number of strikingly strong correlations concentrated almost entirely in circuitry this Dispatch has spent several editions establishing as central to the disorder's pathophysiology. Right amygdala connectivity to left pregenual anterior cingulate cortex predicted symptom change at r equals 0.55, left amygdala connectivity to the same pregenual and medial prefrontal territory predicted it at r equals 0.66, and right amygdala connectivity to bilateral insula predicted it more strongly still, r equals 0.75 and 0.74, each of these individual-difference correlations converted into a receiver operating characteristic area between 0.80 and 0.84 when the continuous connectivity measure was used to classify patients into responders and non-responders outright.

The direction of that effect deserves more scrutiny than the paper gives it. In every one of these pathways, it was greater baseline connectivity, stronger coupling between the amygdala and prefrontal or insular territory at rest, that predicted a better outcome, more symptom reduction, not less. That is at minimum a nontrivial fact to reconcile with the dominant story this same circuitry has been given elsewhere in the literature, in which weakened, insufficiently anti-correlated amygdala-prefrontal coupling during active emotion regulation is treated as the signature of dysfunction and the target regulation is supposed to strengthen. A resting, task-free correlation and a task-evoked regulatory coupling are not the same measurement, and Klumpp and colleagues are careful never to claim they are, but the paper does not offer much beyond that caveat toward explaining why more amygdala-prefrontal synchrony while a patient does nothing at all should forecast a better response to a therapy built around active cognitive reappraisal. One reading, consistent with a predictive-coding framing this Dispatch has used before, is that stronger intrinsic amygdala-prefrontal coupling at rest indexes an already-functioning communication channel between the regions that generate threat signals and the regions that are supposed to revise them, infrastructure CBT can then recruit and strengthen through repeated practice, whereas patients whose amygdala and prefrontal cortex are, at baseline, effectively not talking to each other have less circuitry available for a talk-based therapy to build on. That reading is plausible. It is also, on twenty one patients with no independent replication sample and no held-out test set, entirely unconfirmed, resting on correlations strong enough to be scientifically exciting and small enough in absolute sample size that a handful of unusual responders could again be doing a large share of the work.

Set beside each other honestly, these two studies do not converge on a single circuit-based biomarker for social anxiety disorder so much as they quietly divide the field's stated ambition into two separate jobs and hand each job to a different signal. The measure that carried Frick and colleagues' diagnostic classifier furthest, whole-brain gray matter volume, is by its nature a slowly changing structural property, exactly the kind of feature that has no obvious way of tracking whether a course of therapy is working, and it is telling that restricting the search to the theory-motivated fear network actively hurt this structural classifier's performance, as though whatever separates these patients from these controls anatomically is distributed well beyond the circuitry the disorder's own leading neural theory would nominate. The measure that carried Klumpp and colleagues' prognostic model, amygdala connectivity to prefrontal and insular cortex, is exactly the opposite kind of signal, state-dependent, plausibly modifiable by treatment, and by design incapable of ever being tested as a diagnostic marker in this study, since there was no comparison group against which to ask whether it also distinguishes patients from anyone else. Each paper's best result is, in a real sense, invisible to the other paper's question. A structural feature that best tells you who has the disorder tells you nothing, on its own terms, about who is about to get better. A functional connectivity pattern that best tells you who is about to get better was never tested, and arguably cannot be tested with this design, as a way of telling you who has the disorder in the first place. The blind spot each paper reveals in the other is not a flaw in either one's execution so much as a limit built into what a single study, however well designed, can ask of a single brain measurement.

That division carries real stakes for how this field talks about biomarkers, because the rhetoric surrounding computational and neuroimaging approaches to psychiatric illness has often proceeded as though a sufficiently rich, sufficiently multimodal brain signature would eventually do both jobs at once, diagnose the condition and forecast its response to treatment, as facets of the same underlying pathology. What these two 2014 papers suggest, read together rather than separately, is that this may be closer to a category error than an engineering problem awaiting a bigger sample, that the brain properties which discriminate a categorical diagnosis and the brain properties which forecast an individual's trajectory under a specific intervention may simply live in different signal spaces, one closer to structure and trait, the other closer to function and state, and that a research program chasing a single unified marker for both may be optimizing for a target that does not actually exist as a single thing. The unresolved question this leaves the field, a decade on, is one this Dispatch is now well positioned to ask precisely because of ground already covered here. Both of these 2014 results rest on cross-validation within samples of twenty and thirty, the kind of small, single-site cohort this publication's fifth edition showed to be exactly the wrong scale for testing whether a resting connectivity signature generalizes, when it described the multicenter harmonization effort that assembled resting-state data across dozens of sites and thousands of participants and found, at that scale, an anxiety signature that did not even implicate the amygdala. That harmonized resource did not exist when either the Uppsala or the Chicago group ran these analyses. It exists now, and as far as this publication has been able to determine, nobody has yet gone back with it to ask the harder, more useful version of either 2014 question, not whether amygdala-prefrontal connectivity or whole-brain gray matter volume looks encouraging in twenty or thirty patients, but whether either one survives being asked, at last, of a sample large enough to tell the difference between a real signal and a small sample's flattering accident.
