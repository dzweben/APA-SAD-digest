The Social Brain Dispatch

The Study That Lost the Amygdala

The largest harmonized brain scan of anxiety disorders ever assembled found a signature for social anxiety that never once implicates the amygdala, and a separately run reinforcement learning study, built to catch the biased learning rate this publication has already taught twice, came back with that exact parameter clean. Two of the field’s best resourced frontier projects each went looking for their own favorite mechanism and each came home having found something else instead.

Edition No. 5 · Frontier Neuroscience · July 13, 2026

Four editions of this publication have now built, piece by piece, a specific and fairly confident story about what is mechanically wrong in social anxiety disorder. Etkin and Wager found the amygdala hyperreactive across a pooled meta-analytic sample. Goldin and colleagues showed cognitive reappraisal failing to recruit the prefrontal machinery that should be damping that reactivity down. Grupe and Nitschke gave the whole picture a computational gloss, uncertainty intolerance as a driver of chronically elevated anticipatory threat signaling. Koban and colleagues went further still, showing that self-referential social feedback gets integrated through a biased learning process, a brain that updates its self-model too readily from criticism and too reluctantly from praise. Birbaumer, Tillfors, and then Guyer and colleagues anchored all of it developmentally, an amygdala that flinches at faces and, in a striking twist, a ventrolateral prefrontal cortex that in threatened children was not disengaged but unusually tightly coupled to the very region it should have been restraining. Read across four editions, this adds up to a coherent story with real internal consistency, an overactive threat detector, an unreliable regulator, a learning system biased toward negative self-relevant inference, all of it visible from adolescence onward. It is also, when you say it plainly, a story built almost entirely out of small samples. Guyer and colleagues had fourteen patients per arm. Birbaumer had roughly a dozen per group. Koban and colleagues, doing genuinely sophisticated multivariate work, still worked from a sample in the dozens. Small task fMRI samples are not merely underpowered in the ordinary statistical sense of missing true effects, they are, as the field has spent the last decade painfully re-learning, prone to the opposite failure too, finding effects that look robust in one lab's twenty scans and evaporate the moment anyone tries to reproduce them in a sample built at genuine scale. This edition is about what happens when two of the newest, best-funded, most methodologically ambitious projects in the field finally try to test pieces of this story at a scale none of the founding work ever approached, one testing the circuit claim with the largest harmonized multi-site clinical sample assembled for this purpose to date, the other testing the learning claim with a formal generative model fit to more than a hundred and fifty people rather than a dozen. Neither confirms the story this Dispatch has been telling. Both relocate the mechanism somewhere the story, as told so far, does not reach.

The first project is the culmination of a specific, decades-long bet about what psychiatric diagnosis is for, and the person who made that bet is Ulrike Lueken. Her career runs through the institutional core of German biological psychiatry, the Comprehensive Anxiety Center at the University Hospital of Würzburg, a collaborative SFB research center at the Technical University of Dresden, and now a professorship at Humboldt-Universität zu Berlin, and across that trajectory her research has kept returning to a single, deceptively basic question, whether the diagnostic boundaries psychiatry draws around panic disorder, agoraphobia, social anxiety disorder, and specific phobia correspond to anything a scanner can actually see, or whether, as a decade of dimensional and transdiagnostic theorizing has increasingly argued, these are administratively convenient labels drawn over what is really one continuous fear circuit gone wrong in matters of degree rather than kind. This is not an idle academic question in Lueken's hands. She has spent years building the actual infrastructure needed to answer it rather than simply arguing about it, co-architecting PROTECT-AD, a multicenter German trial network that pools cognitive behavioral therapy outcome and neuroimaging data across sites specifically so that questions requiring hundreds rather than dozens of patients per diagnosis become answerable, and serving within ENIGMA-Anxiety, the international mega-analytic consortium that does for anxiety disorders what its sister ENIGMA working groups have done for schizophrenia and depression, pooling raw or harmonized imaging data across dozens of independent samples worldwide to get past the sample-size ceiling that has quietly limited almost everything this Dispatch has covered so far. The 2024 paper, led by Till Langhammer with Lueken among the senior authors and Jürgen Deckert, the Würzburg center's leading figure in the genetics and psychophysiology of anxiety, among the thirty seven coauthors, is in a real sense the most direct possible test of Lueken's founding bet, the largest attempt yet to ask, inside one harmonized analytic pipeline, whether panic and agoraphobia, social anxiety disorder, and specific phobia actually look like the same disorder wearing different diagnostic clothes, or whether they look like genuinely separate things.

The design earns the scale it claims. Five hundred forty four people, four hundred thirty nine of them patients drawn from two German multicenter trials run across eight sites, one hundred fifty four with panic disorder or agoraphobia, ninety five with social anxiety disorder, one hundred ninety with specific phobia, and one hundred five demographically matched healthy comparison participants, all diagnosed by trained clinicians using standardized structured interviews tied to DSM criteria, all scanned using an eight minute resting state sequence harmonized as tightly as seven different 3 Tesla scanners across different manufacturers and sites can be harmonized. Resting state, rather than a task, is itself a methodological choice worth pausing on, because it trades away the ability to probe a specific provoked mental state, the anticipated peer evaluation Guyer and colleagues built their entire paradigm around, in exchange for a signal that does not depend on every site running an identical behavioral task with identical timing and identical instructions, which is precisely the kind of protocol drift that becomes unmanageable once you are coordinating eight clinical sites rather than one laboratory. The analytic strategy was built around twenty eight bilateral regions of interest selected a priori from the brainnetome and basal ganglia atlases, spanning the periaqueductal gray, amygdala, the anterior cingulate's dorsal, pregenual, and subgenual subdivisions, anterior and posterior insula, hippocampus, thalamus, and several distinct prefrontal subregions including dorsomedial, dorsolateral, ventromedial, and ventrolateral prefrontal cortex along with orbitofrontal cortex, yielding three hundred seventy eight pairwise connectivity values per person, each one a Fisher's Z transformed resting state correlation, entered into one way ANCOVAs covarying for age, sex, and scanner, with false discovery rate correction across the full set of three hundred seventy eight comparisons. This is a categorical design in the fullest sense, each diagnostic group compared against healthy controls as its own contrast, and a separate dimensional analysis, regressing continuous illness severity measures against connectivity within each patient group, run specifically to give the alternative, transdiagnostic hypothesis its own fair chance to win.

It did not win, and the way it lost is the part worth teaching carefully. Panic disorder and agoraphobia showed exactly the widespread, thalamo-limbic-cortical picture a shared fear circuit account would predict, increased connectivity linking insula, hippocampus, and amygdala to thalamus alongside decreased connectivity between regulatory regions, dorsomedial prefrontal cortex and periaqueductal gray, and anterior cingulate. Specific phobia, despite being grouped clinically alongside the other two as a fear-based rather than fear-and-avoidance-based anxiety disorder, showed no significant connectivity differences from controls at all, a genuinely inconvenient result for anyone hoping this dataset would hand them a clean spectrum. And social anxiety disorder, the diagnosis this Dispatch exists to track, produced exactly one result that survived correction, a decrease in negative connectivity between right orbitofrontal cortex and left posterior insula, patients showing a Z of negative 0.07 where controls showed negative 0.16, F of 18.83 with an eta squared of 0.09 at a corrected p of .009. Read that finding against the twenty eight regions on offer and what should immediately strike a reader who has followed this publication from its first edition is what is missing from it. The amygdala sat right there among the seeds, in the same analytic pipeline that found real, FDR-surviving effects for panic and agoraphobia radiating directly through amygdala-thalamic connectivity. For social anxiety disorder specifically, in the single largest, most carefully harmonized clinical sample this exact question has ever been put to, it produced nothing that cleared correction. Whatever is distinctively wrong with the resting brain in diagnosed social anxiety disorder, at this scale, in this design, lives in a narrow cortico-cortical channel between an orbitofrontal region implicated in valuation and an insular region implicated in interoceptive and bodily threat signaling, not in the amygdala at all, and the dimensional analysis, the one built to rescue a spectrum account, found no significant relationship between connectivity and symptom severity within any patient group whatsoever. The authors' own conclusion leans hard into what that combination implies, that these disorders show distinct rather than broadly shared circuit signatures, an argument for taking DSM categories more seriously as biological entities rather than less, exactly the opposite direction the last decade of dimensional, RDoC-flavored psychiatry has been pushing.

The second project comes from a completely different methodological tradition and a completely different kind of career. Bethany Teachman's scientific formation runs through Stanley Rachman, the towering figure in the founding of exposure-based behavior therapy whose research assistant she was at the University of British Columbia, and through Sheila Woody at Yale, a lineage that plants her squarely in the clinical, cognitive-behavioral wing of anxiety science rather than the circuit-mapping wing Lueken represents. For much of her career that meant running the interpretation bias and cognitive bias modification program that made her name, testing whether socially anxious people systematically resolve ambiguous social situations toward threatening interpretations and whether training people to resolve that ambiguity differently could shift the anxiety itself. What changed her trajectory was a friendship formed at Yale with Brian Nosek, later the co-founder of the Center for Open Science, which pulled Teachman's Program for Anxiety, Cognition, and Treatment lab hard toward large scale, technology-enabled, methodologically rigorous research infrastructure, Project Implicit Mental Health and the MindTrails platform among its products. The 2023 paper, led by Miranda Beltzer with Katharine Daniel, Alexander Daros, and Teachman as senior author, reads as exactly the kind of test that trajectory was built to run, taking the lab's decades-old intuition about biased social processing and subjecting it, for the first time in this specific form, to a formal generative computational model rather than to a self-report interpretation questionnaire, asking whether the old story about biased processing actually shows up as a biased parameter inside an explicit account of how people learn.

The task itself, the Social Probabilistic Selection Task, is worth teaching in full because the model built on top of it is only as good as the behavioral structure it is fit to. In a training phase, participants repeatedly chose between pairs of neutral faces that delivered probabilistic reward, a smiling face, or probabilistic punishment, an angry face, at three levels of contingency strength, eighty against twenty, seventy against thirty, sixty against forty percent, across a minimum of sixty trials, learning by direct feedback which face in each pair tended to reward and which tended to punish. In a subsequent testing phase, the faces were recombined into novel pairings and presented without any further feedback at all, forcing participants to rely purely on what they had already learned in order to choose the more rewarding or less punishing option, a design that cleanly separates the learning process itself from the read-out of what was learned. Onto this the authors fit hierarchical Bayesian Q-learning models using the hBayesDM package, the workhorse computational psychiatry toolkit built around exactly this kind of task, estimating for each person a separate learning rate for positive outcomes and a separate learning rate for negative outcomes, governing how much each new reward or punishment updates the running expected value of a given face, along with a softmax inverse temperature parameter capturing how deterministically a person's choices track those learned values rather than behaving noisily. Model comparison by leave-one-out information criterion favored the two-learning-rate model over simpler alternatives, meaning the data themselves supported treating reward learning and punishment learning as separable processes rather than forcing them through one shared rate, which is precisely the parameter split a biased-learning account of social anxiety needs in order to be tested at all, since the entire theoretical claim, echoing Koban and colleagues' finding in this Dispatch's second edition that socially anxious people update self-relevant beliefs asymmetrically from criticism versus praise, is that the punishment learning rate specifically should run hot in high social anxiety while the reward learning rate runs normal or low.

Recruited from an unselected pool of one hundred fifty seven adults screened on the Social Interaction Anxiety Scale into a high group scoring twenty nine or above and a low group scoring ten or below, mean ages a little over twenty in both arms, the study found nothing of the sort in the parameter that mattered most. A mixed effects model predicting learning rate from social anxiety group, feedback valence, and their interaction returned no significant main effect of group, no significant effect of valence, and no significant interaction, the estimated group difference sitting at 0.00 with a confidence interval spanning negative 0.02 to 0.03. The specific computational mechanism this Dispatch has already introduced once, an updating process weighted too heavily toward negative social information, simply did not appear in the parameter built explicitly to detect it, in a sample roughly ten times the size of most task-based imaging studies in this literature. What did appear, and appeared robustly, showed up instead in the testing phase, where accuracy at correctly choosing the historically better option depended on a highly significant interaction between social anxiety group and choice type, an odds ratio of 1.17 with a confidence interval from 1.10 to 1.25. Broken down, high social anxiety participants were reliably worse at avoiding the historically punishing face than they were at approaching the historically rewarding one, an odds ratio of 0.50 relative to their own reward accuracy, and worse specifically at avoiding punishment than the low social anxiety group was, an odds ratio of 0.47, the opposite direction from what a punishment-hypervigilance account would predict going in. A secondary analysis added a further wrinkle, high social anxiety participants showed a stronger boost in accuracy when the reward magnitude difference between two options was larger, while low social anxiety participants showed essentially no such magnitude sensitivity at all, meaning the deficit was not a blanket failure to learn but something more specific, an inability to convert already-learned values into accurate avoidance choices under punishment conditions precisely, even as the same participants got measurably better, relative to low anxiety peers, at exploiting large reward differences.

Put next to each other, these are two of the most methodologically serious things anyone has done recently to test specific, previously reported mechanisms in social anxiety, and both come back having quietly relocated the story. Langhammer, Lueken, and colleagues went looking for a circuit signature at a scale designed to settle, once and for all, whether social anxiety disorder is biologically distinct from its neighboring anxiety diagnoses, and the categorical bet won cleanly, three disorders, three separate stories, no shared spectrum, no dimensional correlation with severity anywhere. But the specific circuit that won the categorical contest for social anxiety was not the amygdala this Dispatch has spent four editions building a case around, it was an isolated decoupling between orbitofrontal cortex and posterior insula that has essentially no history in this publication's own prior coverage. Beltzer, Daniel, Daros, and Teachman went looking for a computational signature of exactly the kind of biased negative learning Koban and colleagues had already reported in a smaller, differently structured self-referential task, built the two-parameter model specifically to catch it, and found the learning rate itself entirely unremarkable, while a real, substantial, directionally counterintuitive deficit showed up one step downstream, in how learned values get converted into accurate choices under threat of punishment rather than in how those values get learned in the first place.

The disagreement between the two studies is at least as instructive as their shared negative result. Lueken's group is making, and on this evidence winning, a bet on categorical specificity, that psychiatric diagnoses correspond to genuinely separable neurobiological entities, strong enough that dimensional severity measures add nothing once diagnosis is known. Teachman's group is making, in the same publication cycle, essentially the opposite bet, that a continuous trait measure in an unselected population, no diagnostic interview anywhere in the recruitment pipeline, will reveal a real, quantifiable computational difference, and it does, cleanly, in the accuracy data if not in the learning rate. Both bets paid off on their own terms and both bets, in paying off, embarrassed the specific mechanism each research program had actually been chasing. That is a genuinely useful kind of failure, because it means the problem is not that either team executed carelessly, both plainly did not, it is that the field's working story about what is mechanically wrong in social anxiety, an amygdala that overreacts and a learning system that updates too readily from negative feedback, may have been built almost entirely out of the kind of small, single-site, single-paradigm samples that are exactly where spurious effects are most likely to look real and most likely to fail to generalize once anyone finally checks at scale. Neither of these papers proves the amygdala hyperreactivity story wrong outright, resting state connectivity and provoked task activation are not interchangeable measurements of the same underlying quantity, and a learning rate parameter fit to a face-based probabilistic selection task is not the same construct as biased updating of self-relevant beliefs from social feedback. But both papers had a clean opportunity to find the field's favorite mechanism sitting exactly where theory said it should be, at a scale that would have made the finding far more credible than anything this Dispatch has reported before it, and in both cases the mechanism was somewhere else.

What neither study can tell you, because nobody has yet built it, is whether the orbitofrontal-insula decoupling Langhammer and colleagues found in clinically diagnosed patients and the punishment-accuracy deficit Beltzer and colleagues found in an unselected, subclinical sample screened purely by questionnaire are two views of the same underlying failure or two genuinely different things that the field has been treating as interchangeable purely because diagnosed clinical samples are expensive and slow to recruit at the numbers a stable computational model needs, while college students scored on the Social Interaction Anxiety Scale are cheap and fast. That is not a small caveat sitting at the edge of this literature, it may be the actual fault line running through it. If subclinical trait social anxiety in an unselected population and diagnosed social anxiety disorder in a clinical trial sample are the same underlying liability at different points on one continuum, then a study built to connect Beltzer and colleagues' punishment-accuracy paradigm to Langhammer and colleagues' orbitofrontal-insula circuit, in one sample, diagnosed patients doing the reinforcement learning task inside the same harmonized scanning protocol, would very likely find that the behavioral deficit and the connectivity signature are functionally the same story told in two languages. If they are not the same liability, if something changes qualitatively between a distressed but functional undergraduate scoring high on a self-report scale and a person who has sought treatment for a diagnosed anxiety disorder, then a great deal of the computational psychiatry literature built on convenient, non-clinical samples, including some of the work this very Dispatch has already taught, may be characterizing a genuinely different population than the one clinicians actually treat. Nobody has yet run the study that could tell these two possibilities apart, and until someone does, this remains the single most consequential open question the frontier of this field has left unanswered.
