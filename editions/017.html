The Social Brain Dispatch

The Region That Keeps Getting Cleared

In 2022 a Princeton-led team ran seven hundred forty-three strangers through a rigged race against a live opponent and found social anxiety’s signature in a single learning parameter, no brain required. In 2026 a Münster-Melbourne consortium scanned sixty-one diagnosed patients forcing themselves to feel differently and filed the amygdala’s discharge papers, handing the evidence instead to a region built to inhibit action rather than to feel fear. Between them the amygdala now has two independent alibis and the field has a much harder question about where the disorder actually lives.

Edition No. 17 · Frontier Neuroscience · September 17, 2026

Every study this Dispatch has read so far that implicates a circuit in social anxiety disorder has implicated the amygdala as at least one half of it, whether as the seat of an exaggerated threat response or as the broken end of a connection to somewhere else. That assumption is old enough, and load-bearing enough, that the two papers in this edition deserve to be read as a genuine crisis for it rather than as one more incremental refinement. Neither study set out to defend the amygdala. One never scanned a brain at all, and found the disorder's signature sitting instead in a single parameter of a learning algorithm, the weight a person assigns to the path they did not take. The other scanned sixty-one diagnosed patients directly, with a method built specifically to ask who is driving whom, and came back reporting that the amygdala's traffic with the prefrontal cortex was, statistically, unremarkable, the entire deficit relocated to a wire nobody had been watching. Read separately, each result is a surprising negative finding about one region. Read together, they start to look like the outline of a different theory of what this disorder actually is, one in which the amygdala is functioning exactly as it should and the disease lives somewhere in how its output gets used.

The computational half of that argument descends from a lineage built almost entirely outside clinical psychology. Nathaniel Daw trained as a machine-learning theorist before turning his attention to human decision-making, and the tool he is most responsible for putting into general circulation, a two-step sequential choice task first reported in 2011 with Samuel Gershman, Ben Seymour, Peter Dayan, and Raymond Dolan, gave the field a way to separate two computationally distinct strategies that had previously been lumped together under the single word learning, a slow, effortful model-based strategy that builds an internal map of how the world's contingencies actually work and plans over it, and a fast, cheap model-free strategy that simply reinforces whatever action recently paid off without any model of the world at all. That distinction, imported from reinforcement-learning theory in artificial intelligence, turned out to be exactly the right instrument for a question psychiatry had never had the vocabulary to ask precisely: not whether a patient's behavior looks compulsive or anxious from the outside, but which specific computation, in which specific brain system, is malfunctioning. Claire Gillan, working from Trinity College Dublin, spent the following decade turning that instrument into an epidemiological one, recruiting thousands of members of the public online rather than a handful of diagnosed patients in a lab, pairing self-report symptom checklists spanning the entire diagnostic manual with Daw's tasks, and using factor analysis to cut across the official categories entirely. Her 2023 paper with a large consortium of collaborators, built on data of this kind, resolved the space of common psychiatric symptoms into three computational dimensions that ignore diagnostic boundaries altogether, one organized around anxiety and depression together, one around compulsivity and intrusive thought, and one around social withdrawal specifically, each dimension tracking its own distinct signature in how people learn and plan. That third dimension is the direct ancestor of the paper this edition reads closely, and it is worth naming the gap it leaves: a factor called social withdrawal, extracted from a battery designed to span all of psychopathology at once, is necessarily a blunt instrument for asking what, computationally, actually goes wrong in a socially anxious mind. Somebody had to go back and build a task narrow enough, and social enough, to answer that question on its own terms.

That is the project Lindsay Hunter, Elana Meer, Gillan, Ming Hsu, and Daw undertook together, and the choice of collaborator matters as much as the choice of question. Hsu is a neuroeconomist at Berkeley whose research identity is built around strategic games, paradigms in which a person's payoff depends not on the world's indifferent statistics but on what another agent, real or simulated, chooses to do, and it is Hsu's toolkit, not a generic bandit task, that gives the resulting study its bite. The vehicle the team built, a competitive game they call the patent race, drops a participant into the role of a financially weaker player racing a stronger, computer-controlled opponent toward a fixed prize, investing money round by round with the threat of being overtaken on every single trial. That structure matters because it is the first task in this Dispatch's coverage built to isolate a specifically social computational bias rather than a generic one; the opponent's presence, and the live possibility of losing to it, is the entire point.

What the task is actually measuring requires one piece of machinery from behavioral game theory to appreciate properly. Reinforcement learning in the narrowest sense updates a person's valuation of only the action they actually took, using only the payoff they actually received. Belief-based, or fictitious-play, learning does something stranger and more cognitively demanding, updating the value of actions never taken at all, based on what those actions would have earned, a counterfactual signal the brain has to construct rather than observe. The experience-weighted attraction framework that Hunter and colleagues fit to their data, a model with roots in Colin Camerer and Teck-Hua Ho's behavioral game theory, blends both processes into one equation and hands the analyst a single parameter, denoted delta, that captures how heavily a person weighs the counterfactual, forgone-action signal relative to the real one they experienced. Crucially, the authors split that parameter in two, delta-plus for counterfactuals running in the upward direction, what I would have won had I invested more, and delta-minus for the downward direction, what I would have lost had I invested less, a distinction that maps almost exactly onto the clinical language of regret versus relief. Fit to eighty rounds of play from four hundred twelve participants in a first experiment and three hundred thirty-one in a second, with self-reported social anxiety measured continuously on the Liebowitz Social Anxiety Scale across a sample whose mean score of fifty, with roughly seventy-two percent scoring above the conventional clinical cutoff, spans the full range from unremarkable to severe, the model returned an answer sharp enough to survive both replications. Liebowitz scores predicted a significantly larger overall delta, at t of seven hundred forty equals three point six three, p equals zero point three thousandths, and when the parameter was split, the entire effect concentrated in delta-plus, p under one in ten thousand, while delta-minus showed no relationship to social anxiety whatsoever, p above point oh five. Socially anxious minds, in other words, are not generically more sensitive to counterfactuals; they are selectively haunted by the versions of events in which they could have done better, and largely indifferent to the versions in which they could have done worse. Hunter and colleagues (2022) read this as a direct computational fingerprint of rumination, not a vague clinical description of dwelling on the past but a specific, quantifiable asymmetry in how a competitive social outcome gets folded into the next decision.

The circuit-level half of this edition's argument comes out of an institution this Dispatch has visited before without naming its full ambitions. Udo Dannlowski directs the Institute for Translational Psychiatry at the University of Münster, the same institutional home behind the multicenter resting-state connectivity study, Langhammer and colleagues' 2024 paper spanning more than a dozen German sites, that this Dispatch read five editions ago under the frontier-neuroscience beat. What that earlier paper demonstrated mostly by its scale, that a single well-resourced German consortium can now pool imaging data across an entire national network of anxiety clinics, this new paper demonstrates by depth of method instead, taking one specific emotion-regulation question and interrogating it with a technique built to answer questions the earlier resting-state design structurally could not. The recruit responsible for that method is Ben Harrison, a Melbourne-based specialist in effective-connectivity modeling with a research record built largely around obsessive-compulsive disorder and depression circuitry, and his presence on this author list, alongside Elisabeth Schrammen, Alec Jamieson, Hannah Meinert, Elisabeth Leehr, Dannlowski, and a long list of Münster collaborators, is itself a small piece of intellectual history, a German phenotyping machine built to generate data at scale importing an Australian methodologist specifically to ask a question about direction rather than magnitude.

That question requires distinguishing two things that get casually conflated under the single word connectivity. Ordinary functional connectivity, the measure behind essentially every amygdala-prefrontal finding this Dispatch has read before, including Phan and colleagues' work two editions ago, is a correlation, a statement that two regions' activity rises and falls together, agnostic to which region is causing which. Dynamic causal modeling asks a different and considerably harder question, fitting a generative, biophysically motivated model in which each region's activity is treated as the output of a small neural system, with fixed intrinsic connections between regions, connections whose strength changes under specific task conditions, and external driving inputs, all estimated together through variational Bayesian inference, so that the result is not a correlation but a directed, causal-in-the-modeling-sense account of who is influencing whom, and by how much, under which condition. Fitting one such model to each of a hundred two individual participants and then asking whether diagnosis explains variation in specific connection strengths across the group is itself a further statistical problem, solved here through parametric empirical Bayes, a hierarchical scheme that treats each patient's individually fitted connectivity parameters as data generated from a group-level model, letting diagnosis, symptom severity, or any other covariate compete to explain the pattern with far more power than simply comparing group averages connection by connection.

Schrammen and colleagues (2026) applied that machinery to sixty-one patients with a confirmed diagnosis of social anxiety disorder and forty-one healthy controls, most in their mid-thirties, while participants either passively viewed neutral and negative faces drawn from the Karolinska Directed Emotional Faces set or actively worked to downregulate their emotional response to those same faces using a self-selected strategy, rating their success after each trial. Five regions anchored the model, the amygdala, the ventromedial prefrontal cortex, the dorsolateral prefrontal cortex, the ventrolateral prefrontal cortex, and the pre-supplementary motor area, the last of these a region whose canonical role has almost nothing to do with emotion at all, sitting instead at the center of the literature on inhibiting a planned action before it executes and switching between competing behavioral sets. In healthy controls, the model recovered exactly the circuit three decades of amygdala-centered theory would predict, reciprocal amygdala-prefrontal influence during passive observation and a clear negative, regulatory signal running from the amygdala outward to every prefrontal region once participants began actively controlling their response. In patients with social anxiety disorder, that entire amygdala-prefrontal story simply failed to differ from controls, no significant group effect on any amygdala-to-prefrontal or prefrontal-to-amygdala connection, in either task condition. The deficit that did survive lived entirely within the prefrontal cortex itself, an abnormally strong inhibitory connection running from the pre-supplementary motor area to the dorsolateral prefrontal cortex during passive observation, before any regulation had even been attempted, and a bidirectional excitatory abnormality between the pre-supplementary motor area and the ventromedial prefrontal cortex once regulation began. Leave-one-out cross-validation showed that these prefrontal connection strengths alone, with no amygdala parameter contributing anything, could distinguish patients from controls and track symptom severity. The authors' own conclusion is stated almost as an explicit correction to the field, that alterations in intra-prefrontal circuitry, not deficits in amygdala-prefrontal coupling, appear to characterize aberrant emotion regulation in this disorder, exonerating, in a single well-powered sample fit with a directional model, the very connection that gave this Dispatch's very first edition its founding claim.

Set beside each other, these two studies converge on a claim neither team frames explicitly but both data sets independently support, that the amygdala in social anxiety disorder is not the broken part. Hunter and colleagues never had the opportunity to indict it, since their design has no scanner in it at all, but the computational deficit they isolate, an overweighting of the road not taken, is precisely the kind of higher-order valuation asymmetry that classical fear-circuit accounts were never built to explain, since it requires representing a counterfactual, a piece of cognitive machinery well beyond a threat-detection system's brief. Schrammen and colleagues had every opportunity to indict the amygdala and declined to, finding instead that the entire measurable deficit sits in a region, the pre-supplementary motor area, whose ordinary job is gating action and switching behavioral sets rather than generating or regulating affect in the first place. Read against each other, both papers point toward the same reframing of what social anxiety disorder actually is, less a disorder of feeling too much fear and more a disorder of what a person's cognitive and motor machinery does once fear, ordinary and appropriately sized, has already been registered, whether that machinery keeps returning to the option not chosen or fails to gate the shift from observing a threat to acting on it.

Where the two studies genuinely part ways is on timescale and population, and neither gap is small enough to paper over. Hunter and colleagues' delta-plus asymmetry is a property that accumulates across eighty rounds of a competitive game, a signature of learning, of how many trials of social feedback it takes to bend a belief, measured in an unselected online sample spanning the full continuum from mild worry to clinical severity, seventy-two percent of it above threshold but none of it diagnostically confirmed. Schrammen and colleagues' prefrontal miswiring is a property of single trials, milliseconds of directed influence during one act of watching a face or one attempt to talk oneself down from it, measured in sixty-one patients who cleared a structured clinical interview. That difference in population is not a footnote. It reopens a question this Dispatch has circled before without resolving, whether the ordinary social nervousness that scores modestly on the Liebowitz scale and the diagnosed disorder that Schrammen's team recruited from clinical referral sit on one continuous dimension or constitute two different states of the same system, one gradually shading into the other or one crossing a threshold the other never reaches. Hunter and colleagues' own data offer a hint against a clean threshold story, since their delta-plus effect rose smoothly with Liebowitz scores across a sample that was never screened for diagnosis at all, a dimensional result obtained without ever admitting a single clinically confirmed patient. Whether that same smooth computational gradient would, if measured inside a scanner, also predict a smooth gradient in preSMA-prefrontal coupling, or whether it would instead reveal a genuine discontinuity once symptom severity crosses into diagnosable territory, is a question about the shape of psychopathology itself, not merely about this one disorder.

It remains entirely unknown whether the two findings are two views of one mechanism at different grains, a slow computational habit of mind implemented, trial by trial, in exactly the preSMA-to-prefrontal circuit that Schrammen's group found disrupted, or whether they are two separate failures that happen to share only the amygdala's innocence. The experiment that would settle it has not been run. Put a version of the patent race inside a scanner, fit each patient's trial-by-trial delta-plus alongside a dynamic causal model of their prefrontal circuitry gathered during the same session, and ask whether the size of one predicts the strength of the other, trial by trial and person by person. A positive answer would hand the field something it has wanted since Daw first split model-based from model-free learning, a bridge connecting a purely computational construct, fit from choice data alone, to a specific, directed, biologically implemented circuit, closing the gap between an algorithm and its hardware in a single disorder where both pieces are now independently well characterized for the first time. A negative answer would be its own kind of progress, forcing the field to accept that social anxiety disorder is not one lesion described twice but a genuine confluence of at least two independent failure modes, a valuation problem and a gating problem, that happen to converge on the same clinical picture without sharing a common cause. Until someone runs that study, this Dispatch is left holding two independent verdicts that acquit the same region and disagree about almost everything else, which is either the beginning of a unified account of this disorder or the discovery that it never had just one broken part to find.
