The Social Brain Dispatch

The Brain That Won’t Believe the Good News

In 2013 a Harvard psychologist synthesized a decade of imaging work into a single claim: adolescence turns up the volume on every social evaluation, indiscriminately, threat and reward alike. Two years later a National Institute of Mental Health team found exactly where that turned-up volume breaks in social anxiety, not in the amplification of threat, but in a striatum that cannot update on a peer’s unexpected kindness, and a memory that then discards the good news entirely.

Edition No. 17 · Child and Adolescent Social Processing · September 21, 2026

Social anxiety disorder has a favorite age of arrival. Median onset clusters tightly around thirteen, a fact so consistent across epidemiological samples that it has stopped reading as a coincidence and started reading as a clue. Whatever machinery turns ordinary shyness into a disorder that will, left untreated, shadow a person for decades, that machinery appears to switch on during a narrow developmental window defined less by chronological age than by a specific reorganization of the social brain, the same reorganization this Dispatch first encountered in its fourth edition, when a 2005 theory paper from the National Institute of Mental Health predicted that adolescence itself would prove to be a neurodevelopmental fault line for this disorder, and a 2008 study from the same corridor found the amygdala and ventrolateral prefrontal cortex confirming it. What neither of those two papers could yet explain is why the window is adolescence specifically, what is actually different about a thirteen-year-old’s brain that makes it the hinge point, and whether the vulnerability that opens during those years is a general property of the adolescent brain gone wrong in some individuals, or a separate malfunction that merely happens to share the same birthday. Two papers, a theoretical synthesis and a mechanistic dissection published two years apart, together make the best case yet for an answer, and the case turns out to hinge on a distinction between a system that notices too much and a system that cannot learn from what it notices.

The synthesis belongs to Leah Somerville, and it is worth knowing that she did not arrive at the teenage brain by way of an instrument or a paradigm but by way of a phone line. As an undergraduate at the University of Wisconsin she spent four years volunteering as a teen crisis counselor, intending, at the time, a career in social work rather than neuroscience, and it is not hard to hear that history in the way her 2013 review in Current Directions in Psychological Science opens, with her own adolescent memory of tripping in front of an assembly and lying awake for weeks afterward replaying who had noticed. She took her doctorate at Dartmouth under the developmental cognitive neuroscience tradition built there, then trained as a postdoctoral fellow at the Sackler Institute for Developmental Psychobiology at Weill Cornell, a lab whose central preoccupation, cognitive control and its slow cortical maturation, gave her the second half of the intellectual toolkit the 2013 paper runs on. By the time she reached Harvard in 2012 to found her own Affective Neuroscience and Development Laboratory, she had spent a decade assembling both halves of an argument that no single prior paper had stated cleanly: that adolescent social behavior is not merely explained by an immature prefrontal cortex failing to rein in an overactive limbic system, the story Nelson, Leibenluft, McClure, and Pine had already sketched in 2005 and this Dispatch already taught, but is driven just as much by a separate, positive process, a set of socioaffective regions, the amygdala, striatum, and medial prefrontal cortex, that do not simply fail to be regulated during adolescence but actively ramp up their own engagement, on a trajectory that is neither flat with age nor a simple downward slope of immaturity resolving into maturity.

That trajectory question is the paper’s real methodological contribution, easy to skate past but load-bearing for everything that follows. Somerville proposes that a developmental effect in this domain can take one of three shapes: an adolescent-specific pattern, elevated only during the teenage years and absent in both children and adults, tracking something like a temporary hormonal surge; an age-linear pattern, rising or falling steadily across the whole span from childhood to adulthood, tracking ordinary maturation; or what she terms an adolescent-emergent pattern, a nonlinear curve that rises sharply into adolescence and then only partially resolves in adulthood, implying a developmental shift that is triggered during the teenage years but leaves a lasting mark rather than reverting. Distinguishing among these three shapes requires a design that most of the field’s clinical imaging work does not bother with, a genuine cross-sectional or longitudinal span running from late childhood through adulthood rather than a two-group comparison of teenagers against grown-ups, and Somerville built her own empirical work specifically to supply it.

The evidence she marshals runs across three distinct manifestations of what she calls social sensitivity, each with its own paradigm and its own developmental signature. The first is straightforward emotional reactivity to explicit social feedback, tested in tasks like the one Gunther Moor and colleagues ran in 2010, in which participants believe an unfamiliar peer is rating their photograph as likeable or not, and the related chatroom paradigm in which a peer either does or does not choose to talk with the participant online, the same family of task, not coincidentally, that Guyer, McClure-Tone, and Nelson’s NIMH group had already been refining since 2008 for exactly the clinical population this Dispatch is tracking. Gunther Moor’s group found striatal and subgenual anterior cingulate activity rising with age while anticipating positive feedback, an age-linear pattern rather than an adolescent-specific spike, but Jill Silk and colleagues, using eye tracking rather than fMRI to catch an implicit measure of salience in the same chatroom-interact task, found a pupillary response to peer rejection that was exaggerated specifically in older adolescents and that predicted, independently, how connected those adolescents felt in their actual offline relationships, evidence that whatever the brain is doing in the scanner has a real behavioral signature outside it.

The second manifestation is vigilance under evaluation even absent any feedback at all, and this is where Somerville’s own empirical contribution does its heaviest lifting. Drawing on the decades-old, contested claim that adolescents behave as though performing for an imaginary audience, a notion Elkind and Bowen had proposed in 1979 and that later critics had challenged as unfalsifiable, Somerville and colleagues designed a study in which participants were simply told that a peer might be watching them live via video feed during parts of a brain scan, no feedback, no evaluation, nothing but the belief of being observed. Relative to both children and adults, adolescents reported a sharply elevated spike in self-conscious embarrassment, showed a uniquely heightened skin conductance response, and, in the medial prefrontal cortex specifically, showed activity that rose steeply into adolescence and then partially receded into adulthood, the adolescent-emergent pattern her taxonomy predicts and the closest thing the imaging literature has to a neural correlate of the imaginary audience made literal. The third manifestation is mentalizing itself, the capacity to model what a peer is thinking, which Somerville traces through Sarah-Jayne Blakemore’s program on the so-called social brain, medial prefrontal cortex, temporoparietal junction, and superior temporal sulcus, and through Burnett and Blakemore’s 2009 finding of heightened functional connectivity among these regions in adolescents relative to adults during an explicitly social-emotional task, evidence that the same circuitry recruited by simple evaluative feedback is also, during these years, working harder at the separate cognitive job of inferring what other minds contain.

What Somerville is careful not to claim is a fully resolved mechanism. She is explicit that pubertal hormones, still-maturing frontostriatal white matter tracts, and a genuinely new social environment, more autonomy, more unsupervised peer contact, more romantic stakes, are three candidate explanations for the same pattern of results and that the field does not yet have the longitudinal, puberty-staged data needed to adjudicate among them. But the closing move of her review is the one that matters most for this Dispatch’s own project, a direct claim that this normative, universal amplification of socioaffective circuitry is the terrain on which psychiatric risk gets planted, citing Guyer and colleagues’ 2008 finding of amygdala and ventrolateral prefrontal hyperactivation during anticipated peer evaluation in pediatric social anxiety as an early instance of biased responding within exactly the circuitry her review otherwise treats as a healthy, species-typical developmental feature. In other words, Somerville’s argument is not that socially anxious adolescents have a different brain from their peers. It is that every adolescent brain is, to some degree, running the same turned-up socioaffective program, and that social anxiety disorder is what happens when that shared program is running with a specific defect somewhere inside it, a defect her review, built from healthy cross-sectional samples, was never designed to locate.

Locating it is exactly the project Johanna Jarcho took up two years later, from inside the same NIMH corridor that had already produced the Nelson and Guyer papers this Dispatch has already read, the Section on Development and Affective Neuroscience, under Daniel Pine and Ellen Leibenluft, with Eric Nelson, the architect of the chatroom paradigm itself, as a coauthor once again. This is a research program with an unusually legible arc. Nelson, Leibenluft, McClure, and Pine laid the theory in 2005. Guyer, Nelson, and colleagues found the first clinical signature in 2008, anticipatory amygdala and ventrolateral prefrontal activation before peer evaluation ever arrived, a study of what happens while a socially anxious adolescent waits. Jarcho, joined by Adrienne Romer, Tomer Shechner, Adriana Galvan, Amanda Guyer, Leibenluft, Pine, and Nelson, published in Developmental Cognitive Neuroscience in 2015 a study of what happens once the wait is over, when the feedback actually arrives, and it arrives, crucially, sometimes wrong.

The reframing that makes this study more than a straightforward extension of Guyer 2008 is a shift from an emotional-reactivity model of social anxiety to a computational, reinforcement-learning one. Rather than asking simply whether the amygdala fires harder at a threatening peer, Jarcho and colleagues ask what happens in the brain’s value-updating circuitry, principally the striatum and its connectivity with medial prefrontal cortex, specifically when social feedback violates a prediction, the same prediction-error architecture that computational psychiatry had already begun importing from reward learning more broadly into anxiety research. Ninety participants across four groups, medication-free socially anxious adolescents, ages spanning roughly nine to sixteen, socially anxious adults in their mid to late twenties, and matched non-anxious adolescents and adults, first sorted sixty peer photographs into thirty desired, high-value chat partners and thirty rejected, low-value ones. In the scanner they then predicted, on a hundred-point scale, whether each peer would give them positive or negative feedback, before actually receiving that peer’s verdict, positive or negative, matched or mismatched against their own prediction. A prediction correctly anticipated is, computationally, uninformative; a prediction violated, an unexpected outcome in either direction, is where a properly functioning value-learning system should show its clearest signal, and it is exactly there that the four groups diverge.

Across the whole sample, a five-way statistical interaction of anxiety, age group, peer value, feedback valence, and prediction accuracy isolated activity in two regions, cerebellum and, centrally, a large bilateral striatal cluster with its peak in right caudate. Decomposing that interaction produced the paper’s single clearest finding: socially anxious adolescents, and only socially anxious adolescents, not anxious adults, not non-anxious adolescents, not non-anxious adults, showed a sharp spike in striatal activity specifically when a high-value peer delivered unexpectedly positive feedback, a pleasant surprise from exactly the peer whose opinion mattered most. That alone might have read as a healthy, even adaptive, hypersensitivity to good news, an amplified version of the value-updating signal every adolescent brain runs, consistent with Somerville’s general amplification account. What breaks the story is what a follow-up connectivity analysis, seeding a psychophysiological interaction model in that same right caudate region, found happening at the same moment between the striatum and the medial prefrontal cortex. In socially anxious adolescents alone, unexpected relative to expected feedback drove that striatal-mPFC coupling negative, into an inverse relationship, while every other group, anxious adults included, showed either flat or positive coupling in the same contrast. A spike in the value signal, in other words, was arriving inside a circuit that had, in this one group alone, stopped communicating properly with the region responsible for integrating that signal into an updated, useable belief.

The behavioral consequence of that broken conversation is where the paper earns its title. A surprise recall task after scanning asked participants simply to remember whether each peer had given positive or negative feedback. Non-anxious adolescents and non-anxious adults both recalled unexpected feedback about as accurately as expected feedback, exactly what a well-functioning learning system should do with a salient, prediction-violating event. Socially anxious adults recalled both types of feedback poorly, a flat, generalized memory impairment unconnected, critically, to any brain-based prediction-error measure the authors could locate. Socially anxious adolescents were the only group with a fully dissociated pattern: they recalled expected feedback accurately, performing above chance, but fell to chance performance specifically for unexpected feedback, the very category, unexpected positive feedback from a high-value peer, that had produced their striatal spike and their negative striatal-mPFC connectivity in the first place. And when the authors correlated that connectivity against memory directly, the relationship held only among adolescents, not adults, and within the adolescent group it was carried almost entirely by the anxious ones, whose negative connectivity predicted worse recall at a magnitude, a correlation of point five three, that differed with statistical reliability from both healthy adolescents and every adult group. The paper’s title inverts the folk intuition about anxious memory, that anxious people simply remember bad things more vividly; what Jarcho and colleagues actually found is a system that cannot properly encode good news when it arrives as a surprise, forgets the best specifically because it was busy, and malfunctioning, while predicting the worst.

Put the two papers next to each other and the disagreement that at first looks like a contradiction resolves into something more interesting, a division of labor between a normative account and a disorder-specific one that each paper, on its own, could not have supplied. Somerville’s synthesis establishes that the entire socioaffective apparatus, amygdala, striatum, medial prefrontal cortex, ramps up its engagement with social evaluation during adolescence as a general, species-typical property, not a symptom, evident in the healthy participants across a dozen studies from Gunther Moor’s reward-anticipation task to her own embarrassment paradigm. Jarcho’s study, run in exactly this age range and largely in the same striatal and medial prefrontal territory Somerville’s review flags as socioaffective circuitry, shows that within that generally amplified system, one specific subpopulation carries a further, qualitatively distinct defect, not more amplification but a directional failure of the connection between the region that registers the surprise and the region that is supposed to integrate it, and a failure that shows up only for positive surprises about high-value peers, the exact currency an adolescent, per Somerville’s own account of what is newly at stake during these years, should care about most.

Each paper also exposes something the other cannot see on its own. Somerville’s taxonomy of developmental trajectories, adolescent-specific, age-linear, adolescent-emergent, was built entirely from healthy, cross-sectional or short-longitudinal samples and has no category at all for a pattern like Jarcho’s, a deficit that appears to be adolescent-specific in the anxious group, since anxious adults show a different, flatter, memory-only impairment rather than the same connectivity failure, but that cannot be properly diagnosed as adolescent-specific, adolescent-emergent, or something else again without exactly the kind of longitudinal, puberty-staged tracking of the same anxious individuals across development that neither paper has. Jarcho and colleagues are themselves candid that their n of fifteen socially anxious adolescents is preliminary and that a study built on one-time, low-stakes feedback from strangers a participant will never actually meet cannot fully speak to the higher-stakes, repeated social learning that presumably drives the disorder outside the scanner. Somerville’s broader theoretical frame, that this circuitry is being sensitized by convergent maturational forces, offers exactly the missing causal story Jarcho’s data need but do not themselves test, why should a striatal-mPFC connection fail to update on good news specifically during the years when Somerville shows that same connection is undergoing its steepest developmental change.

That is also where the genuinely unresolved question sits, and it is not a minor one for anyone thinking about intervention. Somerville’s account implies that adolescent social anxiety is normative sensitivity gone wrong somewhere downstream, which would suggest that treatment ought to work by helping the system regulate an amplification that is, in its origin, healthy and shared by every teenager in the room. Jarcho’s data admit a harsher alternative, that the striatal-mPFC failure to update on positive surprise is not a quantitative exaggeration of a normative process at all but a qualitatively distinct computational lesion, present specifically in the clinical group and absent even in anxious adults, who show impaired memory without impaired connectivity, which would mean the vulnerable circuit is not the same one Somerville is describing turned up too high, but a different piece of the machinery entirely that happens to come online, or come apart, on the same developmental schedule. Nobody has yet run the study that could tell these two stories apart, a longitudinal sample tracked from before adolescence through its peak and out the other side, scanned repeatedly with a task like Jarcho’s own, watching whether the striatal-mPFC connection that fails in anxious thirteen-year-olds is the same connection that Somerville shows rising in everyone, only miscalibrated, or a separate wire that was never supposed to be part of the adolescent surge at all.
