The Social Brain Dispatch

The Paper Everyone Cites, the Paper Nobody Counts

Social anxiety neuroscience traces its founding claim about the amygdala to two small, mismatched studies from the late nineteen nineties and early two thousands, and the field’s own flagship synthesis quietly excluded the more famous one.

Edition No. 3 · Foundational and Landmark Neuroscience · July 6, 2026

Every field of empirical science tells itself an origin story, and the story is almost never identical to the evidence that actually did the founding work. Social anxiety disorder’s neuroscience is unusually explicit about this, because its origin story is short enough to state in a sentence: the amygdala fires too readily to social threat, and everything downstream, the salience network models, the uncertainty and anticipation frameworks, the reinforcement learning accounts of biased social feedback processing this publication has already spent two editions unpacking, treats that claim as a settled premise rather than a live hypothesis. But settled premises have to come from somewhere, and when you trace this one back to its actual source, you do not find a large, well-powered, coordinate-reporting neuroimaging study of the kind the field now demands before it will believe anything. You find two studies with a combined sample of thirty six people, run three years apart on two continents with two different scanning modalities, neither of which resembles what a methods reviewer would wave through today. One of them is cited constantly as the first demonstration that the amygdala responds abnormally in social phobia. The other is cited less triumphantly but was actually used as raw data in the meta-analysis that made the amygdala claim canonical. These are not the same paper, and the gap between being remembered and being counted is itself the most interesting thing this edition has to teach.

The first paper is Birbaumer and colleagues’ 1998 NeuroReport communication, a four page report out of Tübingen showing selective amygdala activation to neutral human faces in seven social phobics against five healthy controls, with an aversive odor condition included specifically to test whether the effect was face specific or just generic arousal. The second is Tillfors and colleagues’ 2001 American Journal of Psychiatry study, a PET study out of Uppsala in which eighteen people with social phobia and six comparison subjects gave a speech under public and private conditions while their regional cerebral blood flow was mapped, producing both an amygdala finding and a striking cortical dissociation that anticipated, by more than a decade, the circuit language this publication introduced two weeks ago through Grupe and Nitschke’s uncertainty and anticipation model. Read them side by side and you get something better than a origin myth. You get an origin argument, about what a small sample can and cannot establish, about which experimental paradigm actually became the field’s working default, and about why one foundational claim survived into the evidentiary record while its more famous sibling survived only into the footnotes.

Niels Birbaumer is not, at this point in his career, best known for anxiety research at all. By 1998 he had already built, and would continue for another two decades to expand, one of the most consequential research programs in human psychophysiology, culminating in his work on brain computer interfaces for communication in locked in patients with amyotrophic lateral sclerosis, the so called thought translation device that made him one of the most cited names in the intersection of neuroscience and assistive technology. That later arc is not free of controversy. A 2017 paper from his group claiming to have achieved reliable yes or no communication with completely locked in ALS patients was retracted in 2019 after an investigation by the University of Tübingen and the German Research Foundation concluded there had been serious problems in how the data were collected and analyzed, a finding Birbaumer and his coauthors disputed. None of that bears directly on the 1998 amygdala paper, a much smaller, much earlier study with a different team and a narrower claim, but it is worth holding in view precisely because this publication has committed to treating researchers as people with careers rather than as names attached to isolated findings, and Birbaumer’s career is an instructive one: a scientist whose stature was earned across hundreds of papers over five decades, whose single most cited later achievement would eventually be retracted for exactly the kind of small sample, high stakes claim that this 1998 study also represents, on a smaller and less consequential scale.

The paper’s intellectual center of gravity, though, sits less with Birbaumer than with its corresponding author, Herta Flor, then at Humboldt University in Berlin and shortly to become scientific director of the Central Institute of Mental Health in Mannheim. Flor had, by 1998, already produced what remains one of the most cited findings in all of pain neuroscience, the demonstration that phantom limb pain tracks the degree of cortical reorganization in somatosensory cortex following amputation, a result that reframed a symptom long treated as peripheral or even psychogenic as a direct readout of maladaptive cortical plasticity. Flor’s broader intellectual project, then and since, has been to insist that chronic pathological states, whether chronic pain or chronic fear, are best understood as failures or distortions of ordinary learning and plasticity mechanisms rather than as static lesions or fixed traits. Her presence as corresponding author on the Birbaumer paper is the tell that this was conceived as a learning theory paper wearing an imaging paper’s clothes: the question was never simply whether phobics show more amygdala activity, but whether that activity is a specific, acquired response to a particular class of stimulus or a generalized failure of arousal regulation, the same question Flor was asking about cortical maps in amputees.

The imaging itself depended on infrastructure that Wolfgang Grodd had built at Tübingen, where he directed the section of experimental magnetic resonance of the central nervous system from the mid 1990s onward and was among the small number of researchers in Germany doing functional MRI methodology work at all at that point, having already published foundational studies mapping motor and sensorimotor cortex. Functional imaging of a psychiatric population in 1998 was not a service one could simply order from a hospital MRI suite. It required a physicist willing to co-design the acquisition and a clinical group willing to bring a fragile patient population into a scanner environment that had barely been validated for cognitive or emotional tasks, let alone tasks meant to provoke the exact symptom under study.

The design that resulted was spare by necessity. Seven people diagnosed with social phobia and five healthy comparison subjects, twelve people total, were scanned while viewing slides of neutral human faces and, in a separate condition, while exposed to an aversive odor. The odor condition is the paper’s most underappreciated methodological move. If the amygdala simply runs hot in phobic patients as a matter of generalized trait arousal, it should respond more in phobics than controls to any sufficiently unpleasant stimulus, faces included but not exclusively. If instead the amygdala’s abnormal response is specific to socially relevant stimuli, the group difference should appear for faces and disappear, or at least attenuate, for a nonsocial aversive control. The result favored specificity. Amygdala activation to neutral faces was selectively elevated in the phobic group relative to controls, while the odor condition activated the amygdala bilaterally in both groups without a comparable between group difference. The paper’s own conclusion, stated with a plainness that later, more heavily hedged neuroimaging papers would rarely permit themselves, was that this was the first demonstration that the amygdala is active in human phobics when exposed to potentially fear relevant stimuli, with the causal direction, whether the overactivation precedes or follows the phobia, explicitly left open.

That specificity question, whether the amygdala’s hyperresponse in social anxiety reflects something particular about social threat or is better explained as a domain general salience or arousal signal that happens to fire whenever anything sufficiently significant crosses the sensorium, did not originate with Lisa Feldman Barrett’s later constructionist critiques of discrete emotion circuits or with Luiz Pessoa’s arguments for the amygdala as a general relevance detector rather than a dedicated fear module. It was already the design question a small German team was trying to answer with an odor stimulus in a scanner in the mid 1990s, using the crude but honest tool of a single dissociating control condition rather than the large scale multivariate pattern analyses that would eventually be brought to bear on the same question. The tool has changed enormously. The question has not moved at all.

What the paper could not do, and this is the detail that determines its entire subsequent evidentiary fate, is report the finding the way the field would come to require. There is no table of stereotactic coordinates, no whole brain statistical parametric map, because the analysis was conducted as a targeted region of interest test on the amygdala rather than as an exploratory whole brain contrast, which was still a defensible and common choice for a four page rapid communication in 1998 but which becomes a disqualifying gap the moment a later researcher tries to pool this study into a coordinate based meta-analysis. Freitas-Ferrari and colleagues, surveying the neuroimaging literature on social anxiety disorder in 2010, credit this study in exactly the terms the field has settled on, writing that Birbaumer and colleagues observed increased amygdala activation in a small sample of seven patients compared to five controls exposed to neutral faces and aversive odor, and that this was the first demonstration that the bilateral amygdala is involved in processing neutral faces in social anxiety disorder. First demonstration is the phrase that recurs across the secondary literature whenever this paper is invoked. It is also, notably, absent altogether from the reference list and data table of Etkin and Wager’s 2007 meta-analysis, the paper this publication opened its very first edition with, the one that established bilateral amygdala hyperactivation as the most consistent shared finding across post traumatic stress disorder, social anxiety disorder, and specific phobia by pooling coordinates across eight studies of social anxiety disorder specifically. Birbaumer and colleagues is not one of the eight. It could not be, given that it never produced the coordinates a coordinate based meta-analysis requires. The paper that the field remembers as having started the amygdala story is, by the field’s own most authoritative quantitative synthesis, invisible.

Frank Schneider, a coauthor from the Düsseldorf psychiatry group, appears to have registered this limitation almost immediately, publishing a follow up the next year with a larger sample and a differential aversive conditioning paradigm designed to generate exactly the kind of trial by trial, contrast driven data that a whole brain analysis could use. The 1998 finding was, in effect, treated by its own coauthor as a proof of concept in need of a proper replication rather than as a completed demonstration, which is a more honest relationship to one’s own landmark paper than the secondary literature’s first demonstration framing usually implies.

Three years later and roughly fifteen hundred kilometers north, Mats Fredrikson’s laboratory at Uppsala was running a different kind of foundational study, and the difference in paradigm turns out to matter as much as the difference in modality. Fredrikson had already spent a decade using positron emission tomography to study regional cerebral blood flow during experimentally induced phobic fear, most notably in snake phobics, before turning that same instrumentation toward social phobia specifically. The move from a nonsocial, evolutionarily prepared fear object to a socially constructed one, public speaking, was not incidental. It reflected a considered bet that social phobia’s neural signature would look meaningfully different from a simple animal phobia’s, because the eliciting stimulus in social phobia is not a fixed perceptual object but an evaluative social situation whose threat value depends on an audience’s imagined judgment.

Maria Tillfors and Tomas Furmark were, at the time, graduate students and close collaborators of Fredrikson’s at Uppsala’s department of psychology, and this paper is drawn directly from the epidemiology to brain function arc that structured Furmark’s 2000 doctoral thesis, a research program that began with population level surveys of social phobia prevalence and moved, study by study, toward its neural substrate. Furmark would go on to build an independent professorship at Uppsala centered on exactly the mechanistic question this paper first raised, using PET imaging of the serotonin system and, later, treatment response studies comparing citalopram and cognitive behavioral therapy, making this 2001 paper not a terminal finding but an early way station in a research program that is still active today. Bengt Långström’s presence on the author list is easy to overlook and probably should not be. Långström was among the founding figures of Swedish PET radiochemistry, having helped build the Uppsala PET Centre’s carbon eleven tracer production capability from the ground up, and it is this kind of in house methodological infrastructure, not merely clinical access to social phobia patients, that let a psychology department produce this volume of symptom provocation PET work in an era when most groups could not.

The paradigm itself was a public versus private speaking task. Participants delivered an impromptu speech about themselves, once in front of an audience and once alone, while cerebral blood flow was measured via PET using radiolabeled water, and while heart rate and self reported anxiety were tracked concurrently. Eighteen people meeting DSM-IV criteria for social phobia and six nonphobic comparison subjects completed the protocol, an asymmetry in group size that looks jarring by contemporary standards but that reflected the genuine ethical and logistical cost of exposing healthy volunteers to repeated radioactive tracer doses purely as a comparison group, a cost that shaped control group sizes across an entire generation of activation PET studies and that later imaging methods, dependent on no ionizing radiation at all, would simply make obsolete as a constraint.

Subjective anxiety rose far more sharply from the private to the public condition in the phobic group than in comparison subjects, which is the manipulation check succeeding exactly as intended. The central neural finding was elevated blood flow in the amygdaloid complex in social phobics relative to comparison subjects during public relative to private speaking, and, more tellingly, the magnitude of that right amygdala blood flow increase correlated with the magnitude of each participant’s own self reported fear increase, a correlation of .52 within the patient group. That is a real, if modest, within group brain behavior correlation of the kind later, larger studies would use far more sophisticated multivariate methods to characterize with more precision, but the basic logic, that the amygdala signal should scale with subjectively experienced fear rather than simply differing by diagnostic group, is already present here in its simplest possible form.

The more theoretically interesting result is what happened outside the amygdala. Comparison subjects showed increased blood flow during public relative to private speaking in orbitofrontal cortex, insular cortex, and temporal pole, along with increases in perirhinal and retrosplenial cortex that social phobics did not show. Social phobics, meanwhile, showed decreased blood flow in orbitofrontal cortex, insula, and temporal pole under the same public speaking stress, along with reduced flow in parietal and secondary visual cortex. This is not merely more or less activation in the same places. It is a directional double dissociation: the nonanxious brain under social evaluative stress recruits more of a distributed cortical network that supports appraisal, evaluation, and self monitoring, while the anxious brain under the identical stress recruits less of that same network and shows its clearest increase in a phylogenetically older subcortical structure. Tillfors and colleagues framed this explicitly as evidence for a shift, under stress, toward a more primitive danger recognition system in social phobia, at the expense of the cortical evaluative machinery that a nonanxious brain deploys instead.

Read against this publication’s second edition, that framing is startlingly prescient. Grupe and Nitschke’s 2013 uncertainty and anticipation model would eventually formalize something very close to this same cortical subcortical trade off, with its account of an aMCC that integrates cortical control signals and an anterior insula and amygdala axis that, when that integration fails, drives escalating subcortical alarm. Koban and colleagues’ 2023 finding that the frontoparietal network fails to elaborate positive social feedback in social anxiety disorder is, in a sense, the same cortical withdrawal Tillfors and colleagues observed in orbitofrontal and insular cortex more than two decades earlier, now characterized with a reinforcement learning model and a mediation analysis instead of a raw regional blood flow contrast. The 2001 paper did not have the computational vocabulary or the whole brain multivariate methods that would later make this trade off precise and mechanistically testable. It had, essentially, one correlation and a pattern of directional contrasts across a short list of anatomically defined regions. But the shape of the claim, cortical engagement substituting for subcortical alarm in the nonanxious brain, and subcortical alarm substituting for cortical engagement in the anxious one, was already fully formed.

This paper’s evidentiary fate could not be more different from Birbaumer and colleagues’. It is explicitly cited and its data explicitly included in Etkin and Wager’s 2007 meta-analytic table, one of eight social anxiety disorder studies, alongside work by Amir, Kilts, Lorberbaum, Phan, Stein, and two studies by Straube, that supplied the actual coordinate level data underlying the field’s consensus claim about amygdala hyperactivation. It receives no special first demonstration billing in the secondary literature, no rhetorical flourish marking it as the founding study of anything. It simply became, quietly and without fanfare, part of the evidentiary corpus that a later, more authoritative synthesis actually used.

Put the two papers next to each other and a genuinely uncomfortable asymmetry appears. The study that the field narrates as its origin point, the first demonstration of amygdala involvement in social phobia, exists almost entirely in the register of citation and reputation. It shaped how people talk about the discipline’s beginnings without ever entering the discipline’s actual quantitative evidence base, because its reporting practices, defensible and ordinary for a 1998 rapid communication, simply do not meet the coordinate level requirements that a later methodological standard would impose retroactively. The study that the field narrates with no particular reverence, a PET paper from a Swedish psychology department that nobody describes as a first anything, is the one whose actual numbers were pooled, weighted, and synthesized into the claim everyone now treats as established. Fame and evidentiary standing came apart, and they came apart in exactly the direction you would not predict from citation counts alone, since Birbaumer and colleagues has accumulated roughly as many citations as Tillfors and colleagues despite carrying none of the latter’s weight inside the field’s own formal synthesis.

This is not a minor bibliographic curiosity. It is a case study in how a young field constructs its origin myth out of whichever early result states its conclusion most quotably, rather than out of whichever early result actually survives the field’s later methodological maturation. A discipline that wants to be taken seriously as a cumulative science has to be honest about this gap, because the alternative is a kind of unexamined folklore in which everyone can recite that the amygdala story started somewhere in the late 1990s without anyone being quite sure whether the paper they are thinking of is the one that actually contributed data to the claim they believe it established. The correct response is not to strip Birbaumer and colleagues of its historical significance, which is real and was earned honestly under the reporting norms of its moment, but to hold two facts in mind at once: that a paper can be genuinely first, genuinely important to how a field came to think about itself, and genuinely absent from the quantitative record that field later built to justify that thinking.

There is also a more specific scientific point buried in the contrast, and it concerns paradigm rather than politics. Birbaumer and colleagues established the amygdala’s abnormal response to a passively viewed social stimulus, a neutral face. Tillfors and colleagues established the amygdala’s abnormal response, alongside a broader cortical withdrawal, to an actively performed social stressor, public speech. Between them, these two designs are close to the entire ancestry of every social anxiety disorder neuroimaging paradigm that followed. Face viewing tasks, whether passive, implicit, or embedded in more elaborate emotion regulation designs like Goldin and colleagues’ reappraisal study from this publication’s first edition, trace their lineage to the Tübingen design. Performance and evaluation paradigms, whether public speaking, simulated social feedback of the kind Koban and colleagues used, or anticipated peer judgment tasks in developmental samples, trace theirs to the Uppsala design. This is worth sitting with, because it means the field’s celebrated convergence across dozens of subsequent studies, all pointing to amygdala hyperreactivity in social anxiety disorder, is not obviously an independent convergence of evidence from unrelated methods. It may in substantial part be a convergence produced by every subsequent study inheriting one of exactly two experimental templates laid down by these two small studies in 1998 and 2001. A finding that replicates across fifty studies that all descend from two ancestral paradigms has not been tested fifty independent times. It has been tested twice, with forty eight variations on the theme.

That is the genuinely unresolved question this edition leaves standing, and it is not a rhetorical one. Nobody has yet run a social threat processing study in social anxiety disorder using a paradigm that is not, at some level of description, a descendant of passive face viewing or active evaluative performance. Until someone does, that is, until a design appears that provokes social threat processing through some structurally independent route, perhaps through naturalistic social interaction paradigms, through virtual reality environments with genuinely novel evaluative structures, through paradigms built around written or narrative social rejection rather than faces or speech, the field cannot fully distinguish between two possibilities that look identical from where we currently stand. One possibility is that the amygdala genuinely is a robust, method independent marker of social threat processing in this disorder, and every descendant paradigm keeps finding it because it is really there. The other is that two underpowered founding studies happened to land on a true but narrow finding under a narrow set of experimental conditions, and three decades of methodological inheritance have mistaken repetition of the same basic design for independent replication of the underlying claim. Deciding between those two stories is not a matter of running a fifty first face viewing study. It is a matter of finally testing the claim somewhere neither Tübingen nor Uppsala ever looked.
