The Social Brain Dispatch

The Average Child Who Never Existed

A ten year German cohort of three thousand fixed social anxiety's cumulative incidence at eleven percent and its most dangerous years at ages ten through nineteen, one curve for one average adolescent. Fifteen years later, a Canadian study aimed a growth mixture model at the same age window and found that the eleven percent was never a single population. It was mostly the trace of one subgroup, fifteen percent of the sample, already distinguishable from everyone else before most of them had crossed anything a diagnostic interview would count as onset.

Edition No. 8 · Developmental Trajectories and Longitudinal Cohorts · July 30, 2026

Every disorder has an epidemiology and every epidemiology has a shape, and for social anxiety disorder that shape has mostly been drawn one way, as a single curve rising and falling across the life span, with a peak somewhere in adolescence and a long, gentler decline after it. That curve is not wrong so much as it is a summary, an average taken across every person who ever developed the disorder, and averages are exactly the kind of object that developmental psychopathology has spent the last two decades learning to distrust. The discipline has a name for what this Dispatch's earlier editions have mostly done without saying so, variable-centered developmental science, the practice of asking how a given risk factor moves a given outcome across an entire sample treated as one population, holding everyone to the same underlying model and letting individuals differ only in where they sit along it. Its rival, person-centered developmental science, asks a more unsettling question, whether the population secretly contains more than one model at all, whether some substantial slice of the sample is not a variant of the average trajectory but riding an entirely different one, with its own starting point, its own slope, and its own eventual destination. The distinction sounds almost philosophical until it is turned on a specific, load-bearing number, and the two papers under review here turn it on the single number this field has cited more than almost any other, the claim that social anxiety disorder is fundamentally an adolescent-onset condition with its incidence concentrated in a specific decade of life. One paper built that number. The other, working with a different cohort, a different instrument, and an approach explicitly designed to break the assumption that a population is a population, went looking for the people who actually made up the number and found that most of them were never really there.

Hans-Ulrich Wittchen did not set out, across a career that made him one of the two or three most consequential psychiatric epidemiologists in Europe, to study social anxiety disorder in particular. His signature contribution to the field was methodological before it was substantive, co-developing and relentlessly validating the Composite International Diagnostic Interview, the structured instrument that let the World Health Organization and a generation of national surveys ask, in a standardized and replicable way, who actually has a mental disorder rather than who merely reports distress on a screening scale. Wittchen spent the first half of his career, much of it built around the Munich-based Composite International Diagnostic Interview program and large cross-sectional surveys in the mold of the American National Comorbidity Survey he collaborated with Ronald Kessler to help internationalize, establishing how many people have which disorders at a single point in time. What he built next, the Early Developmental Stages of Psychopathology study, was a deliberate escalation past that cross-sectional ceiling, a prospective-longitudinal program launched in Munich in the mid-1990s specifically to capture not prevalence but incidence and course, not how many people currently qualify for a diagnosis but when, across the arc of a life already underway, a disorder first appears and what happens to the person afterward. That is a categorically harder thing to measure than prevalence, requiring a sample followed forward in time with repeated, comparably rigorous diagnostic interviews rather than one, and Wittchen recruited an age-stratified community sample of just over three thousand adolescents and young Munich residents to do it, a scale of undertaking that only a handful of programs anywhere in psychiatric epidemiology have matched before or since. Katja Beesdo, then a doctoral researcher working under Wittchen and Roselind Lieb, took the specific question of social anxiety disorder's incidence and course and turned it into the paper reviewed here, a project she would carry forward for the rest of her own career, eventually becoming a professor in her own right at the Technische Universität Dresden and running its outpatient clinic for anxiety disorders, the kind of trajectory, a doctoral analysis becoming a lifelong clinical and scientific specialization, that this Dispatch has by now seen more than once. Daniel Pine's presence on the byline is this publication's most familiar recurring signature, the same NIMH developmental framing that shaped the adolescent social reorientation theory in Edition No. 4 and the fear-conditioning meta-analyses in Edition No. 7, here lending an American developmental-psychopathology vocabulary, onset windows, sensitive periods, cascading comorbidity, to a German epidemiological infrastructure that had, until this collaboration, mostly spoken in the flatter language of prevalence and risk ratios.

The machinery behind Beesdo and colleagues' central claims deserves a moment of its own, because a phrase like cumulative incidence of eleven percent conceals a fair amount of statistical apparatus. The Early Developmental Stages of Psychopathology cohort was interviewed at baseline and then reassessed at up to three follow-up waves across roughly a decade, using the German Munich-Composite International Diagnostic Interview to establish, at each wave, both current diagnostic status and, crucially, the age at which any incident disorder had first appeared, an estimate built from a mix of prospective reassessment, catching a disorder as it emerges between waves, and retrospective dating within the interview itself, which carries the field's well known vulnerability to telescoping, a tendency for respondents to misdate the true onset of a chronic or recurrent problem, usually pulling it forward in time toward the interview itself. Because the sample entered the study at staggered ages, some already fourteen to seventeen, others eighteen to twenty four, the analysis could not simply divide the number of new cases by the number of people at risk. It instead modeled incidence as a hazard, the instantaneous risk of first onset conditional on having survived, in the survival-analysis sense of not yet having developed the disorder, up to that age, expressed per person-year of observation within a given age band, and then used Cox proportional hazards regression to ask which risk factors, behavioral inhibition, panic symptoms, family history, shifted that hazard up or down across the whole observed age range. A hazard ratio of 1.85 for panic, in this framework, does not mean panic doubles someone's chance of developing social anxiety disorder in some fixed window, it means that at any given age, across the entire follow-up period, a person with panic symptoms is carrying 1.85 times the instantaneous risk of onset that a person without them is carrying, an average multiplier applied uniformly across the sample's whole developmental span. That uniformity assumption, that one multiplier can describe everyone equally well at every age, is precisely the assumption the second paper in this edition was built to interrogate.

Run through that machinery, the Early Developmental Stages of Psychopathology data produced the numbers that have since become close to boilerplate in any review of social anxiety's epidemiology. Cumulative incidence of the disorder across the observed age range came to 11.0 percent, against a considerably larger 27.0 percent for major depression, and the hazard of a first onset was sharply age-dependent rather than flat, cresting between ages ten and nineteen at roughly 0.72 percent per hundred person-years and falling to about a fifth of that rate, 0.20 percent, in the years before that window and to a similarly low 0.19 percent afterward. That single asymmetric hump, low, then a steep decade-long plateau, then low again, is the empirical basis for calling social anxiety disorder an adolescent-onset condition rather than a childhood or an adult one, and it held up well enough across the sample that Beesdo and colleagues could also show, using the same hazard framework, that having social anxiety disorder measurably raised a person's later risk of a first depressive episode, a relative risk in a range of 1.49 to 1.85 depending on which covariates the model controlled for, with behavioral inhibition, the same temperamental construct at the center of Kagan's work reviewed in Edition No. 6, emerging as a significant predictor of that progression at a hazard ratio of 1.30 and comorbid panic symptoms emerging as a considerably stronger one at 1.85. The paper's own framing of this result, that social anxiety functions as an early, adolescent-onset gateway into a substantially and consistently elevated risk of subsequent depression, has been repeated in review after review since 2007 largely because it is exactly the kind of clean, population-level, clinically actionable claim that a well-powered epidemiological cohort is built to produce, a specific age window in which to intervene and a specific downstream disorder to intervene against. What that framing cannot do, by the design of the hazard model itself, is say whether the eleven percent who crossed into a diagnosis, or the twenty seven percent who crossed into depression, arrived there by the same route, whether a teenager whose social fear escalated visibly from age ten looks anything at all, statistically, like one whose social fear seemed to appear abruptly at seventeen. A single hazard curve, no matter how precisely estimated, is built to describe an average member of the population at each age, and an average member is a statistical convenience rather than a person any clinician has ever actually treated.

Tracy Vaillancourt did not come to this question from epidemiology at all. Her career, first at McMaster University and now as a Tier 1 Canada Research Chair in Children's Mental Health and Violence Prevention at the University of Ottawa, has been built almost entirely around bullying, peer victimization, and aggression, tracing how children's positions in the social hierarchy of a school shape their biology and their later mental health, work that has as often measured cortisol and testosterone as it has measured symptom checklists. The infrastructure behind the paper reviewed here, a longitudinal cohort recruited out of Hamilton, Ontario schools starting in grade five and known first as the McMaster Teen Study before following Vaillancourt to Ottawa as the Health and Peer Relations Study, was built for that purpose, to track how bullying and victimization unfold across adolescence into early adulthood, not to build a dedicated developmental account of social anxiety disorder. Amanda Krygsman, the paper's first author, is not primarily a social anxiety researcher either. She is a senior research associate who manages the Health and Peer Relations Study day to day and whose own listed expertise is in longitudinal statistics and design, someone whose contribution to this literature is less a novel theoretical claim about social fear than a technical fluency in exactly the kind of person-centered modeling the question demanded. That combination is worth pausing on, because it is not an isolated case in this Dispatch's coverage so far. The Early Developmental Stages of Psychopathology cohort reviewed just above was itself a general psychopathology infrastructure, built to track incidence across the full range of anxiety and mood disorders rather than social anxiety specifically, with Beesdo's contribution being to extract a social-anxiety-specific analysis from a dataset designed for something broader. Neither of this edition's two papers comes from a cohort purpose-built around social anxiety disorder. Both are, in a real sense, guests inside someone else's longitudinal infrastructure, one built for general psychiatric epidemiology, the other for the study of bullying, and that pattern, a field that studies its own central disorder mostly by borrowing other programs' longitudinal architecture, says something quiet but real about how thin the dedicated developmental data on social anxiety specifically still is.

What Krygsman and Vaillancourt brought to that borrowed infrastructure was growth mixture modeling, a technique built to do exactly what a Cox hazard model structurally cannot, ask whether a sample's developmental data are better described as one population following one growth curve or as a mixture of several distinct latent subpopulations, each with its own trajectory shape, its own intercept and slope, estimated simultaneously with the probability that any given individual belongs to each one. Where a hazard model assumes homogeneity and estimates how a covariate shifts risk uniformly across everyone, a growth mixture model assumes heterogeneity as its starting premise and asks the data to reveal how many qualitatively different developmental stories are hiding inside a single sample, choosing the number of latent classes through a combination of fit statistics like the Bayesian information criterion and classification diagnostics like entropy that measure how cleanly individuals can be sorted into one class rather than another. Applied to repeated self-report measures of social anxiety symptoms collected from roughly seven hundred and one participants across annual waves running from age ten through eighteen, with adult diagnostic outcomes assessed at ages nineteen to twenty two, the model did not return one smooth adolescent-onset hump. It returned three separate trajectory classes, a low class comprising 47.2 percent of the sample whose symptoms stayed flat and mild throughout, a moderate class of 37.3 percent with symptoms elevated but comparatively stable, and a high increasing class of just 15.5 percent whose symptoms started elevated relative to the other two groups by age ten and then climbed steadily across the entire observed window rather than peaking and receding.

The consequences of belonging to that smallest class were not modest. Relative to the low trajectory group, membership in the high increasing class carried an odds ratio of 15.74 for meeting criteria for social anxiety disorder in early adulthood and 11.72 for the moderate group, a pattern echoed almost across the board for other adult diagnoses, generalized anxiety disorder at 13.08 and 8.98, panic disorder at 8.43 and 5.90, agoraphobia at 16.39 and 9.68, obsessive-compulsive disorder at a comparatively more modest but still significant 3.49 and 2.98, and a depressive episode at 19.75 for the high group, the single largest odds ratio in the paper. Odds ratios of that magnitude deserve a numerate reader's caution rather than uncritical repetition, since values in the mid-teens to high-teens this large, especially drawn from a sample of seven hundred split across three classes and then cross-tabulated against seven separate low base-rate adult diagnoses, often signal thin cell counts in the underlying two by two tables rather than a uniformly precise estimate, and the paper's own reliance on self-report symptom measures during the developmental window, rather than the structured diagnostic interviews Beesdo's team used, means the trajectory classes themselves are a modeled construct whose boundaries depend on how many classes the fit statistics were willing to support, not a directly observed clinical reality the way a Composite International Diagnostic Interview diagnosis is. One further finding stood apart from the general pattern of elevated risk across nearly everything, that the high increasing trajectory, and only that trajectory, predicted cannabis use in adulthood, with no corresponding elevation in alcohol use, an asymmetry that resists an easy story about generic substance vulnerability and instead suggests something more particular about how a socially fearful young adult chooses a substance, one whose subjective effects and consumption context differ meaningfully from the more socially embedded ritual of drinking.

Set beside each other, these two papers are not so much in conflict as in different registers describing the same underlying phenomenon, and each one's design exposes exactly what the other's cannot see. Beesdo and colleagues' adolescent-onset hump, the finding that has anchored a generation of review articles, is almost certainly not the signature of the whole sample gradually accumulating risk together. It is far more likely the signature of something close to Krygsman and Vaillancourt's high increasing class, a minority subgroup whose symptoms were already diverging from their peers by age ten, crossing a full diagnostic threshold in a concentrated burst sometime in the ensuing decade and, in doing so, generating almost the entire aggregate incidence curve that a hazard model then attributes, by construction, to the population as a whole. An eleven percent cumulative incidence rate is not eleven percent of a homogeneous population moving in lockstep through the same age-graded risk. It is far closer, if the two cohorts describe the same underlying reality, to being mostly the trace of one relatively small, already-distinguishable subgroup crossing a line that a diagnostic interview happens to be built to detect, with a considerably larger, more moderate group contributing a smaller share and a genuinely low-risk majority contributing almost none. That reframing cuts the other way too. Krygsman and Vaillancourt's person-centered model can identify that a high increasing class exists and that its members carry enormous downstream risk, but it cannot, on its own, tell a clinician when within that decade-long climb an actual disorder began, whether the diagnostically meaningful threshold was crossed at eleven or at seventeen, the exact question Beesdo's age-conditional hazard framework was purpose-built to answer. The epidemiological study gives the field a rigorously dated onset window built on validated diagnostic criteria but blurs it across a population that was never actually uniform. The trajectory study gives the field the heterogeneity that population was hiding but cannot, by itself, date a diagnosis or claim the representativeness of a stratified community sample assessed with a structured clinical interview. Neither flaw is a reason to discount either paper. Together they suggest that the field's most repeated developmental fact about social anxiety disorder, that it is an adolescent-onset condition peaking between ten and nineteen, describes an average trajectory that essentially nobody in the underlying population is actually on, an artifact of exactly the kind person-centered developmental science exists to catch.

What neither paper can settle, and what a reader who has now absorbed both should sit with, is what the high increasing trajectory actually is a trajectory of. Krygsman and Vaillancourt's own results offer a clue that cuts against treating it as a dedicated pathway into social anxiety disorder specifically, since that same fifteen and a half percent of the sample showed sharply elevated odds not only for social anxiety disorder but for generalized anxiety disorder, panic disorder, agoraphobia, obsessive-compulsive disorder, and a depressive episode, a nearly indiscriminate elevation across the entire internalizing spectrum rather than a specific arrow pointed at one diagnosis. Beesdo and colleagues, working in the opposite direction, found evidence for a more targeted claim, that social anxiety disorder in particular, not anxiety in some generic sense, elevates the specific subsequent risk of depression, a sequencing result that depends on their being able to date one disorder as preceding the other within the same hazard framework. Read against each other, the two studies leave open whether a single developmental trajectory, visible by age ten and devastating by early adulthood, represents a specific pathway into social anxiety that then cascades outward into other disorders the way Beesdo's sequencing logic implies, or whether it is instead the earliest visible expression of a broader, undifferentiated liability to internalizing psychopathology in general, one that a symptom checklist happens to label social anxiety at age ten only because social evaluative fear is developmentally the most salient thing available to a ten year old to report, with the eventual diagnostic label, social anxiety, generalized anxiety, depression, panic, being decided by something other than the trajectory itself. Two of the most careful developmental datasets this field has produced converge on the existence of that fifteen percent and on how much is riding on their early identification. Neither can yet say whether what develops in them is social anxiety finding its cause, or merely social anxiety being the first name a broader vulnerability happens to go by.
