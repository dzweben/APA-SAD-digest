The Social Brain Dispatch

The Temperament That Only a Mother Could See

A fourteen-year prospective cohort found that toddlers whose mothers rated them as stably behaviorally inhibited were nearly four times more likely to carry a lifetime diagnosis of social anxiety disorder into adolescence, yet three of every four adolescents who actually developed the disorder had never been flagged as inhibited at all. A separate cohort, tracking the same nominal trait from age nine to twenty one, found that when adolescents were asked to rate their own inhibited temperament, it predicted nothing about which of three distinct social anxiety trajectories they were already riding.

Edition No. 8 · Developmental Trajectories and Longitudinal Cohorts · August 6, 2026

Developmental psychopathology has never fully settled which of two questions actually explains a disorder's origin. The older and more intuitive question asks about antecedents, whether some marker present before the disorder exists raises the odds that it will eventually appear, and it produces the kind of finding this Dispatch has already taught twice, a genome that explains almost nothing of who becomes anxious, a temperament visible in a two-year-old's approach to a stranger that explains rather more. The newer question, and the one that now dominates how longitudinal data actually gets analyzed, asks about course instead of cause, treating the individual trajectory rather than the individual predictor as the field's real unit of analysis, indifferent for the moment to where a given trajectory started and interested only in how many shapes the disorder's development actually takes and how many people ride each one. Two studies, fielded four years apart on opposite sides of the Atlantic, put these two questions to what looks like the same construct, a temperamental inhibition that keeps a child quiet around strangers, and returned answers that do not so much contradict each other as reveal how differently the two traditions are willing to define what counts as evidence. One found a modest but real elevation in risk that nonetheless failed to touch most of the adolescents who went on to develop the disorder. The other found that the very trait driving the first study's headline number vanished the moment researchers asked the subjects themselves, rather than their mothers, to report it. The gap between those two results is not noise. It is close to the whole unresolved problem of when, in a life, social anxiety actually starts to become itself.

The behavioral-inhibition side of this pairing comes out of a laboratory whose fingerprints, cited elsewhere in this Dispatch, belong mostly to Nathan Fox, a University of Maryland developmental psychologist who has spent the better part of four decades running one of only two major North American cohorts built explicitly to track behavioral inhibition from infancy forward, the other belonging to Jerome Kagan's Harvard program that this publication has already covered directly. Where Kagan's original claim rested on a two-year-old's motor and vocal reactivity to novelty, observed once or twice and coded by hand, Fox built his career on pushing that same construct through more informants, more timepoints, and eventually more biology, pairing behavioral coding with resting frontal EEG asymmetry to ask not just whether a child looks inhibited but what that inhibition looks like happening inside the brain. The paper under review here, published in 2009 in the Journal of the American Academy of Child and Adolescent Psychiatry, is Fox's cohort put to its most direct clinical test yet, whether behavioral inhibition measured across early childhood actually predicts a diagnosable psychiatric disorder fourteen years later rather than merely a continuous trait score. Andrea Chronis-Tuscano, the paper's first author, is not herself a temperament researcher by training, her own program at Maryland centers on parenting interventions and attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder, and her presence on this byline reads as exactly what it likely was, a clinical diagnostician brought in to do what an inhibition lab alone could not, run and adjudicate structured psychiatric interviews with any rigor. The paper's senior clinical authority belongs instead to Daniel Pine, chief of the mood and anxiety disorders program at the National Institute of Mental Health, whose name has now appeared in this Dispatch attached to a theory of adolescent neural reorientation, a meta-analysis of fear conditioning, and now a longitudinal test of temperamental risk, a small but real illustration of how few hands the American study of pediatric anxiety actually passes through.

The cohort itself began as 443 four-month-old infants screened from Washington-area hospital birth records, narrowed to 178 selected for an oversample of high-reactive temperament profiles, and followed with maternal-report inhibition measures at 14 months, 24 months, four years, and seven years, using the Toddler Behavior Assessment Questionnaire's Social Fearfulness scale in toddlerhood and the Colorado Children's Temperament Inventory's Shyness and Sociability subscale in early childhood. Rather than the median splits and single-timepoint cutoffs that had defined most of the BI literature Kagan's own 1988 paper helped launch, Chronis-Tuscano and her coauthors ran a latent profile analysis across all four childhood timepoints simultaneously, letting the data itself locate a small class of children, twenty in total, who sat in the inhibited top third of the distribution at every single assessment, against a much larger class of 139 who did not, with average posterior classification probabilities between .87 and .98, a meaningfully higher evidentiary bar than simply asking whether a child looked shy on one afternoon in a laboratory. Notably, the same exercise run on behavioral observation data, rather than maternal report, produced no groupings that predicted anything in adolescence at all, a result the authors report plainly rather than bury, and one that already complicates any simple story about which informant actually captures the construct that matters. When the cohort was reassessed at fourteen to sixteen years old, 129 of the original participants, 81 percent of those retained through childhood, sat for K-SADS diagnostic interviews conducted separately with adolescents and their parents and supplemented with probes from the Anxiety Disorders Interview Schedule for Children, interviewers blind to all childhood temperament data, disagreements resolved by expert consensus, interrater kappas of .92 for anxiety diagnoses specifically confirming the diagnostic process itself was not the weak link in whatever the study would go on to find.

What it found was real but narrower than the paper's own title implies. Adolescents whose mothers had rated them as stably inhibited across childhood met criteria for a lifetime social anxiety disorder diagnosis at a rate of 40 percent, six of fifteen, against 15 percent, sixteen of 107, among adolescents in the low-inhibition class, an odds ratio of 3.79 with a 95 percent confidence interval running from 1.18 to 12.12, comfortably significant even accounting for how few high-inhibition adolescents the design ultimately retained. Lifetime anxiety disorders more broadly showed the same direction at a weaker magnitude, an odds ratio of 2.93 that just missed conventional significance at p=.055, and continuous self- and parent-rated social phobia symptoms on the SCARED-R told a consistent story, a moderate effect by adolescent self-report, Cohen's d of 0.39, and a considerably larger one by parent report, d of 0.75. Mood disorders and disruptive behavior disorders showed no association with childhood inhibition in either direction, evidence that whatever the temperament measure was tracking, it was tracking something with real specificity to anxiety and, within anxiety, most sharply to the social variant. And then, quietly, in a result the paper does not dwell on but does not hide either, stable childhood inhibition predicted no elevation whatsoever in current diagnoses at the fourteen-to-sixteen assessment, only lifetime ones, an asymmetry the authors read as evidence that a meaningful share of the risk this temperament confers resolves, whether through natural remission or ordinary developmental change, well before mid-adolescence closes the window in which the diagnosis was first counted.

If Fox and Chronis-Tuscano's question is where risk begins, the second paper's question is what risk actually looks like once it is already underway, and it comes out of an entirely different institutional tradition. Pieter Westenberg's Developmental and Educational Psychology group at Leiden University runs the SAND study, Social Anxiety and Normal Development, built from the outset around a premise the NIMH-Maryland axis does not share, that social anxiety is best studied first as a dimension distributed across an ordinary, nonclinical population rather than as a categorical illness sampled from people who already have it. Westenberg's own scholarly signature, running through decades of work on ego development, self-concept, and later the neural and physiological correlates of social evaluation in adolescence, has always leaned toward treating social anxiety as continuous with normal adolescent self-consciousness rather than as a discrete pathology grafted onto it, and the SAND cohort, drawn from one secondary and two primary schools and followed from ages nine through twenty one, was explicitly designed, in the authors' own framing, as the first study to trace social anxiety's developmental course across that entire span in a community rather than clinical sample. Anna Miers, the paper's first author, working alongside Anke Blöte, Mark de Rooij's methodological expertise, Chantal Bokhorst, and Westenberg as senior investigator, published the resulting analysis in 2013 in the Journal of Abnormal Child Psychology, four years after Chronis-Tuscano's paper and asking, in effect, a mirror-image question, not whether an early trait predicts a later diagnosis but how many distinguishable developmental shapes social anxiety symptoms take across adolescence and what, if anything, tells those shapes apart while they are happening.

The design behind that question is considerably more elaborate than a simple repeated-measures survey. Of 331 adolescents assessed at wave one, mean age 13.34, retention held between 83 and 95 percent across four waves spanning roughly five years, with missing-data analyses confirming attrition was unrelated to baseline age, social anxiety, or gender. At the first and third waves, participants came to the university for two separate sessions, a pre-lab battery of cognitive and pubertal-development measures followed by a lab session built around the Leiden Public Speaking Task, a five-minute speech on assigned topics delivered in front of a prerecorded audience of four boys and four girls matched to the speaker's own age plus a female teacher, a paradigm designed to generate a genuine, observable social-evaluative stressor rather than rely on self-report alone. Social anxiety itself was tracked continuously across all four waves using the Social Anxiety Scale for Adolescents, and rather than fitting a single average growth curve to the whole sample, as most developmental studies of the era still did, Miers and her coauthors ran a latent class growth model through SAS's PROC TRAJ procedure, comparing one- through six-class solutions on Bayesian information criterion and a minimum five percent group-size threshold, retaining higher-order polynomial terms only where they improved fit. A three-class solution won cleanly, average posterior classification probabilities all exceeding .80, confidence bands around the three trajectories nonoverlapping across nearly the entire age range, evidence that the data was not merely tolerating three groups but actively demanding them.

Those three trajectories are worth sitting with in some detail, because their shapes carry as much information as their labels. The largest, moderate and decreasing, held 54.2 percent of the sample, 183 adolescents, starting at a moderate symptom level and drifting gently downward across the study window. A second group, low and decreasing, comprised 36.2 percent, 118 adolescents, starting low and staying low. The third, high and changing, held only 9.6 percent, thirty adolescents, but its shape was the one the paper's title promises, beginning elevated at age ten, climbing further through early adolescence, peaking, and only then reversing to decline and level off from around age eighteen onward, a genuinely nonlinear course that a study limited to two or three widely spaced assessments could never have resolved. What distinguished that high-and-changing minority from the much larger moderate group, when the authors tested cognition, social competence, and temperament as predictors in separate multinomial logistic regressions, turned out to be surprisingly specific. Negative interpretations of ambiguous social situations, assessed at wave one, nearly tripled the odds of high-trajectory membership relative to the moderate group, odds ratio 3.09, and self-focused attention during the speech task did the same at a smaller magnitude, odds ratio 2.27. Observer-rated social skill deficits and self-reported problems with peers at school each independently predicted the high trajectory as well. And actual observable nervousness during the speech, rated by trained coders blind to group membership, predicted nothing at all, the authors concluding in terms this Dispatch's reader will recognize as a restatement, in a very different idiom, of what its second edition already taught about biased self-evaluation, that it is not how anxious an adolescent visibly appears but how that adolescent interprets and dwells on the appearance of anxiety that carries the developmental weight.

Then comes the result that turns this pairing from a simple confirmation into something closer to a genuine argument. Among the temperament variables tested against trajectory membership, extraversion, social withdrawal, and, specifically, self-reported behavioral inhibition, none distinguished the high-and-changing group from the moderate one at all. Only neuroticism showed any effect, and even that only separated the low group from the moderate group, not the clinically interesting high group from anyone. The construct that, four years earlier and on the other side of the Atlantic, had multiplied a Maryland adolescent's odds of lifetime social anxiety disorder by nearly four, when asked of Leiden's own adolescents about themselves at age thirteen, explained essentially none of which developmental course their own social anxiety symptoms were already following.

It is tempting to read that gap as a contradiction and move on, but the more useful reading treats it as two well-designed studies exposing each other's blind spot. Start with the number Chronis-Tuscano's own data makes available but does not foreground, that of the twenty two adolescents in her sample who met lifetime criteria for social anxiety disorder, only six, 27 percent, had been identified as stably inhibited in childhood at all. Sixteen of the twenty two, nearly three in four, came from the low-inhibition class, the class that behavioral inhibition screening, however well measured, would have waved through as unremarkable. An odds ratio of 3.79 is a genuinely strong individual-level relative-risk finding, and it is entirely compatible with behavioral inhibition explaining only a small slice of the population-level variance in who actually develops the disorder, because base rates and screening sensitivity are different questions from relative risk, and a marker can multiply an individual's odds severalfold while still missing most of the eventual cases, simply because most eventual cases were never especially inhibited to begin with. That arithmetic is exactly the space Miers and Westenberg's cognitive and social-competence predictors move into. If early temperament accounts for only a minority of who ends up with clinical-level social anxiety, then whatever accounts for the rest has to be found somewhere else in development, and negative interpretation bias, self-focused attention, and peer-rated social skill deficits, all measured contemporaneously rather than retrospectively, are exactly the kind of somewhere-else candidates a purely temperamental account was never built to find.

The self-report null deserves its own scrutiny rather than a shrug, because it is not obviously a null about behavioral inhibition itself so much as a null about a particular way of measuring it. Fox and Chronis-Tuscano's construct was built from multiple informants across four childhood timepoints, maternal report validated against laboratory behavioral coding, with a latent profile model explicitly rewarding stability over any single occasion. Miers and Westenberg's construct was a single adolescent's single-occasion self-report of their own inhibited temperament, collected at the same wave as the outcome it was being asked to predict, a design that conflates the predictor with the very self-perception the paper's cognitive-bias findings suggest is already distorted in exactly the adolescents most worth predicting. An adolescent riding the high-and-changing trajectory, already primed toward negative self-interpretation and heightened self-focus, is not obviously a reliable narrator of their own baseline temperament, and a null result built on that kind of self-report cannot cleanly rule out that early inhibition still matters, only that it stops being visible once the instrument asks the anxious mind to report on itself. Read that way, the two studies are not disagreeing about whether inhibition matters. They are demonstrating, empirically rather than by assertion, that whatever inhibition contributes to risk has by adolescence been metabolized into something else, an interpretive style, a self-focused attentional habit, a pattern of peer difficulty, that carries forward on its own and no longer announces itself as temperament when you ask the person carrying it.

The two datasets converge, unexpectedly, on timing. Chronis-Tuscano's cohort showed elevated lifetime but not current diagnosis at fourteen to sixteen, implying that for a meaningful share of stably inhibited children, whatever drove the risk had already crested and begun resolving by mid-adolescence. Miers and Westenberg's high-and-changing trajectory, tracked continuously rather than sampled at two points, shows almost exactly that shape from the inside, a rise through early adolescence, a peak, and then a decline and leveling from around eighteen onward. Neither study was designed to speak to the other, and neither cites the other, but laid side by side they sketch the same rough developmental arc, that whatever puts a child on a path toward clinically significant social anxiety tends to do its worst work in the first half of the second decade of life and start loosening its grip by the second half, a claim about timing that would be far more useful to prevention science than either study's headline finding about who, because it points toward when an intervention would need to land rather than merely whom it would need to find.

No study yet has done the one thing that would actually settle the disagreement, follow a single cohort of Fox-style, multi-informant, multi-timepoint inhibited toddlers all the way through a Westenberg-style continuous, growth-mixture-modeled adolescence, using the same children, the same informants, and the same statistical machinery at both ends rather than stitching two separately designed studies together after the fact. Until someone runs that design, the field is left holding two incompatible half-answers about the same suspected construct, one that finds a real but narrow-scope marker visible only to a caregiver watching from outside, and one that finds a genuinely heterogeneous, largely temperament-independent set of forces visible only once you ask what an adolescent's own mind is doing with an ambiguous social situation while it is happening. Whether those are two descriptions of a single causal chain observed from opposite ends, the inhibition an outside observer would have seen quietly converting, by thirteen, into the cognitive and social patterns an inside observer can now report, or whether they describe two genuinely different populations of children arriving at the same diagnostic label by unrelated roads, is the question this pairing leaves open, and it is not a small one, because a field that cannot say which of those two stories is true cannot yet say, with any confidence, when in a child's life the intervention that might change the story ought to arrive.
