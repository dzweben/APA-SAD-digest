The Social Brain Dispatch

The Defense That Cannot Tell Its Targets Apart

In 1989 two British clinical psychologists argued that submission is a targeted signal, sent to a specific rival to survive a live contest for rank. In 2007 a Dallas lab defeated mice with a real, individual aggressor for ten straight days and found that what nearly half of them carry away is not deference to that aggressor at all, but a wholesale retreat from every mouse they meet afterward, traceable to a single growth factor acting in a single nucleus of the midbrain.

Edition No. 11 · Evolutionary and Comparative or Animal Models · August 24, 2026

Something has to explain why a discipline built on human self-report and human neuroimaging keeps returning, decade after decade, to a mouse that has just lost a fight. The chronic social defeat paradigm, in one form or another, has become the default preclinical model not just for depression but increasingly for the social withdrawal seen in human anxiety disorders, and every drug that reaches a clinical trial for a mechanism discovered in that paradigm carries an unstated wager, that whatever a mouse does after being beaten by a bigger mouse for ten days running has something structurally in common with what happens to a graduate student who dreads presenting at lab meeting. The wager is not obviously a safe one. Nothing about a physical beating delivered by an identifiable, still-present aggressor resembles the anticipatory, evaluative, often entirely imagined threat that defines human social anxiety, where the disorder’s hallmark is fear of a judgment that has not happened yet, delivered by people who have done nothing more threatening than exist in the same room. And yet the field’s theoretical vocabulary for what social anxiety even is has, since at least the late 1980s, leaned just as heavily on animals, not laboratory mice but wild primates, and on a specific ethological claim, that submission, appeasement, and withdrawal in the face of a more dominant conspecific are ancient, conserved defensive strategies, and that social phobia is what happens when a human being deploys those strategies chronically and inappropriately. Two papers, eighteen years apart, put that comparative wager to very different kinds of test, one entirely theoretical and ethological, the other entirely molecular and behavioral, and reading them together exposes a gap in the comparative program that neither one, on its own, was positioned to see.

By 1989, Peter Trower had already built a career on a very different account of social phobia than the one he was about to help construct. Trower’s earlier work, done at the University of Birmingham through the 1970s and early 1980s, belonged to the social-skills-training tradition, the view, dominant in British clinical psychology at the time, that socially anxious people are anxious largely because they are, in some measurable sense, bad at the mechanics of interaction, poor at reading cues, timing turns, and modulating gaze, and that treatment is essentially remedial coaching. Paul Gilbert arrived at the collaboration from an entirely different direction. Through the 1980s Gilbert had been developing an explicitly evolutionary account of depression, arguing that depressive states are not simply mood gone wrong but an evolved, involuntary response to the perception of defeat and subordination, a psychological brake that stops a losing competitor from continuing to escalate a contest it cannot win. When Trower and Gilbert combined forces, the result was not a synthesis of two compatible views so much as Gilbert’s rank-based framework annexing and reinterpreting Trower’s territory. Social phobia, in their new account, had nothing essential to do with a deficit in social skill. It was, instead, the same defense system Gilbert had been describing for depression, an inherited capacity for detecting and responding to relative social rank, running chronically and maladaptively in people who possess perfectly adequate social skills but whose threat-appraisal machinery keeps reading the room as more hierarchically dangerous than it is. The paper functions, in retrospect, less as a pivot in either author’s career than as the moment Gilbert’s still-developing rank theory acquired its second disorder, an early proof that the framework could travel, and it previews almost exactly the trajectory Gilbert’s own work would take over the following three decades, from a theory of depression, to a theory of social anxiety, to the fully developed compassion-focused therapy he would spend the 2000s building around the same core claim, that shame and self-attack are ancient submission strategies turned against the self.

The theoretical machinery Trower and Gilbert imported to do this work came not from clinical psychology at all but from primate ethology, specifically from the British ethologist Michael Chance, who had spent decades studying how monkeys organize themselves into stable groups without constant violence. Chance’s central distinction, between what he called an agonic mode, a mode of relating organized around rank, threat, and the constant monitoring of who might attack whom, and a hedonic mode, organized around affiliation, play, and mutual reassurance, gave Trower and Gilbert a vocabulary for a claim that no purely cognitive account of social phobia then on offer had room to make. The dominant cognitive-behavioral models of the era, descending from Beck’s schema-based theory of anxiety, treated the socially phobic person’s fear as a content-neutral cognitive error, a catastrophic misreading of ordinary bodily sensations or ordinary social cues, with no particular explanation for why exposure and evaluation by other people, specifically, should be the trigger that so reliably produces this particular flavor of dread rather than some other. Trower and Gilbert’s answer was that there is nothing arbitrary about the content at all. In a social species organized by dominance hierarchy, being seen to fail, to blush, to freeze, to be exposed as weak in front of others carries a real and ancient cost, reduced access to mates, allies, and resources, and in the ancestral environment, sometimes direct physical retaliation from a dominant individual whose position that failure seemed to threaten. Natural selection, on this account, built a fast, involuntary, largely non-cognitive defense system whose entire job is to prevent an individual from behaving in ways that could be read as a challenge to a more dominant other, and to produce rapid, visible appeasement, lowered gaze, blushing, halting speech, physical withdrawal, the moment such a challenge seems to be underway or about to happen. Socially phobic individuals, in Trower and Gilbert’s reading, are people in whom this system is essentially over-tuned, stuck in the agonic mode across contexts that carry none of the ancestral stakes, appraising an ordinary seminar audience or a work colleague with the same rank-threat vigilance an animal would bring to an encounter with the group’s actual dominant male. The safety-seeking behaviors that a few years later the field would formally christen and build entire treatment protocols around, avoiding eye contact, rehearsing exits, monitoring one’s own performance for signs of weakness, are on this account not incidental coping strategies bolted onto an underlying cognitive error. They are the appeasement display itself, the same behavioral currency a subordinate primate spends to survive an encounter it cannot win, deployed by a nervous system that has lost the ability to tell a genuine rank contest from a graduate seminar.

It is worth being honest about what kind of paper this is and is not. Trower and Gilbert offer a theory, richly argued and integratively ambitious, but built almost entirely from clinical observation, ethological analogy, and a synthesis of existing evidence about submissive nonverbal behavior in socially anxious patients, rather than from any new data of their own. Nothing in the paper specifies a testable neural mechanism, a falsifiable prediction about which physiological system should carry the agonic signal, or any account of why some individuals end up chronically stuck in the agonic mode while most do not. The paper’s staying power, and it has stayed remarkably well, close to three hundred citations and a framework still actively invoked in social anxiety research thirty five years later, rests on the explanatory elegance of the rank-threat reframing rather than on any demonstration that the proposed mechanism is real. That is precisely the gap a very different kind of paper, published eighteen years later out of a laboratory with no interest in clinical psychology at all, would try to close from the opposite direction, not by theorizing about what an evolved defense system for social threat should look like, but by building an actual defeat, delivered to an actual animal by an actual, physically dominant aggressor, and then asking what, mechanistically, distinguishes the animals that carry the defeat forward from the animals that do not.

Eric Nestler did not come to social defeat looking for a model of social anxiety, or even, at first, of depression. By the time his laboratory, then at the University of Texas Southwestern Medical Center, took up the paradigm in the mid-2000s, Nestler had already spent nearly two decades building his reputation on the molecular biology of addiction, work that established proteins like ΔFosB as durable, cumulative molecular switches capable of encoding the kind of long-lasting behavioral change that repeated drug exposure produces in the brain’s reward circuitry. The turn toward chronic social defeat, an ethological paradigm with its own long lineage running back to resident-intruder aggression studies from the 1970s, represented a redeployment of that same toolkit, viral gene transfer, inducible knockouts, chromatin and electrophysiological profiling of the mesolimbic dopamine system, toward a different target, the molecular architecture of stress rather than of drugs. For Nestler, this 2007 paper is one substantial node inside an already mature program rather than its founding gesture, part of a research arc that would culminate years later in his becoming dean of the Icahn School of Medicine at Mount Sinai and one of the most cited living psychiatrists. For Vaishnav Krishnan, the paper’s first author, it was closer to the opposite, a physician-scientist still in training whose name this study effectively made, launching an independent research career built on stress neurobiology and neuroimmune mechanisms in mood disorders that he would go on to run in his own laboratory. What both scientists shared, whatever their respective positions in their own arcs, was a conviction that the field’s fixation on identifying disease mechanisms in already-defeated animals was asking the less interesting question. Most mice subjected to chronic social defeat do not, in fact, all end up the same. Some emerge from ten days of repeated physical loss looking almost exactly like animals who were never defeated at all, and the more consequential scientific target was not what makes an animal sick, but what makes roughly half of them, subjected to the identical assault, come out the other side essentially fine.

The protocol itself is by now one of the most widely replicated in behavioral neuroscience, but it is worth walking through exactly what it does and does not simulate. Male C57BL/6 mice, an inbred strain chosen precisely because its genetic uniformity means that any behavioral variation that emerges cannot be attributed to inherited differences between individuals, were placed for brief physical encounters, several minutes at a time, into the home cage of a much larger, more aggressive CD1 retired breeder mouse, an encounter that reliably ends in the C57 mouse being defeated and displaying clear subordinate posturing. After each physical bout the two mice were separated by a perforated divider that allowed continued sensory, but not physical, contact for the following twenty four hours, and the entire sequence, defeat, then extended sensory exposure to the aggressor, was repeated once daily for ten consecutive days, with a novel CD1 aggressor rotated in each day so that no C57 mouse ever had the chance to habituate to a single, specific opponent. That rotation matters enormously for how the resulting behavioral test should be read. On the day after the tenth defeat, each C57 mouse was placed in an open field containing a small wire mesh enclosure, first empty and then, in a second trial, containing a completely novel CD1 mouse the subject had never encountered before, one that had played no part in any of the preceding ten defeats. The ratio of time the subject spent near that enclosure with a stranger inside it, relative to time spent near the same empty enclosure, is the study’s central behavioral measure, and it produced a striking, reliable split. Close to half of the defeated mice showed a social interaction ratio near or above the level of never-defeated controls, approaching and investigating the novel mouse essentially normally, the population Krishnan and colleagues termed unsusceptible. The remainder showed a collapsed ratio, avoiding the interaction zone almost entirely, and it was this generalized avoidance of a total stranger, not any measure of continued deference toward a specific remembered aggressor, that the paper treats as its behavioral marker of susceptibility. Using a combination of gene expression profiling, protein assays, and in vivo electrophysiology targeted at the mesolimbic dopamine circuit running from the ventral tegmental area to the nucleus accumbens, the authors found that susceptible mice, and only susceptible mice, showed elevated levels of brain-derived neurotrophic factor protein, though notably not elevated messenger RNA, specifically within the ventral tegmental area, alongside increased firing rates in exactly the dopamine neurons of that region that project to the accumbens, where the arriving BDNF drove enhanced downstream receptor signaling. Selectively knocking down BDNF production within the ventral tegmental area itself, using a floxed BDNF mouse line paired with a Cre-expressing virus, roughly tripled the proportion of animals that emerged from the identical ten-day defeat protocol as unsusceptible, from eleven percent in control-virus animals to thirty four percent in animals carrying the local knockdown, a result specific enough in its anatomy that the same manipulation targeted at the accumbens itself, the circuit’s downstream terminal field rather than its origin, did not reproduce the effect. Resilience, on this evidence, is not the absence of a stress response. It is an actively maintained state, requiring the brain’s reward circuitry to keep a specific growth factor pathway in the ventral tegmental area from running the way it runs in animals that go on to avoid every mouse they subsequently meet.

Set beside each other, these two accounts converge on a claim substantial enough to be worth taking seriously as a general principle about social defense systems, not merely a coincidence of two unrelated literatures. Both insist that social withdrawal is not a unitary trait scattered continuously across a population, present in some animals and absent in others as a matter of degree, but the discrete output of a system that is either recruited or not, a switch rather than a dial. Gilbert and Trower frame that switch as a difference in appraisal, whether the agonic, rank-threat mode of relating to others gets triggered and stays triggered. Krishnan and colleagues locate an analogous switch not in appraisal at all but in a specific plasticity mechanism inside a reward circuit, evidence that whatever decides whether an individual, mouse or presumably human, comes out the other side of a social stressor still willing to approach new individuals is a property of neural circuitry that can be identified, manipulated, and in this case, roughly tripled in its resilient output with a single localized molecular intervention. But set the papers beside each other honestly, rather than charitably, and the convergence gives way to a real problem for Gilbert and Trower’s account specifically. Their entire theory is built on a dyadic logic, appeasement as a targeted communicative signal, sent by a subordinate to a specific dominant, functioning to defuse a specific, ongoing status contest between two known individuals within a stable group. Nothing about that logic predicts, or even comfortably accommodates, what the mouse experiment actually measures, which is not deference toward the actual, remembered aggressor at all, but a wholesale collapse in approach behavior toward a completely novel individual who was never part of the original contest and against whom no rank relationship of any kind yet exists. A defense system built, as Gilbert and Trower describe it, to negotiate a specific hierarchical relationship ought to leave an animal free to engage normally with third parties outside that relationship. What the defeat model instead exposes is generalization so complete that the identity of the other individual stops mattering at all, which looks considerably less like calibrated rank-tracking and rather more like a blunt-force reward and motivation deficit, an anhedonic withdrawal from social contact as a category, hijacking the same circuitry that would ordinarily let a defeated animal distinguish a genuine continuing threat from a stranger who has done it no harm. If that is the right description, the ventral tegmental area’s BDNF pathway may be less a mechanism for social rank appraisal specifically than a much more general stress-resilience switch that happens, in this particular paradigm, to express itself through a social behavior because the stressor administered happened to be social. The blind spot runs the other direction too. Nothing about Krishnan and colleagues’ design, or the dozens of studies that have since used their paradigm, tests for anticipatory, purely evaluative threat of the kind that defines the human disorder, fear that arrives before any interaction, any contest, any defeat has actually occurred. Their mice are only ever avoidant after a real, physical fight they unambiguously lost. Human social anxiety routinely peaks in people who have never lost anything, who arrive at the seminar room already dreading a verdict nobody has yet rendered. A model built entirely on consequence has essentially nothing to say about a disorder whose defining feature is anticipation.

What neither paper, and no study built directly on either one’s logic, has yet managed to ask is the question that would actually settle whether this entire comparative program deserves the weight the field keeps placing on it. Is the resilience Krishnan and colleagues located in the ventral tegmental area a mechanism for regulating social threat specifically, the same kind of system Gilbert and Trower’s ethology would predict, or is it a domain-general buffer against any repeated, uncontrollable aversive experience, one that would show the identical molecular signature if the ten days of stress involved no other mouse at all, no rank, no rival, nothing recognizably social in it whatsoever. Nobody has run that control, a chronic, physically comparable, non-social stressor administered on the same schedule, scored against the identical BDNF and firing-rate measures in the identical circuit. Until someone does, the field’s most detailed molecular story about social defeat remains agnostic on the one question a genuinely comparative, evolutionarily grounded account of social anxiety most needs answered, whether there is anything specifically social left to explain once the stress is stripped out of the paradigm, or whether decades of research have been elaborating, with extraordinary technical precision, a mechanism that was never really about being social at all.
