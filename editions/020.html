The Social Brain Dispatch

The Self That Weighs Too Little

In 2017 a team at Queen Square wrote down an equation for self-esteem and found it behaves like a running tally of recent social surprise, with the vulnerable among us wiring that surprise from the insula straight into the brain’s self-value signal. Four years later the same building turned the equation on social anxiety and found that the fearful hold a thinner picture of themselves, carrying too little good evidence into the room to absorb a single cold verdict, and that finding quietly rewrites what a learning rate is.

Edition No. 20 · Neural Methods and Computational Accounts · November 19, 2026

Every cognitive model of social anxiety this Dispatch has read so far rests on a noun that nobody has ever measured directly. Clark and Wells called it the self as a social object, Rapee and Heimberg called it the mental representation of the self as seen by the audience, and Beck, before both, called it the schema. In the fifteenth edition we watched those models draw closed loops and filing cabinets around this thing, and in the sixteenth we watched Philippe Goldin and his colleagues hand patients their own worst beliefs about themselves inside a scanner and ask them to argue back. In every case the self-belief sat at the center of the theory as a kind of prime mover, the thing that biases attention, colors memory, and keeps the fear alive after every party ends without catastrophe. And in every case it was inferred from questionnaires or from the downstream biases it supposedly produced. Nobody could say how much a self-belief weighs, how it changes when evidence arrives, or why the same piece of disapproval moves one person a great deal and another hardly at all. A noun that cannot be quantified cannot be targeted, and a theory whose central term cannot be targeted is a theory a clinician must take on faith.

Computational psychiatry promised to change that, and the promise had a specific shape. If a belief is something an agent holds with a degree of confidence and revises in light of evidence, then it can be written down as a probability distribution, and the revisions can be written down as an update rule, and the parameters of that rule can be estimated from what people do and say on a carefully designed task. The schema stops being a metaphor and becomes a prior. This edition reads two papers that tried to make good on that promise for the social self, both produced within a few hundred meters of each other at University College London, sharing an author, and separated by four years and one decisive change in the kind of model they were willing to believe. The first, Geert-Jan Will, Robb Rutledge, Michael Moutoussis, and Raymond Dolan’s 2017 paper in eLife, asked what self-esteem is computationally and where it lives in the brain. The second, Alexandra Hopkins, Ray Dolan, Katherine Button, and Michael Moutoussis’s 2021 paper in Computational Psychiatry, asked why people who fear negative evaluation learn so readily from it. Read together, they suggest that the field has been asking the wrong question about the socially anxious self-image. The question has been whether it is negative. The better question may be how much it weighs.

To understand why these papers came from where they came from, one has to understand what happened at 12 Queen Square over roughly two decades. Ray Dolan, an Irish psychiatrist who came to London in the era when positron emission tomography was turning the study of emotion into a laboratory science, became one of the founding figures of the Functional Imaging Laboratory and eventually director of the Wellcome Trust Centre for Neuroimaging. His own early work is part of the foundational literature this Dispatch keeps circling, including the PET and fMRI studies with John Morris and others in the 1990s that showed the human amygdala responding to fearful faces even when they were masked from awareness. But the more consequential thing Dolan did was institutional and intellectual at once. Through the 2000s his group fused the reinforcement learning formalism imported from machine learning and primate electrophysiology, prediction errors, learning rates, model-based and model-free control, with human imaging, so that by the time Nathaniel Daw, Samuel Gershman, Ben Seymour, Peter Dayan, and Dolan published their 2011 paper on model-based influences on striatal prediction errors, the Queen Square style had a recognizable signature. One writes down an explicit generative model of behavior, fits it to choices, derives latent quantities trial by trial, and asks which brain regions track them. In 2014 Dolan co-founded, with the Max Planck Society, the Max Planck UCL Centre for Computational Psychiatry and Ageing Research, whose name announced the ambition to carry that style out of the reward laboratory and into the clinic.

Michael Moutoussis is the connective tissue between the two papers and, in some ways, the more interesting intellectual figure for this edition’s purposes. A psychiatrist by training, he came to computational work through persecutory delusions, writing with Richard Bentall, Wael El-Deredy, and Peter Dayan some of the earliest Bayesian accounts of why people with paranoia jump to conclusions on probabilistic reasoning tasks. That background matters because paranoia and social anxiety share a phenomenology that psychiatric nosology keeps apart. Both are beliefs about what other people think of me, both are maintained against disconfirming evidence, and both seem to involve a self that is unusually exposed to the imagined gaze of others. Moutoussis had spent years thinking of such beliefs as priors whose strength determines how readily evidence moves them. He also became, through the Neuroscience in Psychiatry Network, a Wellcome Trust strategic award spanning Cambridge and UCL, a central figure in an adolescent cohort study designed to ask how such computational traits develop. His fingerprints on both papers are the fingerprints of a clinician who wanted computational models to say something a therapist could use.

Geert-Jan Will arrived at Queen Square from a very different lineage. He trained at Leiden University in the developmental social neuroscience tradition built by Eveline Crone and Berna Güroğlu, where his doctoral work asked how chronic peer rejection in childhood shapes neural responses to social exclusion in adolescence, using Cyberball and related paradigms in a longitudinal cohort that had been followed since elementary school. That is the tradition this Dispatch met in its fourth and seventeenth editions, the tradition that treats rejection as the central event of adolescent social life and asks what it does to a developing brain. Will brought that preoccupation with being evaluated to a laboratory whose native language was the prediction error, and the 2017 paper is the result of that collision. Robb Rutledge, the fourth author, supplied a specific template. In 2014 Rutledge, working with Dolan, had published an equation for momentary happiness, showing in the laboratory and in a smartphone sample of eighteen thousand people that how happy people feel from moment to moment is well described by an exponentially decaying sum of recent expected rewards and recent reward prediction errors. Will’s question was whether self-esteem obeys a similar law, and the answer turned out to be yes with one revealing exception.

The design is elegant and, like most social evaluation paradigms, rests on a deception. Forty-four healthy right-handed adults were recruited from UCL participant pools and asked to upload a personal profile to what they were told was an online database, then to return a week later to learn what strangers had made of them. Four were excluded after eye tracking showed they had fallen asleep in the scanner, leaving forty participants, mean age 23.3 years, fourteen of them male, with no history of psychiatric or neurological disorder. In the scanner they saw, on each trial, the name of one of 184 ostensible raters together with a colored cue signaling which of four groups the rater belonged to. The groups were described as differing in how generous they had been toward all profiles, and participants learned their rank ordering in advance, though never the actual numbers. In truth the computer delivered approval on 85, 70, 30, and 15 percent of trials for the four groups. On each trial the participant predicted whether the rater liked or disliked them, waited six seconds, and then saw a thumbs up or a thumbs down, with 24 additional trials showing no feedback at all. Every two or three trials, 78 times in all, they rated how good they felt about themselves at that moment on a visual analogue scale, after explicit instruction distinguishing state self-esteem from mood and from self-concept.

The behavioral facts came first and were clean. Predictions tracked the groups closely, with a main effect of group on predicted approval of F(3,117) = 209.47 and a partial eta squared of .84, participants predicting approval on about 95, 87, 30, and 13 percent of trials. A Rescorla-Wagner model fit to these choices explained a respectable share of variance, mean r squared of .40. In this model each group carries an expected social value that is nudged after every outcome by a social prediction error, the difference between the feedback received and the feedback expected, scaled by a learning rate. A response bias parameter allowed people to predict approval even from groups whose expected value had turned negative, what the authors nicely call the extra credit people give themselves. The crucial test of the prediction error hypothesis was simple. If self-esteem responds to surprise rather than to valence alone, trial-by-trial changes in self-esteem should rise with approval but also fall with how much approval was expected. After removing the positive effect of feedback valence, r = .18, there remained a small but reliable negative relation with expectation, r = −.06. Approval from the stingy 15 percent group lifted self-esteem the most, and disapproval from the generous 85 percent group cut it the deepest. Self-esteem, in other words, behaved like a learning signal.

The formal model made this precise. In the winning model, momentary self-esteem equals a baseline plus a weight on an exponentially discounted sum of past social prediction errors, with a forgetting factor that determines how quickly old surprises fade. Fitted per participant by maximum likelihood to both choices and self-esteem ratings, it captured individual self-esteem trajectories with a mean r squared of .32 and beat its competitors on summed BIC. It beat a model without the response bias by 255 BIC units, a model in which people started with correct beliefs about each group and never updated by 1,042, and, most tellingly, a model in which only the valence of feedback mattered, with no expectations, by 90 units when both were fit to self-esteem ratings alone. The fitted parameters told a story of two timescales. The average learning rate for updating expectations about the groups was tiny, .04, so beliefs about how approving the social world is accumulated slowly and resisted change. The average forgetting factor for self-esteem was .66, which means that momentary self-worth depended on roughly the last six appraisals. People were sluggish about revising what they expected from others and volatile about what those others’ surprises did to their sense of themselves.

The exception to Rutledge’s happiness law is where the paper becomes theoretically interesting. In the mood equation, expectations have their own term, so that merely entering a situation where good things are likely makes people happier before anything happens. Will and colleagues tested exactly this, and a model with a separate expectation term lost to the simpler model by 131 BIC units. Being among generous raters did not raise self-esteem. Only being surprised by them did. The authors read this as a refinement of Mark Leary’s sociometer theory, the proposal from 1995 that self-esteem is an internal gauge of one’s inclusionary status. On their data the gauge reads the recent derivative of acceptance, the local gradient of one’s social standing, and is indifferent to its level. That is a substantive claim, and it carries an implication that matters enormously for social anxiety. If self-esteem is driven by prediction errors, then accurate expectations are protective. A person who expects rejection from a cold audience is not wounded by it, and a person whose expectations are miscalibrated is wounded constantly.

The neural results map this computational anatomy onto familiar valuation circuitry. With trial-wise social prediction errors entered as a parametric modulator at feedback onset, the team found a cluster spanning bilateral ventral striatum and subgenual anterior cingulate, Brodmann area 25, with peaks at MNI −8, 21, −5 and 5, 20, −8, family-wise error corrected at the cluster level. Model-derived updates in self-esteem at feedback tracked a cluster in ventromedial prefrontal cortex peaking in left medial orbitofrontal area 14m at −6, 33, −15 and extending into pregenual cingulate, area 32pl, at a corrected p of .047. The authors place this squarely in the line of work, from Antonio Rangel, Matthew Rushworth, Joseph Kable and others, establishing area 14m and its neighbors as a common currency for subjective value, and they draw the conclusion with real nerve. Self-evaluation, they suggest, may be reducible to valuation, the same computation the brain performs on juice and money and faces, with the self now as its object.

The clinical link came through a canonical correlation analysis, and it is the part of the paper a methodologically trained reader should handle with the most care. The team entered nine computational parameters on one side and eleven questionnaire measures on the other, covering trait and state self-esteem, self-perception, narcissism, state and trait anxiety, the Liebowitz Social Anxiety Scale, rejection sensitivity, the Brief Fear of Negative Evaluation scale, and two depression measures. One canonical dimension survived, Wilks’s lambda of .01, F(99, 152.5) = 1.40, p = .029, with a canonical correlation of .87. They labeled it interpersonal vulnerability. People high on it reported lower self-esteem and more depression, social anxiety, and trait anxiety, and on the computational side they carried a lower baseline self-esteem, lower average initial expectations of approval, a wider spread between their expectations for the best and worst groups, and, critically, a larger weight on social prediction errors. Their self-esteem swung harder in response to each surprise. Using each participant’s score on this dimension as a between-subjects regressor, the team found that vulnerability scaled prediction error responses in a left anterior insula cluster extending into inferior frontal gyrus, peaking at −44, 11, 9, and a psychophysiological interaction seeded there showed vulnerability scaling insula coupling with a right medial orbitofrontal cluster, peak 11, 32, −11, during self-esteem updates. Vulnerability did not modulate the striatal prediction error response or the ventromedial update response themselves. The authors note that the insula peak overlaps the region Goldin and colleagues reported in socially anxious patients reappraising negative self-beliefs, the paper this Dispatch read in its first edition, and the insula findings Amit Etkin and Tor Wager pooled across anxiety disorders in the same edition.

That is a striking convergence, and it deserves to be stated plainly before it is interrogated. On this account vulnerable people compute the same prediction errors in the same striatal circuitry as everyone else, and they update a self-value signal in the same ventromedial cortex as everyone else. What differs is a third region, the anterior insula, which responds more strongly to their prediction errors and couples more strongly to the self-value signal while it updates. The insula, on this reading, sets the gain on how much each social surprise is allowed to move the self.

Now the interrogation. A canonical correlation of .87 between nine parameters and eleven questionnaires in forty people is close to what one would expect from noise alone, since the analysis finds the linear combinations on each side that maximize their correlation, and with twenty variables and forty observations it has enormous freedom to do so. The significance test addresses that inflation in principle, but p = .029 on a test with ninety-nine numerator degrees of freedom is a fragile thing, and the specific weights that defined the dimension, which variables loaded and in what direction, would almost certainly shift in a new sample. The neural individual differences analyses then used those very scores as their regressor, so any instability in the dimension propagates directly into the insula result. The cluster-forming threshold of p < .005 is permissive by the standards Anders Eklund and colleagues established for cluster inference in 2016, and the authors’ supplementary permutation test, while reassuring about the cluster-extent threshold, does not fully address that concern. The PPI cluster is reported at 78,570 voxels, which at that size is less a localized orbitofrontal finding than a brain-wide shift in insula coupling, whatever its peak. The sample was healthy and young, and its mean Liebowitz score sits far below any clinical range. And the computational model was fit by maximum likelihood with flat priors, participant by participant, an approach whose parameter estimates in tasks of this length are notoriously noisy. None of this makes the paper wrong. It makes the clinical half of it a hypothesis, and a very good one, still awaiting its demonstration.

The second paper begins not in a scanner but in Bristol, and with a researcher whose name carries a peculiar weight in any discussion of statistical fragility. Katherine Button trained in the Bristol group of Marcus Munafò and the psychiatric epidemiologist Glyn Lewis, and in 2013 she was first author on what became one of the most cited methodological papers in modern neuroscience, the Nature Reviews Neuroscience analysis showing that the median statistical power of neuroscience studies was around twenty percent and that small samples inflate effect sizes while lowering the probability that a significant finding is true. The paper was a turning point in the reproducibility movement, and Button has since built her career at the University of Bath on both social anxiety and research practice. Her substantive contribution here is the task. In a 2012 study with Michael Browning, Munafò, and Lewis, and then in a 2015 PLOS ONE paper with Dimitra Kounali, Lexine Stapinski, Ron Rapee, Lewis, and Munafò, she developed the Social Evaluation Learning Task, in which participants learn by trial and error which words a computer persona would use to describe them or to describe an unknown other. The headline result from 2015 was that people high in fear of negative evaluation chose fewer positive words when predicting how they themselves would be described, and showed no such bias when predicting descriptions of a stranger.

Alexandra Hopkins, a doctoral fellow in the Max Planck UCL program, inherited that dataset and a question about what model could explain it. The question had been sharpened by a 2017 paper in Emotion by Leonie Koban, Joanna Arch, and colleagues at Colorado, a precursor of the self-perception learning work this Dispatch read in its second edition, which had fit Rescorla-Wagner models to a related task and found that patients with social anxiety disorder showed larger learning rates for negative feedback about themselves. That is an important finding, and Hopkins and Moutoussis had a precise objection to what it explains. A learning rate is an unstable psychological property. Work by Michael Browning, Timothy Behrens, and Sonia Bishop, among others, had shown that learning rates shift with the volatility of the environment and the structure of the task, and in a Bayesian framework a learning rate is a derived quantity, the consequence of how much confidence one already has in a belief. Telling a patient that their negative learning rate is high is like telling them their temperature is high. It names the symptom of the computation without naming its cause. And it says nothing about the explicit beliefs patients bring to therapy, the beliefs that clinicians spend sessions trying to shift.

The data come from Button’s 2015 sample of one hundred participants recruited with a deliberately efficient design, a third drawn from the bottom quartile of Brief Fear of Negative Evaluation scores, a third from the top quartile, and a third from the middle, with mid-range scorers randomly thinned to oversample the informative extremes. Clinical status was assessed with the Clinical Interview Schedule-Revised, a lay-administered structured interview mapping onto ICD-10 and DSM-IV, and seven participants met criteria for social phobia, while 62 exceeded the clinical cutoff on the Brief Fear of Negative Evaluation scale. The task had six blocks of 32 trials. In each block a persona was evaluating either the participant or an unknown other, and on each trial the participant chose between a positive and a negative word, with feedback revealing whether the persona would have used it. Three persona types were defined by the probability that the positive word was correct, .8 for the liking persona, .5 for the neutral, .2 for the disliking. This gives a two by three design, self versus other crossed with like, neutral, and dislike, and the 2015 result lived in its interaction.

Hopkins built two families of models. The associative family extended Rescorla-Wagner with separate learning rates by valence and by target, a positivity bias that boosted the value of choosing positive words regardless of learning, an initial bias parameter, and a softmax with decision noise. The belief family is where the paper’s originality lies. Each participant is modeled as holding, for each persona, a beta distribution over the probability of approval, parameterized by an amount of positive evidence alpha and negative evidence beta, whose mean alpha over alpha plus beta is the expected approval rate. Feedback adds one unit of evidence to the appropriate side. The innovation is that each participant’s evidence has two components. One is a state component that accumulates task feedback and decays with a memory parameter, so that older evidence fades. The other is a trait component, a fixed quantity of positive and negative evidence that the participant brings to bear the moment the context switches to an evaluation of the self or of another, independent of anything the task delivers. This trait evidence is the formal stand-in for the activated self-schema. The authors are candid in a footnote that it represents notional evidence, a numerical summary of whatever memories, images, and convictions a person summons when they walk into the room.

The fitting procedure is the second methodological contribution, and it is a response to a problem Moutoussis and Hopkins had written about in 2018. Hierarchical Bayesian fitting improves parameter estimates by shrinking each individual toward a group distribution, but if that group distribution ignores a variable one later wants to correlate with the parameters, the shrinkage pulls everyone toward a common mean and can wash out precisely the individual differences one hoped to find. Their solution, which they call clinically informed model fitting, is to let the mean of the group prior for each parameter depend linearly on the symptom score, so that the slope relating symptom to parameter is estimated inside the model with a full posterior. The models were fit in Stan with four chains, convergence assessed by the Gelman-Rubin statistic, and compared by approximate leave-one-out cross-validation.

The model comparison came out more evenly than the paper’s title might suggest. Among associative models, those with separate learning rates for self and other clearly won, and adding the initial and positivity biases produced large improvements. The full self-other valence model, with four learning rates, had a LOO of −9858, barely better than the asymmetric version with one self-positive, one self-negative, and one shared other learning rate at −9862, and on grounds of parameter recoverability the authors carried the simpler one forward. Among belief models, separate trait evidence for self and other was essential, and the best version reached −9762, better than any associative model, with a slightly simpler variant at −9768 carried forward. The authors do not overclaim this advantage. When they computed each participant’s log-likelihood difference between the best associative and best belief models, most people showed no clear preference, while about a fifth showed conventionally strong evidence for one or the other. Some people, it seems, learn about their own social standing like a model-free agent stamping in action values, and others like a model-based agent revising a belief, and fear of negative evaluation did not predict which.

The symptom results are compact and decisive. In the associative model, the only parameter whose slope on fear of negative evaluation excluded zero was the self-negative learning rate, slope .11 with a 95 percent credible interval of .02 to .20. Learning rates for positive feedback about the self, slope .01, and for feedback about the other, slope −.05, were untouched. This replicates Koban and colleagues in a different task and a different sample. In the belief model, the only credible slope was on the trait positive evidence about the self, −.47 with an interval of −.87 to −.06. The more someone feared negative evaluation, the less positive evidence about themselves they brought into an evaluative encounter. Trait negative evidence about the self also trended downward, slope −.24, but its interval, −1.55 to 1.08, was so wide as to be uninformative. Nothing about the other moved with fear of negative evaluation at all.

Then the bridge between the two families. Using maximum likelihood estimates, trait positive self-evidence correlated with the self-negative learning rate at Spearman rho of −.49. Using the hierarchical posterior estimates the correlation rose to −.85, and the self-negative learning rate correlated at −.78 with the mean of the activated self-belief. The authors take this as reconciliation of the cognitivist and behaviorist perspectives, and the logic is worth seeing. In a beta-distribution model the impact of one new piece of evidence on the mean belief is inversely proportional to the total evidence already held. A person who walks in with little positive self-evidence has a light prior, so every cold verdict moves their belief further, and when one forces the same behavior through a Rescorla-Wagner lens it appears as a large learning rate for negative outcomes. The learning rate, on this account, is the shadow cast by a thin self-schema.

The models also earned their keep generatively. Simulating one hundred synthetic experiments of one thousand participants each, the team reran the original 2015 mixed-effects analyses on the simulated data. Both models reproduced the main effects of fear of negative evaluation, of self versus other, and of persona in essentially every simulated sample. The associative model reproduced the crucial three-way interaction between fear of negative evaluation, persona, and target in more than 95 percent of samples, and the belief model in 89 percent, so the more psychologically interpretable model was slightly worse at regenerating the very interaction that motivated it. That is the kind of candor one wishes were universal.

The critical points are real, and some are acknowledged. The sample is largely subclinical, only seven of one hundred meeting diagnostic criteria, so the paper is about a dimension of fear rather than a disorder. Several parameters of interest, including the initial bias in the belief model and the positivity bias in the associative one, recovered poorly in simulation, which the authors honestly concede renders individual-level estimates unreliable even where group-level slopes are credible. A more subtle concern goes unremarked. The jump in the alpha-to-learning-rate correlation from −.49 under maximum likelihood to −.85 under hierarchical fitting should give pause, because both parameters were estimated under priors whose means were themselves functions of the same symptom score. Clinically informed fitting pulls each person’s alpha and each person’s learning rate toward values predicted by their fear of negative evaluation, and two quantities pulled toward the same covariate will correlate more strongly for that reason alone. The maximum likelihood estimate of −.49 is the more honest number. Finally, the beta model’s trait evidence is fixed for the whole task, so it can describe a person who arrives with a thin self-schema but cannot describe a schema that thins over the course of an encounter, which is exactly what the safety-behavior and post-event processing accounts claim happens in real social life. There is a certain irony, which Button would be the first to recognize, in a paper built from a 2013 critique of underpowered neuroscience resting its central individual-differences claim on parameters that its own simulations show are hard to recover. That irony does not invalidate the group-level slope. It does mark where the next study has to be stronger.

Set side by side, the two papers converge on something the cognitive models of the 1990s could only gesture toward. In both, the signature of social vulnerability lives in expectations about the self brought to the encounter, and in neither does it live in how the world is learned about in general. Will and colleagues found that vulnerable people carried lower initial expectations of approval and a larger weight on social prediction errors, so their self-esteem swung harder with each surprise, while their slow learning about the raters themselves looked like everyone else’s. Hopkins and colleagues found that the fearful brought less positive evidence about themselves to the room, while their learning about the stranger was intact. The self-other dissociation runs through both, and it is the computational echo of what Button found descriptively in 2015 and what Clark and Wells predicted from the clinic in 1995. Socially anxious people are pessimistic, or more precisely underpowered, learners about one specific object, and ordinary learners about everything else.

The convergence goes further than either paper says. Hopkins and colleagues note in their discussion that the fearful showed lower positive self-evidence and also a trend toward lower negative self-evidence, which together would mean less total self-evidence and therefore more uncertain self-beliefs, and they point to Will’s paper as the place where that uncertainty would show up, as unstable self-esteem. Read through the beta model, Will’s larger weight on social prediction errors in vulnerable people is what a thin self-prior would produce at the level of feeling. Read through Will’s neural data, Hopkins’s thin prior has a candidate implementation. If ventromedial area 14m holds the current value of the self and the anterior insula modulates how strongly each prediction error is allowed to update it, then the weight of the trait evidence a person brings to the encounter might be expressed as the inverse of insula-to-ventromedial gain. A heavy self-schema would be a self-value signal the insula cannot easily push around, and a light one a signal that every surprise can move. That is a bold synthesis, which neither paper tests. But it is the first account this Dispatch has encountered that connects the schema of cognitive therapy, the learning rate of reinforcement learning, and a specific circuit through quantities that could, in principle, all be measured in the same person on the same afternoon.

The tension between them is sharper than their shared building suggests, and it concerns the role of pessimistic expectations. In Will’s prediction error model, expecting rejection is protective. If you think a rater will disapprove, their thumbs down generates little surprise and does little damage, and the authors make this explicit when they write that accurate expectations about rejection buffer against it. Yet in their data vulnerable people expected less approval and were hurt more. In Hopkins’s belief model, a low expectation of approval does no protective work at all. A thin self-prior absorbs disapproval readily because there is little evidence on the positive side to resist it. These are two different theories of what a gloomy expectation does. In the first it is a shield whose failure must then be explained by a separate gain parameter. In the second it is a weakness in itself. Will’s model reconciles its own finding only by adding the weight on prediction errors as an independent quantity, while Hopkins’s model derives the heightened sensitivity directly from the thinness of the prior. On grounds of parsimony the belief account is more attractive. On grounds of neural evidence only Will’s model has any.

Each paper also reveals the other’s blind spot with unusual clarity. Will’s team measured the thing patients actually suffer, the moment-to-moment feeling of self-worth, and tied it to the brain, but in forty healthy young adults, with a symptom link built on a fragile multivariate analysis and a learning model fit without the hierarchical machinery that might have made its individual parameters trustworthy. Hopkins’s team sampled across the full range of fear of negative evaluation, used the more principled fitting method, and posed the more theoretically incisive model comparison, but measured only word choices, never feelings and never the brain, and its trait evidence is static by construction. Neither design can see what the other sees. A study that ran Hopkins’s belief model on Will’s self-esteem ratings in a clinical sample in the scanner would test the synthesis outright, and the remarkable thing, given that these two teams shared an author and a building, is that as far as this Dispatch can tell nobody has yet published it.

There is also a problem that the thin-prior account has not yet faced, and it is where this edition has to end. If the socially anxious self-schema is light, carrying little total evidence, then it should be moved easily by good news as well as bad. A thin prior is symmetric in its fragility. Yet Hopkins found no relation between fear of negative evaluation and learning from positive feedback about the self, slope .01, and the trait negative evidence parameter was too poorly estimated to show the reduction that symmetric thinness would require. Johanna Jarcho and colleagues’ adolescent work, read in the seventeenth edition, went further and found socially anxious teenagers whose striatum failed to update on a peer’s unexpected kindness and whose memory then discarded it. And Koban and colleagues found the same asymmetry in patients. The data, in other words, describe a self that is thin on the side of good evidence and normally weighted, or even resistant, on the side of bad. The single beta distribution with its two evidence counts cannot easily produce that pattern without adding the very kind of asymmetric learning parameter the belief framework was meant to explain away. So the question that matters most is this. Is the socially anxious self-schema a light prior that happens to be centered low, in which case the therapeutic target is to add weight, letting good experiences accumulate until each cold verdict moves the self less, or is it a prior that actively discounts positive evidence while admitting negative evidence at full value, in which case adding good experiences will do little until the discounting itself is broken? Those two answers imply different therapies, different neural signatures in the insula and ventromedial cortex, and different predictions for what exposure does across sessions, and nothing in the record yet distinguishes them.
