The Social Brain Dispatch

The Wrong Emotion, the Wrong Region

A 2002 scan of fifteen patients found that the amygdala in generalized social phobia does not fire hardest at fear, it fires hardest at contempt. A 2012 scan of fifty five patients and controls found that by the time anyone went looking for that same amygdala again, the more reliable signal, and the more interesting failure, had already moved to the insula, and to a wire between the insula and the cortex that no longer carried its normal current.

Edition No. 10 · Neural Circuits and Mechanisms · August 20, 2026

Two editions of this publication have already asked what is wrong with the amygdala in generalized social anxiety disorder, and both left a question sitting untouched at the center of the answer. Edition 1 established that the amygdala fires on time in both anxious and healthy brains alike, and that the actual pathology lives downstream, in a prefrontal regulatory response that arrives too late to catch the wave it is supposed to dampen. Edition 3 traced that same amygdala claim back to its origin in two small, paradigm-defining studies from Tübingen and Uppsala, one built around passively viewed faces and one around an actively performed public speech, and showed that nearly every subsequent imaging study in the field, including the two this edition now takes up, descends from one of those two ancestral designs. Neither edition asked the more basic question sitting underneath both of them. When researchers say the amygdala hyperreacts to threat in this disorder, what, specifically, counts as the threat. A snake and a stranger’s contemptuous glance are both capable of eliciting fear, but they are not the same kind of danger, and a threat-detection system built by natural selection to flag predators has no obvious reason to also be the system that flags a raised eyebrow across a seminar table. If the amygdala’s hyperreactivity in social anxiety is simply an instance of a general, phylogenetically old fear module running hot, that has one set of implications for treatment, closer to the extinction-based logic that works reasonably well for snake and spider phobias. If instead it is calibrated specifically to signals of social rejection and evaluative judgment, emotions like contempt and anger rather than fear itself, that is a different claim entirely, one that locates the disorder’s core vulnerability in a social-cognitive system for reading other people’s verdicts rather than in a general danger-detection apparatus that merely happens to include people among its inputs. A second, equally consequential question sits alongside the first. Even granting that some region reliably overreacts to socially evaluative faces, is a magnitude difference, more activation here than there, actually the right unit of explanation, or is the field’s entire vocabulary of hyperreactivity a category error that a later generation of connectivity methods was built to correct. Two papers, a decade apart, tried to answer these questions with methods sharp enough to actually distinguish the possibilities, and what they found does not so much resolve the field’s founding claim as relocate it, first onto a specific emotion nobody had previously isolated, and then onto a specific wire between two regions that the field’s original magnitude-only vocabulary had no way to see at all.

By 2002, Murray Stein had already spent a decade making his name on social phobia before he ever put anyone in a scanner. His earlier work at the University of California San Diego, much of it with Kathleen Chartier, had been epidemiological and genetic, establishing through family and twin studies that social phobia clusters in families in ways that implicate real heritable liability rather than mere modeled anxiousness passed down through observation and parenting style. That background matters for reading the 2002 paper correctly, because it was written by someone who had already spent years thinking about social phobia as a population-level phenomenon with a demonstrable biological substrate, arriving at neuroimaging not as a methods specialist looking for a clinical population to apply a technique to, but as a clinical epidemiologist looking for the mechanism that could explain a heritability estimate he had already established. Stein would go on to become one of the most prolific psychiatric researchers of his generation, eventually directing UCSD’s Anxiety and Traumatic Stress Disorders Program and, later still, pivoting the bulk of his research effort toward large-scale genomics and epidemiology, co-leading the Army STARRS consortium’s search for the genetic and epidemiological architecture of suicide risk and PTSD across hundreds of thousands of soldiers. Read against that trajectory, this 2002 paper is not a late-career capstone applying a mature theoretical framework. It is an early, field-founding foray into neuroimaging by a researcher whose lasting scientific identity would ultimately be built on a much larger canvas, and the paper’s central methodological choice reflects exactly the kind of specificity question an epidemiologist trained to distrust unspecified category labels would think to ask.

That choice was to stop treating threatening faces as an undifferentiated category. Stein, Goldin, Sareen, Zorrilla, and Brown recruited fifteen patients meeting modified SCID-IV criteria for generalized social phobia, ten men and five women with a mean age of thirty nine, and fifteen sex-matched healthy comparison subjects of nearly identical age, and showed each of them sixty photographs spanning five distinct expression types, angry, contemptuous, fearful, happy, and neutral, drawn from a standardized set of Japanese and Caucasian facial expressions. The task itself was incidental to emotion entirely: participants simply judged the sex of each face, a gender-discrimination button press that gave the researchers no reason to expect any conscious, deliberate processing of emotional content at all, and gave them a clean behavioral check on whether any observed brain differences were riding on top of differences in task performance. They were not. Groups did not differ in gender-discrimination accuracy, ninety seven percent correct in both, nor in their ability to correctly label the expressions when asked afterward, seventy seven percent in the patient group against seventy nine percent in controls, a difference nowhere near significance. Whatever the scanner found, it was not a difference in how well these two groups could see or interpret the faces in front of them. It was a difference in what their brains did with those faces automatically, beneath the level the gender-discrimination task was even asking about.

What the scanner found was not evenly distributed across the five expression types. When the researchers contrasted the harsher expressions against happy faces, generalized social phobia patients showed reliably greater activation than controls in a cluster of regions running through the left amygdala, the uncus, and the parahippocampal gyrus, along with bilateral dorsomedial and inferior frontal cortex, but that group difference was carried almost entirely by contempt and anger rather than by fear. Contemptuous faces against happy faces produced a signal change of positive 0.72 percent in patients against essentially flat, negative 0.01 percent, in controls, a group difference reaching an effect size of 1.15 standard deviations, about as large an effect as clinical neuroimaging tends to produce. Angry faces against happy faces showed the same pattern at slightly smaller magnitude, 0.45 percent against negative 0.09 percent, an effect size of 1.00. Fearful faces against happy faces, the contrast every prior study in this publication’s third edition had built its entire paradigm around, produced no reliable group difference at all. The single most theoretically loaded number in the paper is the one that never crossed significance. If the amygdala’s business in this disorder were simply a heightened response to danger signals in general, fear ought to have been the most potent driver of any group difference, not the one contrast that produced nothing. Instead the discriminating stimuli were the two expressions whose entire communicative content is a judgment about the person being looked at, contempt saying you are beneath me and anger saying you have wronged me, both of them signals of social rejection and evaluative condemnation rather than signals of external danger. The amygdala these patients carried into the scanner was not, on this evidence, a generically overactive fear detector. It was a detector calibrated with unusual precision to exactly the kind of interpersonal verdict that a person with generalized social phobia spends their waking life anticipating and dreading.

Stein and colleagues were careful about what this design could and could not establish, and the limitations they flagged are worth taking as seriously as the finding itself. Fifteen patients and fifteen controls, split across five expression categories, leaves relatively few trials per condition and correspondingly modest power to detect anything but a fairly large effect, which raises the possibility that a genuine fear-specific signal existed but was simply too weak, given the trial count, to clear the statistical bar that the contempt and anger contrasts cleared with room to spare. The researchers also declined to preselect the amygdala as a region of interest, running a whole-brain analysis instead, a choice that is more conservative and less vulnerable to circular inference but that further reduces power relative to a hypothesis-driven ROI approach. Eight of the eleven patients who were formally assessed for it also met criteria for avoidant personality disorder, a comorbidity so common in generalized social phobia that excluding it would have produced an unrepresentative sample, but one that leaves open whether the specificity finding belongs to social phobia as a diagnostic category or to something closer to a dimensional avoidant-interpersonal profile that cuts across it. And at 1.5 tesla, with a 7-millimeter smoothing kernel applied to voxels already 3.44 by 3.44 by 7 millimeters, the paper’s own authors caution that its claims about left-lateralized amygdala involvement, and any claims about which amygdala subdivision, basolateral or central, is actually doing the work, sit at the edge of what the acquisition could resolve. None of that undercuts the specificity finding, which held up under a whole-brain multiple-comparisons correction and produced two of the largest effect sizes in the paper. But it leaves the paper, by design, a purely magnitude account. It tells you which regions lit up more in patients than controls, for which expressions. It says nothing at all about whether those regions were still talking to the rest of the brain the way they should have been, because a 2002 block-design activation study, run on a 1.5-tesla scanner with three imaging runs and no explicit measure of moment-to-moment coupling between regions, was not built to ask that question in the first place.

Ten years is a long time in functional neuroimaging, long enough for the field’s default unit of explanation to shift from where does more blood flow to how do regions talk to each other, and the paper that carries that shift into this beat comes out of a very different kind of research program than Stein’s. K. Luan Phan, the senior author, had by 2012 already built a decade of continuously NIH and VA funded work on corticolimbic circuitry in emotion and anxiety, first at the University of Michigan and the Ann Arbor VA, a translational program substantial enough to eventually earn him a Presidential Early Career Award for Scientists and Engineers, the United States government’s highest honor for an early-career scientist, before he went on to chair psychiatry departments first at the University of Illinois at Chicago and, more recently, at Ohio State. For Phan, this particular paper is one node inside an already mature and ongoing research program rather than a defining or founding contribution, part of a much longer arc concerned with mapping and eventually treating disordered corticolimbic function across the anxiety and mood disorders. Heide Klumpp, the first author, occupied a different position in her own trajectory entirely. At the time of this study she was an early-career researcher in Phan’s Michigan group, and the paper marks close to the starting point of what would become her own independent program at UIC, using functional imaging and EEG to identify biomarkers that predict which patients respond to cognitive behavioral therapy versus pharmacotherapy for anxiety and depression. Where Stein’s 2002 paper sits inside a career that would later pivot away from imaging altogether toward population genomics, Klumpp’s 2012 paper sits at the literal foundation of an imaging-centered career still actively being built around the model this paper first articulated, insula dysfunction and its cortical coupling as a candidate mechanism and, eventually, a candidate treatment biomarker.

The design reflects the era’s methodological maturity in ways Stein’s could not have. Klumpp, Angstadt, and Phan scanned twenty nine patients with generalized social anxiety disorder, seventeen women and twelve men with a mean age of twenty five, against twenty six healthy comparison subjects of similar age, using an emotional face matching task in the tradition Ahmad Hariri had popularized for probing amygdala reactivity, in which participants select which of two faces below a target expresses the same emotion as the target above, across fear, angry, happy, and neutral geometric-shape control blocks. Unlike Stein’s incidental gender-discrimination task, this is an implicit but attentionally engaged matching paradigm, still not asking anyone to explicitly label an emotion, but requiring active perceptual comparison of expressive content rather than a judgment orthogonal to it entirely. The clinical separation between groups was, if anything, sharper than in Stein’s sample, a mean Liebowitz Social Anxiety Scale score of 81.0 in patients against 8.2 in controls, and the sample itself nearly doubled Stein’s in size, fifty five participants against thirty, giving the study meaningfully more power to detect the kind of effect Stein’s design had been forced to leave ambiguous.

The headline reactivity finding, on its face, looks like a straightforward extension of Stein’s story into a new region. For fear faces contrasted against happy faces, patients showed significantly greater bilateral anterior insula activation than controls, a robust effect on the left surviving correction for multiple comparisons within the region of interest, and a smaller but still corrected effect on the right. The authors are explicit about the shape of that difference: patients showed genuine positive activation in the insula to fearful faces, while controls showed essentially none at all, which rules out a simpler and less interesting explanation, that patients were not so much hyperreactive as controls were unusually good at suppressing a response every brain generates by default. But look at which expression drove the effect, and a genuine tension with Stein’s finding appears rather than a simple confirmation of it. In Klumpp and colleagues’ data it was fear, not anger, that produced the robust, corrected insula effect; the angry-versus-happy contrast reached significance only at a considerably more liberal, uncorrected threshold. Stein’s amygdala effect a decade earlier had gone the other direction entirely, driven by contempt and anger with fear producing nothing at all. Different regions, different tasks, and different threat content each carrying the discriminating signal is not, on its own, a contradiction, since an implicit matching task that requires sustained attention to expressive content is not the same cognitive operation as an incidental gender judgment, and the insula’s interoceptive, felt-arousal function is not the amygdala’s more classically threat-detection role. But it does mean that any tidy story in which generalized social anxiety disorder is simply, uniformly, more sensitive to evaluative rejection signals specifically, contempt and anger over fear, does not survive contact with both papers held together. What survives is narrower and less satisfying: automatic hyperengagement of bottom-up affective machinery to socially relevant faces, with the specific expression that trips that wire apparently depending on which region, and which task, you happen to be measuring it with.

There is a second, quieter finding buried in Klumpp and colleagues’ results that this publication’s readers are especially well positioned to notice, because Edition 5 already taught them how the story ends. The amygdala does appear in this paper’s whole-brain results, bilaterally, greater in patients than controls for fear against happy faces. But it appears only at an uncorrected threshold, a much weaker statistical showing than the insula’s corrected effect, with a left cluster of just seventy three voxels and a right cluster of only eleven. In a sample more than three times the size of Stein’s, using a task built in the same Hariri tradition that has anchored amygdala research for two decades, the amygdala’s signal was already the secondary, barely-surviving finding, while a different region entirely carried the study’s real statistical weight. This publication’s fifth edition taught a 2024 harmonized multicenter connectivity study, pooling data across dozens of German research sites, that found a resting-state signature for anxiety disorders which never once implicates the amygdala at all. Read backward from that later result, this 2012 paper looks less like an isolated finding and more like an early, quiet symptom of the same erosion, a moment at which the field’s founding structure, the one this publication’s third edition traced back to a four-page 1998 report out of Tübingen, was already losing its grip on the data even as researchers kept including it in their models because the field’s inherited paradigms told them to look there.

What actually earns this paper its place in the field’s methodological history is not the reactivity finding at all, but what the researchers did with it next. Using the right anterior insula as a seed, Klumpp and colleagues ran a psychophysiological interaction analysis, testing not simply which regions activated alongside the insula but which regions showed a task-dependent change in their statistical coupling with it, a fundamentally different kind of question than anything Stein’s design could ask. Exactly one region in the entire brain showed a reliable group difference in that coupling: the dorsal anterior cingulate cortex, a large, robust cluster with a z-value of 4.40. And the nature of that difference was not simply more or less connectivity. It was a reversal in sign. Healthy controls showed strong positive coupling between the anterior insula and the dorsal anterior cingulate during threat processing, the two regions moving together in a way consistent with an intact salience-detection-to-cognitive-control pathway. Patients with generalized social anxiety disorder showed the opposite, an inverse relationship between the same two regions under the same task demands, insula activity rising as cingulate coupling fell rather than rose alongside it. That is not a magnitude difference of the kind Stein’s paper reported. It is a difference in the sign of a relationship, evidence that whatever the anterior insula is signaling about threat in these patients, the cortical region that ought to be receiving and regulating that signal is doing something closer to disengaging from it than amplifying it further, exactly at the moment the insula is working hardest.

Klumpp and colleagues were candid about what this design still could not do. A psychophysiological interaction analysis establishes a statistical relationship between two regions’ time courses; it cannot establish which region is driving the other, or whether some third, unmeasured region is driving both, a limitation the authors state plainly rather than overselling their finding as evidence of a causal breakdown. And because the analysis used only the anterior insula as its seed, it has nothing at all to say about whether the amygdala, sitting right there in the same dataset with its own weaker, uncorrected reactivity signal, shows the same inverted coupling to the dorsal anterior cingulate, or a different pattern entirely, or no altered coupling at all. The paper answers a connectivity question about exactly one node of the threat-processing system it studied, and by its own design, leaves the equivalent question about the other node, the one every prior study in this beat had spent a decade treating as the field’s central structure, completely untouched.

Set next to each other, these two papers converge on something worth taking seriously and diverge on something the field has still not built the study to settle. The convergence is a real methodological achievement, not merely a repeated assumption. A decade apart, using entirely different tasks, in samples drawn from different continents and different age ranges, both studies found automatic, behaviorally silent hyperengagement of bottom-up affective machinery specifically to socially threatening faces in generalized social anxiety disorder, with neither study finding any corresponding group difference in overt task performance, accuracy, or explicit emotion recognition. That is not a fragile or paradigm-dependent finding. It has now survived a change in task structure, a change in scanner field strength, a change in analytic era, and a near tripling of sample size, and it held up every time. Whatever else is unsettled about this disorder’s neural architecture, the automaticity of its threat bias, operating beneath conscious perceptual judgment, looks like real, cumulative evidence rather than an artifact of any one lab’s design choices.

The divergence is where this beat’s real unfinished business lives. Stein and colleagues’ design, for all its methodological care, could only ever produce a magnitude account, a claim about which regions light up more in patients than controls, for which specific facial expressions, with no way even in principle to distinguish a region that is intrinsically hyperreactive from a region whose local threshold is normal but whose cortical regulation has failed to arrive, the exact ambiguity this publication’s first edition already flagged for a completely different pair of regions and a completely different paradigm. Klumpp and colleagues built precisely the tool that ambiguity calls for, a connectivity analysis capable of asking not just how much a region fires but whether it is still wired the way it should be to the cortex meant to regulate it, and what that tool found was not simply confirmation of a familiar story with a new region substituted in. It found a signed reversal in a specific corticolimbic pathway, insula to dorsal anterior cingulate, alongside a weakening, not a strengthening, of the amygdala’s own claim to centrality. Those are not compatible pieces of the same simple story. They are evidence that the field’s unit of explanation itself needs to change, from which region is louder to which connections have stopped carrying the right sign.

No study yet has done the one thing that could actually adjudicate between the possibility that generalized social anxiety disorder involves two independently miscalibrated threat detectors, an amygdala and an insula each malfunctioning for its own local, task-specific reasons, and the more parsimonious possibility that both regions’ input signals are essentially unremarkable, and that the field has been detecting the shadow of a single lesion, a broken connection between an intact salience-detection system and the prefrontal cortex meant to regulate it, that would show up as reactivity in whichever node a given study happened to seed its analysis around. Settling that would require a single sample, scanned with a task capable of separating fear from evaluative content the way Stein’s five-expression design did, with connectivity analyses seeded from both the amygdala and the anterior insula simultaneously, so that their coupling to shared cortical targets, and their coupling to each other, could finally be measured in the same brains rather than inferred by stitching two studies together a decade apart. Until that study exists, this beat’s two most careful papers leave their readers holding a genuinely uncomfortable position: certain that something in this disorder’s threat-processing machinery engages automatically and abnormally to socially evaluative faces, and still unable to say, after twenty years of increasingly sophisticated method, whether that something is a place or a connection.
