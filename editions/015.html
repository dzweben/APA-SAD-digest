The Social Brain Dispatch

The Feedback Loop and the Filing Cabinet

In 1997 a Sydney psychologist and his Philadelphia collaborator drew a single closed loop to explain why anyone grows anxious in a room full of strangers, built in large part to explain a therapy that already worked. A decade later a Boston researcher broke that loop into seven interchangeable parts, because the therapy the loop explained had stopped improving. Neither team has ever had to decide which of their architectures is actually the one running inside a given patient's head.

Edition No. 15 · Theoretical and Framework-Spanning Reviews · September 10, 2026

By the middle of the 1990s, cognitive behavioral treatment for social phobia already worked, moderately. Richard Heimberg's cognitive behavioral group therapy, built and tested through the 1980s at the Adult Anxiety Clinic he directed at Temple University, had beaten a credible educational placebo in a randomized trial before anyone had published a complete theory of what the therapy was supposed to be correcting. A decade later, in the largest treatment trial the disorder had ever seen, that same therapy was pitted against phenelzine, the monoamine oxidase inhibitor whose apparent selectivity for social phobia symptoms had built Michael Liebowitz's entire research program at the New York State Psychiatric Institute, a program this Dispatch traced to its founding paper one edition ago. Fifty eight percent of patients responded to the group therapy. Sixty five percent responded to the drug. Twenty seven percent responded to an inert control condition designed only to look credible. A treatment that halves a disabling disorder's grip on a little more than half its patients is a genuine achievement, and it is also, starkly, a plateau. What nobody could yet say with any precision, in 1997 or for a long while after, was why the therapy that worked, worked, for which patients it worked least, and which of several plausible mechanisms inside an anxious mind the exposure and the cognitive restructuring were actually supposed to be touching.

Two papers, published exactly a decade apart by researchers who read and leaned on each other closely, tried to answer that question by doing something neither a trial nor a treatment manual does, drawing an actual architecture of the anxious mind, wiring diagram and all. The first, published in 1997 by Ronald Rapee of Macquarie University and Richard Heimberg of Temple, drew that architecture as a single closed loop, one signal generated, compared, judged, and fed back into itself. The second, published in 2007 by Stefan Hofmann of Boston University, broke the loop into seven largely independent parts and insisted that no patient necessarily runs on all seven. Between them sits an entire argument about what kind of thing a psychological theory of a disorder is even supposed to be, an argument the field has never quite settled and mostly stopped noticing it was having.

Heimberg came to the 1997 paper from an unusually well positioned vantage. He had spent the preceding decade turning the Adult Anxiety Clinic into the place where cognitive behavioral group therapy for social phobia was invented, refined, and tested against a placebo condition explicitly designed to be credible enough that a null result would have meant something, the trial that appears in this very paper's own reference list as evidence the treatment worked before the theory existed to say why. He had also just finished coediting, with Liebowitz and two others, the volume Social Phobia: Diagnosis, Assessment, and Treatment, a single table of contents that happened to contain David Clark and Adrian Wells's new cognitive model of social phobia alongside separate chapters on the ethological, conditioning, and psychodynamic traditions that had each, until then, been talking past one another. Heimberg was, in short, the person in the field who had just finished convening every partial account of social phobia between two covers and could see exactly where they agreed and where they didn't. Two years later he tried to reconcile them into a single mechanism, in partnership with Rapee, whose own career was, at that moment, quietly bending in the opposite direction, outward from clinic and cognition toward etiology, parenting, and childhood. Rapee would spend the following years building the developmental research program, anchored in his own 1997 review of childrearing and anxiety that this very paper cites as a source, that eventually produced the Cool Kids program he still directs from Macquarie's Centre for Emotional Health, one of the most widely disseminated child anxiety treatments in the world. The 1997 model sits at a hinge in his career, arguably the last paper in which his contribution to the science of social anxiety was primarily architectural rather than developmental.

The architecture itself begins from a premise so plain it barely reads as a claim, that a person with social phobia assumes other people are inherently critical and attaches enormous weight to being appraised well by them. From there, on entering, anticipating, or merely brooding afterward over a social situation, the individual forms what Rapee and Heimberg call a mental representation of the self as seen by the audience, a construct they take pains to say is not some internal photograph or, in their own italicized phrasing, an homunculus, but a loosely integrated amalgam assembled in real time from long term memory, from proprioceptive and autonomic cues such as a racing heart or a flushed face, and from the audience's own ambiguous verbal and nonverbal feedback. Attentional resources are then split between monitoring that representation and scanning the environment for external signs of disapproval, a frown, a shift in posture, boredom, while the individual simultaneously generates a second construct, a prediction of the performance standard the audience is presumed to be holding them to in that specific situation. The entire architecture turns on the gap between those two constructs. The representation of how one is being seen is compared against the presumed standard, and the size of the resulting discrepancy determines the perceived probability and cost of negative evaluation, which in turn generates the cognitive, behavioral, and physiological symptoms of anxiety, which feed straight back into the mental representation and restart the cycle. It is, deliberately, a single serial engine with one moving part repeated at different stations, audience in, representation out, standard in, discrepancy out, anxiety out, representation updated, audience in again.

Every station of that engine carries its own empirical weight, and Rapee and Heimberg are careful to attach it. The claim that attention is captured early and involuntarily by threat draws on the modified Stroop task, in which patients with social phobia take reliably longer to name the ink color of words like stupid than of matched neutral words, and on a dot probe study by Asmundson and Stein showing the same patients orient faster toward a screen location that had just displayed a negative evaluative word. The claim that this priming is not merely learned but evolved is imported wholesale from ethology, from Ohman's account of preattentive threat detection and, by direct citation, from Trower and Gilbert's 1989 argument that human social anxiety descends from agonic, dominance-structured threat displays in other primates, submission as the price of staying inside the safety of the group, a paper this Dispatch already spent an entire edition inside four issues ago. The claim that the mental representation is systematically more negative than reality draws on a cluster of findings that patients underrate their own social performance even when independent raters do not, a bias specific to social situations and to judgments of the self rather than of others, and reversible, notably, by video feedback and by cognitive behavioral therapy itself, evidence the authors read as proof the representation is a moving target rather than a fixed trait. And in a passage that would prove more consequential than its authors could have known, they note, almost in passing, that one 1996 study found a shift in patients' estimate of the cost of a hypothetical social catastrophe predicted treatment outcome better than a shift in their estimate of its likelihood, a single sentence buried in a section on appraisal that a very different theorist would, a decade later, promote to load bearing status.

The model's account of etiology is comparatively modest and openly speculative, and it is worth pausing on precisely because of how carefully it is hedged. Twin studies, Rapee and Heimberg note, show a substantial genetic contribution to anxiety in general, but that genetic signal is shared across nearly every anxiety disorder and offers no purchase on why one person's threat appraisal style gets channeled specifically toward social evaluation rather than, say, contamination or heights. The channeling, they argue, is more plausibly the work of family environment, citing evidence that overprotective parenting, parental modeling of excessive concern with others' opinions, and restricted family socialization each correlate more strongly with social phobia than with other anxiety disorders, and citing as well a study, coauthored by Rapee himself, showing that anxious parents observably reinforce avoidant coping in their children in real time during a laboratory task. None of this etiology feeds back into the moment to moment mechanics of the loop. It sits alongside it, an origin story bolted onto a maintenance model, and the paper is honest enough to say so.

Ten years is not a long time in a field's history, but it was long enough for the plateau to harden into a diagnosis of its own. By 2007, Stefan Hofmann, who had built Boston University's Social Anxiety Program into one of the field's most productive engines of randomized trials and meta-analysis, was reaching for the same numbers this Dispatch opened with, the fifty eight, the sixty five, the twenty seven, Heimberg's own flagship trial, to argue that conventional cognitive behavioral therapy for social anxiety disorder had settled into small to medium effect sizes against placebo and needed something other than a better delivery format to move past them. A modified protocol built by Clark and colleagues in 2003, targeting self focused attention and safety behaviors with unusual explicitness, had produced uncontrolled effect sizes above one and even above two at posttest, a result strong enough to suggest the mechanism, not merely the dose, was the thing worth fixing. Hofmann's answer was not a new treatment manual but a new theory, and in an odd generational irony, the evidence he used to justify replacing the architecture Heimberg's generation had built was drawn substantially from a trial Heimberg himself had run.

Where Rapee and Heimberg had built one engine, Hofmann built what reads more like a parts list, seven maintaining factors, each with its own dedicated literature, each capable in principle of operating on its own in a given patient without the others. The first factor is a mismatch between perceived social standards and perceived ability to meet them, and here Hofmann imports something genuinely new to the field, Heinz Heckhausen and Peter Gollwitzer's action theory, the distinction between a deliberative mindset appropriate to deciding whether to pursue a goal and an implemental mindset appropriate to actually executing one. Patients with social anxiety, in his reading, get stuck rehearsing the deliberative question of whether they can succeed socially right through the moment they should have switched to simply acting, a failure of goal setting borrowed from motivational psychology rather than from any theory of fear. The second factor, self focused attention, restates and extends Rapee and Heimberg's attentional claim with newer evidence, including a study by Chen and colleagues finding that patients with social phobia processed faces, of any emotional valence, less than they processed ordinary household objects in the same scene, and a study by Hirsch and Matthews finding that patients lack the positive inferential bias that lets non-anxious people read an ambiguous social cue charitably by default. The third factor, a negative self image experienced from an observer's vantage rather than from one's own, draws on Hackmann and colleagues' finding that patients spontaneously generate mental images of themselves as if watching from outside their own body, and on Higgins's self discrepancy theory, the gap between the traits one has and the traits one believes an observer expects, giving Rapee and Heimberg's amalgamated representation a sharper theoretical home than the earlier paper had offered it.

The fourth factor is the one buried sentence from 1997 grown into an architectural claim. Hofmann returns to Foa and colleagues' 1996 finding and reports the actual numbers behind it, a partial correlation of point seven six between change in estimated social cost and posttreatment symptom severity once change in estimated probability is statistically controlled, against a partial correlation of only point two seven in the other direction. Where Rapee and Heimberg had let probability and cost sit side by side as two inputs to the same judgment, Hofmann promotes cost to the dominant term, arguing that what actually drives social anxiety is less a belief that humiliation is likely than a conviction that it would be unbearable if it happened. The fifth factor, perceived lack of control over one's own emotional response, draws on evidence that patients attribute their anxiety to something closer to spontaneous panic than to any traceable cause, compounding the cost problem, since an uncontrollable catastrophe is worse than a controllable one. The sixth, perceived rather than actual social skill deficit, leans on Stopa and Clark's finding that patients underrate performance judged equivalently competent by outside raters, and on the awkward fact that social skills training adds little over exposure alone, evidence Hofmann reads as showing the deficit is almost entirely one of self perception rather than of behavior.

The seventh and eighth factors are where Hofmann's model does something Rapee and Heimberg's could only gesture toward. Safety behaviors, the subtle avoidances buried in a single paragraph of the 1997 paper, eye contact avoided, voice kept flat, position taken at the edge of a room, become in Hofmann's account the single most consequential maintaining mechanism of all, because they close what he calls a positive feedback loop, letting a patient survive a social encounter without ever letting the encounter disconfirm the belief that drove the safety behavior in the first place. He cites Wells and colleagues' demonstration, and a later replication by Morgan and Raffle, that exposure therapy works measurably better when patients are explicitly forbidden from using their safety behaviors during it, the same response prevention logic Rapee and Heimberg had floated as a treatment implication without direct evidence in hand a decade earlier, now with the trial behind it. And post event rumination, essentially absent from the 1997 architecture, becomes the mechanism by which the whole cycle survives from one social encounter to the next, evidenced by Rachman and colleagues' finding that brooding over a past interaction predicts anxiety and avoidance the next time around, and by Abbott and Rapee's demonstration of an asymmetry that should stop any clinician cold, that non-anxious people's memories of an ambiguous performance actually improve over the week following it, while anxious people's memories, examined a week out, have curdled further than they were on the day.

Hofmann's treatment implications follow directly and specifically from each factor, down to a recommended set of what he calls behavioral experiments designed to test a patient's catastrophic cost estimates directly rather than merely argue them down, walking a busy street with one's zipper open, buying a book and returning it minutes later, asking a stranger out, engineered social mishaps whose entire point is to let a patient discover, empirically, that the ceiling on social catastrophe is much lower than their nervous system insists. And running through the whole treatment section is a vocabulary that would have been foreign to Rapee and Heimberg's Beckian cognitive restructuring, an instruction, in Hofmann's own words, to encourage patients to experience and accept the feeling of anxiety to its fullest rather than to argue it away, a phrase closer to acceptance and commitment therapy than to classical cognitive therapy, and an early tell of the direction Hofmann's own career would bend over the following fifteen years, toward acceptance based and process based treatment far from where this 2007 paper starts. Crucially, Hofmann does not claim all seven factors operate in every patient. He offers, as an appendix, a short self report instrument he calls the Approach to Social Situations Scale, intended not as a diagnostic tool but as a way to find out which two or three of the seven levers are actually worth pulling for the person in the room, an explicit admission that the theory is meant to be disassembled patient by patient rather than applied whole.

Set side by side, the two papers agree on more than their different vocabularies suggest. Both treat a distorted, audience facing image of the self as the center of gravity of the disorder, an agreement concrete enough that this Dispatch has already watched it tested directly, in Philippe Goldin's imaging of cognitive reappraisal of exactly these negative self beliefs, and in the randomized trial his group ran four years later asking whether shifting the neural timing of that reappraisal tracked who actually got better. Both single out cost over probability as the sharper predictor of outcome, a finding first noticed as a footnote in 1997 and elevated to a structural axis in 2007, without either paper's authors apparently noticing they were reporting the same fact at different volumes a decade apart. Both, finally, orbit a third paper neither one supersedes, Clark and Wells's 1995 cognitive model, cited by Rapee and Heimberg as a co-founder and by Hofmann as a foundation to be extended, a paper without a stable DOI that nonetheless functions as the field's actual shared ancestor, present in every room these two models occupy without ever quite being in either of them.

Where they part is not a matter of emphasis but of what each team thought a theory of psychopathology is for. Rapee and Heimberg wanted one mechanism, elegant enough to generate testable predictions, and they got one, a discrepancy signal whose moment to moment fluctuation Poulton and Andrews were able to track directly against state anxiety during an actual speech. But a single mechanism bought at the price of specificity, because the 1997 paper's own treatment section can only gesture at attentional retraining through a single case study of one man's fear of blushing, treated over six sessions, its authors admitting outright that no adequately tested technique yet existed to redirect the attentional allocation their model made central. Hofmann's seven factors, evidenced one by one across seven separate literatures, buy exactly what Rapee and Heimberg's engine could not, a concrete lever for nearly every patient who walks in the door. What they cost is the earlier paper's explanatory economy. A theory built to be individualized, matched factor by factor against a given patient's scale scores, is no longer quite predicting anything in the way a single feedback loop predicts a speech's rising and falling anxiety, it is cataloguing. Even the intellectual imports each team reached for trace this same fork. Rapee and Heimberg reached backward into evolutionary ethology, Ohman's preattentive threat systems, Trower and Gilbert's dominance hierarchies, to explain why any of this should exist at all. Hofmann reached sideways into motivational and self regulation psychology, Gollwitzer's action phases, Higgins's self discrepancy, vocabularies with nothing to say about why a nervous system would ever have built these mechanisms in the first place, only about how they operate once built. Forty years into cognitive models of social anxiety, the models have not stood still. They have simply absorbed whatever adjacent field happened to be productive that decade, evolutionary theory in the late eighties, motivational and regulatory psychology by the mid two thousands, and the neural computation that this Dispatch's own second edition surveyed since. The biology has, in its own way, already stumbled into the same fork from the opposite direction. Heidi Klumpp's finding, covered here three editions ago, that resting amygdala to prefrontal connectivity before treatment predicts who actually responds to cognitive behavioral therapy is a connectivity based answer to precisely the question Hofmann's Approach to Social Situations Scale is asking with a questionnaire, which patient, out of many plausible mechanisms, is actually running the one this treatment can reach.

What neither paper has ever attempted, and what nobody fully has in the thirty years since the first of them, is a translation of any of this into something a model could fit rather than a story a clinician could recognize. A discrepancy signal, a cost weight that outruns a probability weight, a positive feedback loop sustained by a safety behavior, all of it is described here entirely in words and boxes, never in a parameter estimated from a person's actual choices or physiology. The uncertainty modeling this Dispatch reviewed under Grupe and Nitschke, and the biased social learning rate Koban and colleagues later measured directly inside the brain of someone updating a belief about their own reputation, are exactly the kind of formal apparatus that could in principle give Rapee and Heimberg's discrepancy, or Hofmann's cost that outweighs probability, an actual number rather than a construct a self report scale merely asks about. Neither team made that leap, and the gap between a box-and-arrow model precise enough to guide a therapist's next question and a computational model precise enough to be fit, tested, and falsified against behavior remains the field's largest unclaimed territory. The question this edition leaves is not which of these two architectures is correct, since substantial parts of both plainly are, in different patients, sometimes in the same patient at different points in treatment. It is whether a field that has spent three decades building increasingly detailed verbal machinery for the anxious mind is capable of ever turning one of its own boxes into an equation, or whether the cognitive model of social anxiety disorder is destined to remain exactly what both of these papers are, at their best, a precise and clinically durable story that no computation has ever been asked to prove.
