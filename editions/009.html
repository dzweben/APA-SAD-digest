The Social Brain Dispatch

The Category That Never Watched Itself Grow

A cohort followed from 1975 to 1992 found that a fourteen-year-old’s social phobia predicts, with real specificity, a twenty-two-year-old’s social phobia, using three photographs taken nine years apart of a trajectory nobody ever watched in motion. A second study, watching the same developmental window four times in four years, found that only one adolescent in ten rides a trajectory serious enough to matter, and that it is already bending back down by the time the first study’s cameras open again.

Edition No. 9 · Developmental Trajectories and Longitudinal Cohorts · August 17, 2026

Eight editions into this publication’s education in social anxiety disorder, the reader has been taught the disorder mostly as a set of simultaneous facts about a single moment. Edition 1 taught a circuit, an amygdala that fires and a regulatory system that answers late. Edition 2 taught an arithmetic, a brain that under-updates its estimate of safety once a threat has passed. Edition 4 taught a developmental window, the specific years in which a maturing affective system outpaces a maturing regulatory one. Edition 6 taught a heritability estimate and a two-year-old’s approach-avoidance latency that predicts, imperfectly, the shy adult that two-year-old will become. Edition 7 taught a conditioning deficit, a failure to learn safety once a threat cue is repeatedly presented without consequence. None of that, taken together, actually answers a question a clinical scientist should find uncomfortable to have gone eight editions without confronting directly, what does it mean, mechanistically and empirically, for social anxiety disorder to have a course at all. Is the socially anxious fourteen-year-old and the socially anxious twenty-five-year-old, assuming both exist in the same body, living through one continuous illness that simply persists, the way an infection persists until something clears it, or is calling both of them by the same diagnostic label a convenient fiction that papers over what might really be several distinct developmental pathways, most of which resolve on their own and a minority of which do not. The stakes are not abstract. Every neuroimaging study this publication has taught so far scanned people at a single cross-section of an unknown trajectory, and Edition 4 already flagged the resulting confound directly, an adult who has spent fifteen years inside a chronic disorder does not have the brain that same person had the week the disorder became possible. Whether that confound is a minor nuisance or the central fact of the field turns entirely on how heterogeneous and how variable the underlying developmental course actually is, and that is a question no circuit study, however elegant, is built to answer. Two papers, seventeen years apart, tried to answer it directly, and they answer it in ways that do not so much confirm each other as reveal, each to the other, exactly what its own method cannot see.

The earlier paper is also, in a specific and slightly surprising sense, a prequel to Edition 4. Its senior author is Daniel Pine, the same Daniel Pine whose Section on Development and Affective Neuroscience at the National Institute of Mental Health this publication has already met testing Eric Nelson’s three-node maturational theory against a scanner. But in 1998 there was no NIMH section yet. Pine would not join the institute’s intramural program until 2000, and the paper this edition teaches was written two years before that, while he was still a child psychiatrist at Columbia and the New York State Psychiatric Institute, working not with an fMRI console but with someone else’s data, a nineteen-year-old prospective community cohort he had not designed and did not own. That cohort, the Children in the Community Study, belonged to Judith Brook and Patricia Cohen, who had begun it in 1975 as something genuinely uncommon for its era, a study of psychiatric disorder drawn from a random sample of households in two upstate New York counties rather than from a psychiatric clinic’s waiting room. The distinction matters more than it sounds. Almost everything psychiatric epidemiology had produced up to that point about the course of anxiety and mood disorders came from people who had already been identified as patients, a sampling frame that guarantees severity, guarantees treatment contact, and guarantees a kind of survivorship that makes any conclusion about a disorder’s natural course suspect from the start. Cohen, a methodologist by training, and Brook, a developmental psychologist whose own substantive interests ran toward parenting and adolescent risk behavior, built instead a resource that could, in principle, capture children who never came near a clinician at all, which meant it could answer a question clinic-based samples cannot even ask, what happens, on average, to the ordinary range of a disorder’s severity as an unselected population ages. By the time Pine borrowed the resource in the mid-1990s, it had already tracked its youngest participants from early childhood into their twenties, structured diagnostic interviews administered separately to each young person and, in earlier waves, to a parent, generating DSM-III-R diagnoses at three widely spaced points, 1983, when the sample’s mean age was fourteen, 1985, roughly two years later, and 1992, when most of the cohort had reached their early twenties. Pine, Cohen, Gurley, Brook, and Ma treated the first two waves as the adolescent measurement and the third as the early-adulthood outcome, in a sample of seven hundred seventy six young people, and asked a single, deceptively large question, does having an anxiety or depressive disorder as a teenager change the odds of having one as a young adult, and if it does, does it matter which specific disorder a person started with.

The headline answer was yes on both counts, and the second half is the more theoretically interesting one. In simple logistic models, having any anxiety or depressive disorder in adolescence raised the odds of having an anxiety or depressive disorder in early adulthood by roughly two to three fold, a number large enough to matter clinically but, on its own, not large enough to settle anything about mechanism, since a two to threefold elevation is compatible with genuine continuity of a single illness, with a shared underlying vulnerability that simply re-expresses itself twice, or with nothing more specific than general psychiatric morbidity begetting more general psychiatric morbidity. What broke that ambiguity was the specificity analysis, which asked not whether any adolescent disorder predicts any adult disorder but whether particular diagnoses predict themselves. Some categories showed almost no specificity at all. Adolescent depression predicted a wide spread of adult outcomes, generalized anxiety disorder among them, with a particularly strong link between the two that cut straight across the internalizing boundary supposedly separating a mood disorder from an anxiety disorder, evidence that whatever these categories track, they are not tracking cleanly separable disease processes at the level DSM-III-R’s own architecture implies. Two categories broke from that pattern in the opposite direction, and social phobia was one of them. Adolescent social phobia predicted adult social phobia specifically, more than it predicted adult panic disorder, generalized anxiety, or depression, and simple phobia showed the same self-predicting pattern, adolescents afraid of dogs or heights becoming adults afraid of dogs or heights rather than adults with some other, more diffuse anxiety profile. That is a real finding about the taxonomic status of social phobia, not merely about its persistence, evidence that whatever holds a socially anxious fourteen-year-old and a socially anxious twenty-two-year-old together across eight years, one household move, and one full transition through adolescence into an independent adult life is doing something more specific than general trait anxiety simply drifting forward in time. It behaves, in this data, like a genuine kind, a disorder that reproduces itself as itself rather than dissolving into or re-emerging as something else.

But look closely at what that finding is actually built from, and its evidentiary shape turns out to be strangely thin for a claim this strong. The entire study rests on three photographs, taken years apart at the extremes, of a phenomenon the paper never once observes in motion. A young person coded as having social phobia in 1983 and again as having social phobia in 1992 might have been continuously, unremittingly ill for the full intervening decade, or might have fully remitted by 1986 and relapsed independently in 1990 for reasons that have nothing to do with whatever process produced the original episode, and the logistic regression cannot distinguish those two young people, because both are simply coded as adolescent-onset, adult-present. The design has no access to the shape of the curve connecting its two endpoints, not its slope, not whether it is monotonic, not whether there were one, two, or five distinct episodes in between, not whether the eventual adult case looks anything like the adolescent case beyond sharing a diagnostic label applied at two moments the better part of a decade apart. It also, by construction, treats the population as though it sorts cleanly into people who have social phobia and people who do not, at each of three time points, discarding whatever information lived in the difference between a subclinical fourteen-year-old with real but threshold-missing social fear and a fourteen-year-old with none at all, a binary collapse that a purely categorical, low-frequency-assessment design has no way around. None of this makes the specificity finding wrong. It makes it a finding about where a trajectory starts and where it ends, authoritative on exactly that question and silent on almost everything a developmental scientist would actually want to know about what happens between those two points, which is precisely the gap a very differently built study, published fifteen years later out of an entirely different research tradition, was designed to fill.

That second study’s intellectual lineage runs through a stranger path into developmental psychopathology than Pine’s did, and the path explains a great deal about why the paper it produced looks the way it does. Michiel Westenberg, the senior author, did not start in anxiety research or in neuroscience at all. His doctoral training, at Washington University in St. Louis, was under Jane Loevinger, the psychologist who built one of the field’s most ambitious stage theories of ego development, a largely clinical, projective-instrument-driven account of how a person’s whole way of construing self and morality matures across the lifespan, about as far from a growth-curve model of anxiety symptoms as developmental psychology gets. From there Westenberg went to Berkeley as a postdoctoral fellow inside Jack and Jeanne Block’s longitudinal study of personality, the same tradition that gave the field its foundational vocabulary for individual differences in self-regulation, ego-control and ego-resiliency, constructs built entirely around the observation that people do not develop along one shared curve but sort, durably, into qualitatively different patterns of adjustment under the same environmental pressures. That is the sensibility Westenberg carried to Leiden University, where he has now spent more than two decades building a program explicitly organized around distinguishing typical from atypical developmental course in social anxiety, a research question that is, at its core, the Blocks’ individual-differences intuition transplanted from personality into psychopathology. The paper this edition teaches, led by his then-doctoral student Anne Miers together with Anke Blöte, Mark de Rooij, and Cathy Bokhorst, is the empirical instrument that intuition built, a study designed from the outset not to estimate one average developmental curve for social anxiety across adolescence, the way a simpler growth model would, but to ask how many genuinely distinct curves are hiding inside that average, and who ends up on each one.

The design earns the question it asks. Rather than following one cohort from age nine to age twenty-one, which would require a study lasting twelve years, Miers and colleagues used a cohort-sequential, or accelerated longitudinal, design, recruiting overlapping age cohorts simultaneously and following each through four assessment waves across roughly four calendar years, so that a nine-year-old in the youngest cohort and a seventeen-year-old in the oldest cohort were both being measured at the same real-world moment, and their overlapping age ranges could be spliced together statistically into a single continuous developmental curve spanning ages nine to twenty-one, the entire span of interest, compressed into a study that actually took only four years to run. Three hundred thirty one adolescents from Dutch secondary and primary schools, a nonclinical, community sample in the same spirit as the Children in the Community Study though drawn from an ordinary population rather than deliberately tracked from early childhood, completed the Social Anxiety Scale for Adolescents at each of the four waves, a twenty two item continuous measure rather than a categorical diagnosis, retained at rates of eighty three to ninety five percent across waves, a genuinely strong figure for a four-wave adolescent study. Where Pine’s team asked a logistic regression to sort people into two boxes at two distant time points, Miers and colleagues fit latent class growth models, using the SAS PROC TRAJ procedure, to the entire continuous trajectory of each adolescent’s social anxiety score, letting the data itself determine how many distinct shapes of development were present in the sample and estimating, for every participant, a posterior probability of belonging to each shape, a diagnostic the field uses to judge whether the resulting classes are genuinely separable groups of people or an arbitrary carving of what is really one smooth, continuous distribution.

Three shapes emerged, and their relative sizes are the finding most worth sitting with. The great majority of adolescents, fifty four percent of the sample, sat on a moderate and decreasing trajectory, entering the study with midrange social anxiety scores that eased gradually across adolescence, and a further thirty six percent sat on a low and decreasing trajectory that started low and eased further still, meaning that ordinary adolescent development in this data, for roughly nine adolescents in ten, involves genuine, real-time attenuation of social anxiety rather than escalation. Only thirty individuals, 9.6 percent of the sample, rode a high and changing trajectory, entering already elevated around age ten, climbing further through early adolescence, and only beginning to decline and level off after age eighteen, a shape that maps almost exactly onto the developmental window Nelson, Leibenluft, McClure, and Pine’s 2005 model predicted in Edition 4, a maturational imbalance that opens at puberty and narrows gradually across the second decade of life. Average posterior probabilities of .92, .87, and .85 for the high, moderate, and low classes respectively indicate the three groups are cleanly separable rather than statistical artifacts of an underlying continuum, a meaningfully strong result for a growth mixture model of this kind. What actually distinguished the classes turned out to be layered in a way no single predictor domain could have shown alone. Cognitive variables, a tendency toward negative interpretations of ambiguous social situations and a habit of self-focused attention during social performance, differentiated all three trajectories from one another, with adolescents on the high trajectory showing markedly elevated odds of negative interpretation bias relative to the moderate group and adolescents on the low trajectory showing markedly reduced odds relative to that same reference group. Social competence variables told a narrower story, distinguishing the high group specifically from the moderate group, poorer observer-rated social skill and greater teacher-reported social difficulty at school both raised the odds of riding the high trajectory rather than the moderate one, but said nothing about what separated moderate from low. Temperament, measured through parent-reported neuroticism, extraversion, and behavioral inhibition, told a narrower story still, differentiating only the low trajectory from the moderate one and touching the high trajectory not at all, meaning that whatever pushes a child from an ordinary moderate developmental course up into the small, clinically consequential minority that stays elevated through late adolescence, it is not simply more of the same temperamental ingredients this publication already taught, in Edition 6, predict shyness and behavioral inhibition in early childhood. Cognition and social competence, not temperament alone, are doing the work of explaining who ends up in the tail that actually matters.

Put next to each other, these two papers converge on one point sturdy enough to treat as established and diverge on a point that neither, on its own, has the design to resolve. The convergence is real, both studies place the transition through adolescence at the center of the story, both find that a meaningful subset, not the whole population, carries a socially anxious profile forward in a way that looks consequential rather than incidental, and Miers and colleagues’ high and changing trajectory, at 9.6 percent of an entirely unselected sample, sits in strikingly plausible proximity to prevalence estimates for social anxiety disorder itself, suggesting the growth-mixture method may be resolving, at the level of continuous symptom trajectories, something close to the same population Pine’s diagnostic interviews were resolving at the level of categorical caseness. The divergence is where the real intellectual work is left undone. Pine and colleagues’ design assumes, by its very logistic-regression architecture, that social phobia behaves like a discrete kind, present or absent, and their specificity finding is genuinely strong evidence for that assumption at the level of diagnostic labels reproducing themselves across a decade. Miers and colleagues’ design assumes the opposite from the outset, that the true structure is continuous and that any apparent categories are latent classes carved out of that continuum by growth-modeling convenience, and what they actually find, a graded population with the overwhelming majority on trajectories that ease over time and only a thin minority on one that does not, is not obviously the same claim as Pine’s. A young person could plausibly cross a diagnostic threshold at fourteen, again at twenty two, and be coded by Pine’s method as an instance of self-predicting social phobia, while riding, in Miers and colleagues’ framework, a moderate and decreasing trajectory that happened to dip above a clinical cutoff at two unlucky measurement occasions nine years apart rather than genuinely occupying the high and changing class the whole time. Neither paper can tell you which of those two young people its own headline finding actually describes, because Pine’s method cannot see the shape of the curve between its three widely spaced snapshots, and Miers and colleagues’ method, precisely because it never applies a diagnostic instrument at all, cannot tell you whether membership in the high and changing class corresponds to clinical caseness the way Pine’s interviews defined it, or to something merely elevated but subclinical throughout.

There is a further complication buried inside Miers and colleagues’ own numbers that Pine’s design would have simply erased. Their high and changing class is already declining by age eighteen, on average, four to five years before Pine’s early-adulthood assessment window even opens, which raises a real possibility that some meaningful fraction of what Pine’s team measured as a persisting adult case, in a sample assessed at a mean age of roughly twenty two, was not persistence in the sense either paper’s theoretical framing implies, but a late, partial re-elevation off an already-declining curve, driven by a wholly different set of early-adulthood stressors, leaving home, a first serious job, a first serious romantic relationship, that neither a cognitive interpretation bias measured at seventeen nor a behavioral-inhibition score measured at nine has any obvious way to predict. Nobody has yet run the study that could adjudicate this directly, a design dense enough in its sampling to map the actual shape of a trajectory the way Miers and colleagues did, but extended far enough in calendar time, and paired with the kind of structured diagnostic interview Pine’s team used at each wave rather than a continuous symptom scale alone, to know for certain whether the people riding a high and changing trajectory through age eighteen are the same people who show up, nine or ten years later, as Pine’s self-predicting adult social phobia cases, or a substantially different group that a purely categorical, widely spaced design would count as the same kind of continuity regardless. Until that study exists, this publication’s readers should hold both findings and claim no more from either than each was actually built to support, a specific, real form of diagnostic self-prediction across a decade whose internal shape remains genuinely unknown, and a specific, real form of population heterogeneity in symptom trajectory whose relationship to clinical caseness remains, just as genuinely, unmeasured.
