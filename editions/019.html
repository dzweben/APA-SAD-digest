The Social Brain Dispatch

The Amygdala Was Never the Problem, or Was It?

Two Melbourne-trained connectivity analyses, three years apart and run on different patients doing different tasks, both find the social anxiety signal somewhere other than the amygdala, inside a cortical network that talks to itself differently. The finding is either the field’s overdue correction or an artifact of where the models were allowed to look.

Edition No. 19 · Frontier Neuroscience · November 5, 2026

For a quarter century the working picture of the socially anxious brain has been a story about a gate and its keeper. The amygdala responds too vigorously to faces and evaluative threat, prefrontal cortex is supposed to dampen it and does so poorly, and the disorder lives in the weakened line of command between them. This Dispatch has met that picture repeatedly. Etkin and Wager (2007) gave it its meta-analytic credentials, Prater and colleagues (2013) found the coupling failing even at rest in generalized social anxiety, and Langhammer and colleagues (2024) went looking for the same architecture at rest across a multicenter anxiety-disorder sample. The stakes of getting this circuit wrong are practical. Every neuromodulation trial that targets lateral prefrontal cortex to calm the amygdala, every neurofeedback protocol that trains patients to suppress amygdala signal, and every biomarker meant to predict who responds to cognitive behavioral therapy inherits the assumption that the amygdala-prefrontal axis is where the disorder sits.

The two papers at the center of this edition ask what happens if you stop assuming it. Jamieson and colleagues (2023) examined the default mode network of young people with social anxiety while they judged themselves and judged how others would judge them. Schrammen and colleagues (2026) put adults with social anxiety in the scanner and asked them to look at angry and fearful faces and then to bring their reaction down, modeling the whole prefrontal-amygdala loop with directed connectivity. They share a method, dynamic causal modeling with group inference by parametric empirical Bayes, and they share a result that is awkward for the received view. Neither found the damage where the textbook says to look.

It helps to understand the method before the people, because the method is what unites them. Dynamic causal modeling, introduced by Friston, Harrison, and Penny (2003), treats the BOLD signal in a handful of chosen regions as the output of a generative model. Neural populations in each region are driven by experimental inputs and by one another, a hemodynamic model converts neural activity to the measured signal, and the fitting procedure estimates the coupling strengths that best explain the observed time series, with a penalty for complexity. The coupling parameters are rates, measured in hertz, and they are directed and signed. A positive value from region A to region B means activity in A pushes activity in B up, and a negative value means it pushes B down. This is the property that undirected functional connectivity cannot provide, and it is why the technique is attractive for a disorder theorized around top-down control. Correlation between amygdala and prefrontal cortex cannot say which is regulating which. A directed model at least claims to.

The group-level machinery matters as much. Parametric empirical Bayes, described by Friston and colleagues (2016) and laid out as a practical workflow by Zeidman and colleagues (2019), takes each participant’s fitted model and asks, at the second level, how group membership and covariates shift the connection strengths. Bayesian model reduction then prunes away parameters that do not earn their place, and Bayesian model averaging produces posterior estimates over the survivors. The currency is posterior probability, and the convention in both studies is to call a parameter credible when that probability exceeds a threshold, 0.95 in one and 0.99 in the other. Two features of the approach will matter when the papers are set against each other. A parameter pruned away is absent from the model, and the framework treats absence of evidence for a difference as the default. And the model space is chosen by the investigator in advance, so a region that is never entered as a node can never be found to differ.

The Melbourne thread in this story begins with a depression paper. Davey, Breakspear, Pujol, and Harrison (2017) used the same self-referential task and a three-node core of the default mode network, medial prefrontal cortex, posterior cingulate, and inferior parietal cortex, to show that disturbed self-appraisal in depression has a directed signature. Ben Harrison, a senior author on that paper and on the 2023 social anxiety study, has built a program at the University of Melbourne around precisely this move, taking large-scale networks that the resting-state literature described correlationally and giving them causal grammar, with depression as the proving ground and anxiety as the extension. Chris Davey, based at Orygen, the youth mental health center, anchors the clinical recruitment, and the 2023 sample of sixteen to twenty-five year olds comes out of that youth-service setting. Alec Jamieson, first author of the social anxiety paper, appears again on the 2026 paper, and the 2026 author list carries Harrison’s name beside a long roster from Germany. I draw the biographical detail here from the papers’ own affiliations, funding statements, and reference lists, and the lineage they reveal is a method crossing a hemisphere.

The German side of the 2026 paper belongs to a different tradition. The corresponding author, Elisabeth Leehr, works at the University of Münster, where Udo Dannlowski directs a large translational psychiatry program whose funding lines appear in the acknowledgments, the FOR2107 research unit on affective disorders and a newer collaborative research center on affective disorder trajectories. Leehr also sits in the long author list of the Langhammer consortium study that this Dispatch covered in Edition 5, which means the Münster group has already spent years looking at anxiety disorders through the resting-state lens. The 2026 paper is the same laboratory graduating from undirected to directed connectivity, importing a Melbourne analysis pipeline to do it. It is also preregistered on the Open Science Framework in February 2023, with a documented deviation protocol, which in a literature where the amygdala-prefrontal story was built partly on flexible analyses is a credential worth noting.

Start with the earlier study, because it asks the more conceptually ambitious question. Jamieson and colleagues (2023) recruited thirty-eight unmedicated people with social anxiety disorder, retained from forty-two after excluding one for motion and three for poor performance on the control task. They were aged sixteen to twenty-five, with a mean near twenty, twenty-three of them female, recruited from outpatient clinics in western and northern Melbourne and diagnosed with the structured clinical interview for DSM-5. Twenty-five of the thirty-eight, nearly two thirds, had comorbid major depression. Their Liebowitz scores averaged 84.5 against 23.2 in controls, which places them well into the severe range. The comparison group was seventy-two healthy controls drawn from a pool of 104 usable scans by variable-ratio matching on age and gender, with no lifetime psychiatric history.

The task is the quiet star of the design. Participants saw trait adjectives drawn from Anderson’s 1968 likeableness norms, deliberately chosen from the middle of the distribution so that words were neither flattering nor damning, and answered one of three questions in blocks. Would you use this word to describe yourself, would other people use this word to describe you, or does this word contain four or more vowels. The last condition is the baseline, matched on stimulus and button press but requiring attention to an external feature rather than the self. Each 27-second block held five words, six blocks per condition, interleaved with rest. The contrast between direct and reflected self-appraisal is the theoretical heart. Reflected appraisal, thinking about how one appears from outside, is the cognitive operation that the Clark and Wells cognitive model, which Jamieson and colleagues invoke, places at the center of the disorder, the construction of an observer’s-eye image of oneself that is then treated as evidence.

Behaviorally the groups barely separated. Reaction times did not differ, accuracy on the vowel task was 94.4 percent in controls and 94.0 in patients, and patients endorsed more adjectives as self-descriptive, 51.8 percent against 45.6 in direct appraisal, with a borderline difference in reflected appraisal, 47.9 against 42.7. Whole-brain activation analysis found nothing at family-wise corrected thresholds. At an uncorrected threshold patients showed less activation in left inferior frontal and premotor regions during both appraisal types. This is the profile of a disorder that a conventional activation analysis would have pronounced invisible.

The directed analysis found it. The authors defined three nodes by a conjunction across groups of regions engaged by both appraisal conditions and by rest, which is how they isolated a core-self default network, with peaks in medial prefrontal cortex at MNI coordinates of negative 10, 54, 10, posterior cingulate at negative 6, negative 50, 26, and left inferior parietal lobule at negative 48, negative 60, 24. Each participant’s time series was the first eigenvariate within 5 millimeters of the individual maximum, a driving input entered into the posterior cingulate, and both appraisal conditions were allowed to modulate every connection. The group model then included mean, diagnosis, and age. Parameters were averaged across the 256 models inside Occam’s window and thresholded at posterior probability above 0.95.

Two intrinsic pathways into medial prefrontal cortex differed. The posterior cingulate to medial prefrontal connection was weaker in patients, estimated at negative 0.09 hertz with posterior probability near 1, and the left inferior parietal to medial prefrontal connection was likewise altered by 0.08 hertz. During reflected appraisal, and only reflected appraisal, the modulation of those same two pathways moved in the opposite direction, 0.05 hertz more excitatory from posterior cingulate and negative 0.06 hertz from parietal cortex. Direct self-appraisal produced no group difference in modulation at all. Cross-validation of the intrinsic parameters predicted trait anxiety on the STAI at r = 0.25 (p = .004) and predicted nothing for Liebowitz severity or state anxiety.

The authors read this as a model of fear of negative evaluation. The posterior cingulate carries the narrative self, the inferior parietal cortex contributes perspective-taking and episodic retrieval, and the medial prefrontal cortex gates which self-representation reaches awareness. In their account social anxiety changes how the gate is fed when the self is viewed from outside. I find the account plausible and the arithmetic underneath it more interesting than they let on. The intrinsic and modulatory terms for the same pathway have opposite signs and similar magnitudes, so the summed coupling in patients during reflected appraisal is close to the control value, and the patient-control gap is widest at rest and during direct appraisal and narrowest during the very condition the paper highlights. That is my reading of the reported expected values and it should be treated as an inference, since I could not verify the supplementary tables and the paper itself flags a sign inconsistency in how it describes the parietal pathway as inhibitory while reporting a positive intrinsic value. If the inference holds, the finding is less a spike of pathology during reflected appraisal than a baseline abnormality that the task transiently masks, which is a different disease model and a more interesting one.

The authors also volunteer the limitation that matters most. Because the driving input was not mean-centered, the intrinsic connectivity here is the coupling expected when all task regressors are at zero, and that does not translate directly into resting-state connectivity measured without a task. They propose that future work estimate effective connectivity at true rest.

Schrammen and colleagues (2026) move from the self to the face and from default mode to the regulation loop, and in doing so they test the textbook claim head on. Their sample is 102 adults, sixty-one with social anxiety disorder and forty-one healthy controls, scanned in Münster between October 2018 and July 2022 as part of a transdiagnostic study of emotional processing in depression and anxiety. Diagnosis came from the SCID-I, confirmed against DSM-5 criteria. The patient group permitted comorbid acute or remitted depression and mild to moderate substance misuse, and eighteen patients were taking antidepressants, three of them more than one. Controls had no current or lifetime disorder.

The paradigm was adapted from an earlier published regulation task (Kuo and colleagues, 2016, as cited by the authors). Participants viewed angry, fearful, and neutral faces from the Karolinska set and either simply observed them or observed while trying to decrease their emotional response by a strategy of their own choosing. Behavioral ratings showed the expected manipulation. Negative faces were rated more negatively than neutral ones, F = 51.0 for angry and 34.5 for fearful, and a valence-by-group interaction showed a larger rise in negative affect in patients, F = 4.39 (p = .039) for angry and F = 6.35 (p = .014) for fearful. Regulation made ratings more positive in both groups. The groups did not differ in self-reported regulation success, and patients descriptively endorsed more maladaptive strategies.

The model has five nodes, amygdala, ventromedial, dorsolateral, and ventrolateral prefrontal cortex, and the pre-supplementary motor area, chosen from first- and second-level activation contrasts, with bidirectional connections among all of them, driving input into the amygdala, and modulation by the observe and regulate conditions. Here the input was mean-centered, so the baseline connectivity reflects the average across conditions. Group inference used parametric empirical Bayes with group and gender regressors, thresholded at posterior probability above 0.99, followed by leave-one-out cross-validation.

In controls the loop behaved as the textbook would predict. Observing negative faces produced excitation from amygdala to ventromedial prefrontal cortex of 0.34 hertz and inhibition of 0.68 hertz from ventromedial prefrontal cortex back onto the amygdala, with the pre-supplementary motor area exciting the amygdala at 0.52. Regulation then made the amygdala’s influence on every prefrontal node more negative, negative 0.34 onto dorsolateral, negative 0.38 onto ventrolateral, negative 0.60 onto ventromedial, and negative 0.54 onto the pre-supplementary motor area. Regulation, on this evidence, works partly by changing how the amygdala’s signal is received.

The patient-control comparison is where the paper pays off or disappoints, depending on your priors. No amygdala-prefrontal connection differed between groups in either condition. Every difference that survived was inside the prefrontal cortex. During observation the pre-supplementary motor area exerted a more negative influence on dorsolateral prefrontal cortex in patients, by 0.30 hertz. During regulation the coupling between pre-supplementary motor area and ventromedial prefrontal cortex was enhanced in both directions, plus 0.37 hertz one way and plus 0.31 the other, each at posterior probability 0.99. Predictive performance was modest. Leave-one-out models separated diagnostic groups at r = 0.24 (p = .009), predicted Liebowitz severity only marginally at r = 0.17 (p = .048), and did not predict the dysfunctional emotion regulation subscale of the cognitive emotion regulation questionnaire at all.

The authors interpret the enhanced premotor-ventromedial coupling as compensatory, an additional route that patients recruit to achieve the same regulation outcomes the behavioral data show they achieve, and they take the confinement of effects to intra-prefrontal circuits as a categorical neural signature of the disorder. They are candid about the constraints. Comorbid depression was not separated out and the pure-SAD subgroup was too small to analyze alone, antidepressants might shift connectivity, strategies were chosen freely so the regulation condition is a heterogeneous mixture, faces were only mildly negative, the insula and anterior cingulate were never modeled, and a sample of 102 is thin for cross-validating continuous outcomes.

Set side by side, the papers converge on something that the amygdala-centered tradition has been slow to absorb. In two independent samples, in two continents’ worth of scanners, with two different cognitive operations, the directed connectivity differences that reached a Bayesian threshold were cortico-cortical and were organized around a medial or ventromedial prefrontal hub. Medial prefrontal cortex receives the altered posterior cingulate and parietal inputs in the first study, and ventromedial prefrontal cortex is the end of the altered premotor coupling in the second. Whatever else social anxiety does, it appears to change how the prefrontal cortex is addressed by its cortical neighbors. Both studies also arrive at a prediction accuracy of about r = 0.25, which is statistically reliable and clinically useless, and both use samples in which depression is heavily represented. The convergence is therefore less strong than it looks and the shared weakness is as informative as the shared result.

The tension is conceptual and I think it is productive. Jamieson and colleagues tie their connectivity to trait anxiety, a dimensional variable, and to a cognitive process the disorder is theorized to maintain, which would make the signature graded and process-bound. Schrammen and colleagues argue the opposite, that the effect tracks diagnosis and not severity, a categorical signature. One of these inferences may be an artifact of what each study measured. The first predicted a dimensional trait inventory, the second a categorical label, and each prediction came out at about the same weak level. The papers have not tested the two hypotheses against each other, and neither could, because they differ in tasks, ages, medication status, and node sets all at once.

Each paper also exposes the other’s blind spot. Jamieson’s model contains no amygdala and no lateral prefrontal region, so it could not have found an amygdala-prefrontal abnormality even if one were there, and a task that asks about words rather than threatening faces gives such an abnormality little reason to appear. Schrammen’s model contains the amygdala and five prefrontal nodes but no posterior cingulate, no parietal cortex, and no self-referential component, so the default network pathways that Jamieson found would be invisible to it. The amygdala null in the 2026 study, meanwhile, deserves a caution proportionate to its prominence. A pruned parameter in a Bayesian model reduction pipeline is not positive evidence of equality, and a 61-versus-41 contrast for a coupling that has been small in every prior study could easily be underpowered, particularly when the amygdala signal itself was being extracted from voxels near each participant’s peak. The honest summary is that these studies fail to find amygdala-prefrontal group differences in directed models, which differs from showing that the differences are absent.

What the amygdala-centered tradition should concede is narrower than the headline in the 2026 abstract suggests, and what the newer tradition should concede is larger than its authors do. Social anxiety has been studied overwhelmingly with faces and anticipated evaluation, stimuli that were chosen because they drive the amygdala, and then described as an amygdala disorder. A model that includes cortical self-representation nodes and tasks that engage the self as an object of judgment finds cortical differences. The disorder is plausibly distributed across both and the field has been measuring the half that its tasks illuminated.

The unresolved question is one of direction and time. If the altered pre-supplementary motor to ventromedial coupling is compensatory, as Schrammen and colleagues propose, then it should appear in at-risk youth before symptoms peak, strengthen with regulatory demand, and normalize as cognitive behavioral therapy removes the need for it. If instead it is a core pathology, it should persist after successful treatment. The same logic applies to the posterior cingulate and parietal inputs to medial prefrontal cortex that Jamieson and colleagues identified, which the authors themselves suggest might track treatment response. Neither study is longitudinal, neither is interventional, and a cross-sectional directed model cannot distinguish a cause from a scar from a workaround. The trial that settles it would scan the same patients before and after therapy with both node sets in a single model, in a sample large enough that a null amygdala result means something. Until someone runs it, the field is choosing between a circuit it can see well and a circuit it can only now begin to model, and calling the choice a finding.
