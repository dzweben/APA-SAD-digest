The Social Brain Dispatch

The Amygdala Was Never One Thing

A consortium pooled 3,890 brains from ten countries to find the structural signature of social anxiety and came back with a putamen effect too small to see in any single patient and almost nothing in the amygdala. A year later a Northwestern laboratory scanned six people for hours apiece and found the brain’s social network wired into two specific amygdala nuclei and pointedly not into a third, which suggests the consortium’s near-null may say more about how coarsely the field has been looking than about what is there.

Edition No. 19 · Frontier Neuroscience · November 16, 2026

Every edition of this Dispatch that has read a social anxiety imaging paper has rested, somewhere underneath, on a measurement problem that the field has spent the last decade discovering it cannot solve by working harder in the way it used to. The classic clinical neuroimaging study, the kind this publication has walked through from Birbaumer’s 1998 amygdala paper onward, scans twenty or thirty patients and a matched control group, finds a difference, and reports it at an effect size that is almost always large, because only large effects survive at that sample size. In 2022 Scott Marek, Nico Dosenbach, and a large group of colleagues showed with the ABCD and UK Biobank cohorts that brain-behavior associations measured across individuals are typically tiny, correlations in the range of a few hundredths, and that the large effects reported in small studies are, in substantial part, the expected inflation of a field running underpowered designs against a noisy signal. That paper did not invent the problem so much as name it at a scale nobody could wave away, and it left the discipline with two exits that point in opposite directions. One exit says the answer is more people, thousands of them, pooled across sites and harmonized so that a small true effect can finally be estimated with honest precision. The other says the answer is more data per person, hours of scanning in a handful of individuals, so that each brain is mapped finely enough that the blur created by averaging across people never enters the picture at all. This edition reads one paper from each exit. Neither was designed with the other in mind, and only one of them is about social anxiety disorder at all, yet together they say something uncomfortable about what the amygdala, the structure this Dispatch has spent eighteen editions circling, actually is when one looks closely enough.

The stakes are concrete. If social anxiety disorder carries a structural signature in the brain, that signature would constrain every causal story told about the disorder, whether it is an inherited vulnerability visible before onset, a scar left by years of avoidance, or a marker that could someday stratify patients for treatment. If it carries no reliable signature at the resolution the field typically measures, then a large literature of small-sample volumetric findings, amygdala smaller in this study and larger in that one, has been reporting noise, and the theories built on those findings have been resting on air. And if the true signature lives at a finer grain than standard structural imaging can resolve, inside the amygdala rather than across it, then the field has been asking the right question of the right structure with an instrument that cannot answer it. The two papers read here map almost perfectly onto the second and third of those possibilities, and the honest conclusion is that nobody yet knows which one holds.

The consortium paper comes out of ENIGMA, and to read it properly one has to understand what ENIGMA is and why its existence changed the sociology of psychiatric neuroimaging. Paul Thompson founded the Enhancing Neuro Imaging Genetics through Meta-Analysis consortium in 2009, while he was at the Laboratory of Neuro Imaging at UCLA, with a genetic question in mind. Genome-wide association studies needed tens of thousands of participants, no single imaging group had them, and so Thompson and his collaborators proposed that groups around the world run identical, openly distributed processing scripts on their own scans and share only the derived measures. The first landmark result, Jason Stein, Sarah Medland, and colleagues’ 2012 Nature Genetics study of genetic variants affecting hippocampal volume, proved the model could work. What followed was less expected. The same infrastructure, once built, could answer clinical questions, and over the next decade ENIGMA working groups for schizophrenia, bipolar disorder, major depression, obsessive-compulsive disorder, post-traumatic stress disorder, and autism each published a standardized subcortical volume analysis using the same FreeSurfer segmentation of the same fourteen structures. Thompson, who moved the operation to the University of Southern California’s Stevens Neuroimaging and Informatics Institute in 2013, had in effect built the field’s first common ruler, and with it a way to compare effect sizes across disorders that no individual laboratory could have produced.

The ENIGMA-Anxiety Working Group came late to this enterprise, and its social anxiety paper, published in Molecular Psychiatry in 2023 by Nynke Groenewold, Janna Marie Bas-Hoogendam, and roughly a hundred co-authors under the senior direction of Dan Stein at the University of Cape Town and Nic van der Wee at Leiden University Medical Center, carries the imprint of both its leaders. Stein, who runs the South African Medical Research Council’s unit on risk and resilience in mental disorders, has for three decades been one of the most persistent advocates of a global, cross-national psychiatry of anxiety, and the paper’s inclusion of samples from Cape Town and Stellenbosch alongside Münster, Houston, and Chengdu is a direct expression of that commitment. Groenewold, a Dutch-trained neuroimager working in Cape Town, is the consortium analyst who makes such a thing run. The more intimate lineage, though, runs through Leiden. Bas-Hoogendam built her doctoral work around the Leiden Family Lab study on Social Anxiety Disorder, a design that scanned patients with social anxiety together with their unaffected relatives in an effort to find neurobiological endophenotypes, features that track genetic risk for the disorder rather than its current presence. Two of the earlier findings this paper set out to adjudicate are hers. A 2017 voxel-based morphometry mega-analysis she led, pooling 174 adult patients, had found larger gray matter in the right dorsal putamen in social anxiety, and her family study had found an enlarged left pallidum that tracked social anxiety symptoms within genetically enriched families. In a real sense the ENIGMA paper is a scholar submitting her own prior results to a test ten times larger than the one that produced them, and that is worth holding onto when one reads what came back.

The design is straightforward in principle and laborious in execution. The working group assembled T1-weighted scans from 37 samples in ten countries across five continents, 1,115 patients with social anxiety disorder and 2,775 healthy controls. Each site ran FreeSurfer under the ENIGMA protocol, segmenting the thalamus, hippocampus, amygdala, caudate, putamen, pallidum, and nucleus accumbens in each hemisphere, and visually inspecting every segmentation, with 97.7 percent of volumetric observations surviving quality control. Diagnosis was established by structured interview at every site, mostly the SCID or MINI for adults and the K-SADS or DAWBA for children, although two population cohorts, the Philadelphia Neurodevelopmental Cohort and the Study of Health in Pomerania, assessed only lifetime and never current disorder. The patients averaged 26.9 years of age, the controls 31.9, and the patients were 61.5 percent female against 55.2 percent for controls. Across samples, about 36 percent of patients had a lifetime comorbid anxiety disorder, 37.5 percent lifetime major depression, and 22 percent were taking psychotropic medication at the time of the scan. Where measured, mean Liebowitz Social Anxiety Scale scores sat around 71, solidly in the clinical range, and mean age of onset was about 15.

The analytic choice that matters is the word mega. A meta-analysis pools summary statistics, one effect size per site, and so inherits whatever covariate choices and processing quirks each site made. A mega-analysis pools individual participants, so the same model with the same covariates is fit to everyone at once, with scan site entered as a random effect. The ENIGMA-Anxiety group fit linear mixed models predicting each structure’s volume from diagnosis while adjusting for sex, age, age squared, their interactions, and total intracranial volume, and they tested whether letting the diagnostic effect vary by site, a random slope, improved fit over a random intercept alone. For nine of fourteen structures it did not, so the simpler model was used throughout. Effects are reported as mixed-effects d, a quantity scaled like Cohen’s d but corrected for the non-independence of participants nested within sites, and therefore, as the authors themselves note, slightly more conservative than a naive d. Significance required a family-wise corrected p below .05 across the fourteen regions, an uncorrected threshold of roughly .0036.

Here is what came back. In the full sample, patients with social anxiety disorder had smaller putamen volumes in both hemispheres, with a mixed-effects d of minus .077 on the left and minus .104 on the right. These are effects in the vicinity of a tenth of a standard deviation, so small that the patient and control distributions overlap almost completely, so small that no individual scan could ever be classified on this basis, and so small that only a sample of this size could have detected them at all. The left putamen result survived exclusion of patients with comorbid obsessive-compulsive or post-traumatic stress disorder but lost significance when the analysis was restricted to patients meeting current criteria. The amygdala, the structure at the center of nearly every neurobiological model of the disorder, showed nothing in the main analysis. Neither did the hippocampus.

The more interesting pattern emerged when the sample was cut by age. A significant negative interaction between diagnosis and age on left putamen volume, small at r of minus .034, motivated separate analyses in adolescents, defined as eight to twenty-one, and adults, twenty-two to sixty-nine. In 668 adult patients against 1,984 adult controls, the putamen was smaller bilaterally, d of minus .141 on the left and minus .158 on the right, and the pallidum was larger bilaterally, d of .129 on the left and .099 on the right. In 437 adolescent patients against 788 adolescent controls, there was nothing at all, no structure reaching even an uncorrected threshold. The combination of a smaller putamen and a larger pallidum, the authors point out, has not appeared in any other ENIGMA disorder. The generalized anxiety disorder group had found a trend toward larger putamen, the obsessive-compulsive group larger pallidum without putamen change, and the autism group smaller putamen and pallidum together. On ENIGMA’s common ruler, social anxiety disorder has a structural profile that is faint but, at least in its configuration, its own.

The clinical subgroup analyses add texture that the main result lacks. Patients with a comorbid anxiety disorder showed a smaller left amygdala, d of minus .145, and this is the only place in the entire paper the amygdala appears, which raises the possibility that much of the small-sample amygdala literature this Dispatch has read was, without knowing it, measuring comorbidity. Early-onset patients, those whose disorder began before thirteen, showed a smaller right hippocampus, d of minus .194, which grew to minus .326 when restricted to adults with early onset, a pattern the authors read as a possible consequence of longer illness duration. And the later-onset patients produced the largest effects in the paper, bilateral putamen reductions of about minus .34 and minus .31 and pallidum enlargements of about .24 and .20, roughly three times the size of the full-sample estimates. Medicated patients showed no corrected differences, and unmedicated patients showed the smaller right putamen, which argues against the putamen effect being a drug artifact. No subcortical volume correlated with symptom severity after correction.

Read critically, the paper is both a triumph of method and a document of its limits, and the authors are candid about most of them. Scans were acquired at 1.5 and 3 tesla with different sequences and different FreeSurfer versions, heterogeneity the random intercept absorbs only partly. The patient group was younger than the control group, which the age covariates address statistically but not by design. The severity analyses rested on 523 patients from 21 samples, a fraction of the whole, because sites had not measured the same things. And the design is cross-sectional, so the most tantalizing pattern in the paper, adults different and adolescents not, cannot distinguish between a structural change that accumulates over years of disorder, a structural signature that marks a later-onset form of illness, and a cohort difference between the kinds of adolescents and adults who end up in research samples. The finding that later-onset patients show the larger effects sits awkwardly with any simple developmental vulnerability story, since one would expect a trait present from childhood to show most strongly in those who fell ill earliest. What the paper cannot say, and what no FreeSurfer volume can say, is where inside these structures any difference lives. The putamen, the pallidum, and above all the amygdala enter the model as single numbers, one volume per structure per hemisphere, and that is a choice with consequences the second paper makes visible.

The second paper comes from the opposite end of the methodological spectrum and from a lineage that has been quietly dismantling one of the most famous constructs in cognitive neuroscience. Rodrigo Braga trained at Imperial College London on attention networks before spending a postdoctoral period in Randy Buckner’s laboratory at Harvard, and the significance of that apprenticeship is hard to overstate. Buckner, together with Marcus Raichle’s group at Washington University before him, did as much as anyone to establish the default network, the set of medial prefrontal, posterior cingulate, lateral parietal, and temporal regions that are active when a person is left alone with their thoughts and that Buckner, Jessica Andrews-Hanna, and Daniel Schacter’s 2008 review cast as a domain-general system for internally directed cognition, remembering, imagining, and thinking about other minds alike. Braga’s contribution, beginning with a 2017 Neuron paper with Buckner, was to scan a small number of individuals over many sessions and show that this single network, when estimated within a person rather than averaged across people, breaks into at least two parallel networks whose regions sit side by side and interdigitate across the cortex like the fingers of two clasped hands. Group averaging had smeared them together. A follow-up by Lauren DiNicola, Braga, and Buckner in 2020 then showed the two networks had dissociable functions, one, which they called DN-A, active when people construct imagined scenes and remember episodes, and the other, DN-B, active when people reason about another person’s beliefs or emotional pain. The default network’s supposed domain generality was, on this account, partly an artifact of looking at a blurred average of two specialized systems.

This style of work belongs to a movement often called precision functional mapping, associated also with Evan Gordon, Timothy Laumann, Nico Dosenbach, and the Midnight Scan Club at Washington University, and its premise is the inverse of ENIGMA’s. Where ENIGMA treats individual variability as noise to be averaged out across thousands, precision mapping treats it as signal, on the grounds that the functional layout of association cortex differs enough from person to person that any group average misplaces boundaries. Braga, now running his own laboratory in the Department of Neurology at Northwestern University’s Feinberg School of Medicine, has pushed that premise into territory where it becomes technically brutal, the medial temporal lobe. His earlier work at 7 tesla had already shown that DN-A contains a small region near the subiculum, a finding 3 tesla data had missed, and that set up the question this 2024 Science Advances paper answers. If DN-A talks to the posterior hippocampal formation, the memory and spatial machinery that makes scene construction possible, what does the social network DN-B talk to?

The paper’s first author, Donnisa Edmonds, worked with a team whose composition is itself an argument. Kendrick Kay of the University of Minnesota’s Center for Magnetic Resonance Research contributed data from the Natural Scenes Dataset, the extraordinary resource he and Emily Allen and colleagues built by scanning eight people at 7 tesla across thirty to forty sessions each, primarily to study visual recognition of tens of thousands of photographs but with resting-state runs collected along the way. Christina Zelano, a Northwestern neuroscientist best known for showing that the rhythm of breathing entrains oscillations in human limbic structures including the amygdala, brought expertise in the olfactory and medial temporal circuits that turn out to matter a great deal for the interpretation. A further co-author, Q. Yang, drew amygdala nuclei by hand. The resulting paper, titled The Human Social Cognitive Network Contains Multiple Regions Within the Amygdala, is on its face a basic science study of healthy adults and never mentions social anxiety in its results. It belongs in this edition because of what it implies about the structure ENIGMA measured as one number.

The core data are resting-state functional connectivity at 1.8 millimeter isotropic resolution in six Natural Scenes participants aged 23 to 27, after two of the original eight were excluded for head motion. Each contributed between six and thirty-five five-minute runs that survived a strict motion screen, about two hours of resting data on average before exclusions. For participants with enough data, runs were split into discovery, replication, and in some cases triplication sets, so that each finding could be checked against held-out data from the same brain. The team first defined DN-A and DN-B in each person by placing seeds in left dorsal prefrontal cortex and confirming the maps with a data-driven multi-session hierarchical Bayesian parcellation, and then, crucially, redefined both networks directly in the volume rather than on the cortical surface, because the surface representations that precision mapping usually relies on cannot see deep structures like the amygdala. To confirm that DN-B really is the social network in this framework, they turned to a second dataset of eight individuals scanned at 3 tesla at Northwestern, each of whom also performed false-belief and emotional-pain theory of mind tasks and a scene construction task. A mixed-effects analysis found a significant network by task interaction, with theory of mind activity falling inside DN-B and scene construction inside DN-A, and the crossover was individually significant in four of the eight participants.

Then came the discovery. Thresholded at a correlation of .2, the volumetric maps showed DN-B regions in the anterior medial temporal lobe that had never appeared in 3 tesla data, one near the entorhinal cortex and others inside the amygdala itself. At coronal slices around MNI y of minus 6 to minus 9, five of six participants showed DN-B regions in what appeared to be the basolateral complex, bilateral in four, with one participant requiring a lower threshold of .15 and one, S6, showing no distinct region at that location. Overlaid on individualized automated segmentations of amygdala nuclei and quantified with Dice coefficients, these regions fell most consistently within the accessory basal nucleus, extending into the basal nucleus, with a possibly separate region in ventral lateral nucleus. A second set of regions sat further forward and more medially, around y of minus 3 to minus 5, and here the team did something unusually careful. Because automated parcellations tend to underestimate the medial nucleus, they had Yang, the expert on the team, hand-draw it in each participant while blind to the network maps, using its position relative to the optic tract and septal anatomy. In every one of the six participants, a DN-B region overlapped or bordered the hand-drawn medial nucleus, bilaterally in three. Seeds placed in that medial region reproduced the full distributed DN-B network across the cortex. DN-A, by contrast, showed no consistent amygdala regions in anyone.

Equally important is what DN-B avoided. Neither in the individualized segmentations nor in a group-defined atlas did its regions overlap the central nucleus. That absence will land hard for readers of the last edition of this Dispatch. The central nucleus is the output station of Joseph LeDoux’s fear circuit, the nucleus whose projections to hypothalamus, periaqueductal gray, and brainstem orchestrate freezing, autonomic arousal, and potentiated startle. Edition eighteen argued that the field had quietly conflated two functions sharing one anatomical address, LeDoux’s first-person defensive response and Ralph Adolphs’s third-person reading of another person’s face. Edmonds and Braga’s data offer, at least in healthy resting brains, a candidate anatomical separation of exactly that kind. The network people use to think about other minds couples to the basolateral input nuclei and to the medial nucleus, and it does not couple to the nucleus that runs the defensive output.

The medial nucleus finding deserves its own moment because it reaches into comparative biology this Dispatch visited in its evolutionary edition. In rodents the medial amygdala receives input from the accessory olfactory system and projects heavily to the hypothalamus, and it is essential for the pheromone-guided social behaviors of mating, aggression, and parenting. Developmentally and embryologically it is a different kind of tissue from the basolateral complex, subpallial rather than pallial, early maturing, phylogenetically old. The basolateral complex, by contrast, expanded across mammalian evolution in step with the neocortex that feeds it. Edmonds and colleagues therefore report a social network that spans both the newest and among the oldest parts of the amygdala, and in some individuals they saw DN-B regions near the ventromedial hypothalamus, the primary hypothalamic target of the primate medial nucleus. They note too that their medial regions sit close to where Leslie Brothers and colleagues recorded neurons responding to socially meaningful video in macaques in 1990, the same year Brothers coined the phrase that gives this publication its name, the social brain.

The team took two further steps toward credibility. Because large draining veins near the amygdala can produce spurious BOLD correlations, they seeded those veins directly and showed their correlation patterns barely overlapped the amygdala regions of DN-B. And to ask whether any of this generalizes beyond six people, they examined publicly available group-level independent component maps from 4,181 UK Biobank participants. At the default threshold, the component resembling DN-B showed its familiar cortical regions. Lowered to z of 3, it additionally showed medial temporal regions at the basolateral and medial amygdala and subiculum, recapitulating the individual-level anatomy at the level of a population average.

The weaknesses are as instructive as the strengths. Six participants, all healthy, all in their twenties, recruited from a university community, is a sample from which no claim about variability across the population, let alone across psychopathology, can be made. The network boundaries depend on correlation thresholds the authors chose, and while they defend the choice by showing that high-correlation cores are non-overlapping, a reader should recognize that a threshold of .2 on resting correlations in a low signal region is a judgment call. Replication within individuals was good but imperfect, failing in one held-out set for participant S1. The amygdala has lower temporal signal to noise than cortex even at 7 tesla, and the authors acknowledge that correlation strength from amygdala seeds varied widely across people. Theory of mind was established only in the separate 3 tesla sample, so the claim that the amygdala regions belong to a social network rests on transitive inference, amygdala connects to DN-B in one set of brains, DN-B carries theory of mind in another. And resting-state connectivity is neither anatomical connectivity nor task engagement. It tells us these regions fluctuate together, which is evidence of a circuit but not a demonstration of what flows through it. The press coverage that greeted the paper, framing it as an explanation for why people overthink what they said at a party, went well beyond anything the data test, and the authors’ own speculation about network-targeted brain stimulation for anxiety is labeled clearly as a future direction.

Put the two papers side by side and the first thing to notice is that they converge on a demotion of the whole-amygdala story, from opposite directions. ENIGMA, with nearly four thousand brains, found that the amygdala as a single segmented structure does not differ in volume between people with and without social anxiety disorder, except in the subgroup carrying additional anxiety disorders. Edmonds and Braga, with six brains, found that the amygdala as a single structure is the wrong unit of analysis, because within it lie at least three functionally distinct territories, a basolateral input zone and a medial zone tied into the social cognitive network, and a central output zone tied to something else. A FreeSurfer volume of the whole amygdala sums tissue belonging to the social network with tissue belonging to the defensive output system, and any disorder that altered one in one direction and the other in another, or altered only a few cubic millimeters of medial nucleus, could vanish entirely in that sum. A null result at the level of the whole structure is consistent with the absence of any effect and equally consistent with an effect located beneath the resolution of the measurement, and the Edmonds paper gives that second possibility a specific anatomical shape it previously lacked.

The tension between them is deeper and more interesting than a simple call for higher resolution. Each method buys its strength with exactly the property the other lacks. ENIGMA can estimate an effect of a tenth of a standard deviation with confidence because it treats every brain as interchangeable after registration and segmentation, and that interchangeability is precisely what precision mapping argues is false for association cortex and its subcortical partners. Braga’s approach can find a medial nucleus region a few voxels across because it refuses to average across people, and that refusal is precisely what makes it unable to say anything about how a population of patients differs from a population of controls. The ENIGMA paper cannot tell whether its putamen effect of minus .1 is a small change in everyone or a large change in a minority, a question that matters enormously for any mechanistic interpretation. The Edmonds paper cannot tell whether the DN-B amygdala coupling it found looks any different in a socially anxious brain, or whether it is a fixed feature of human architecture that social anxiety leaves untouched. Each paper’s blind spot is the other’s design principle.

There is also a quieter disagreement about where to look. The structural signal ENIGMA actually found was in the putamen and pallidum, the dorsal striatum and its output, structures that sit outside both the amygdala-centered fear models and the default network framework. The authors connect it to the striatal account of behavioral inhibition developed by Amanda Guyer, Eric Nelson, and Daniel Pine, himself an author on this paper, in which adolescents with an inhibited temperament show heightened striatal responses when anticipating social reward and punishment, a sensitivity read as an intense motivation to avoid failure. Readers will recall edition seventeen, where a striatum that failed to update on a peer’s unexpected kindness turned out to matter more for socially anxious adolescents than any amplified threat response. A Braga-style precision map of the striatum would ask which cortical networks each striatal territory couples to, since the striatum is organized into loops that mirror cortical networks. Nobody has published such a map in socially anxious individuals. It is conceivable that ENIGMA’s putamen finding is the volumetric shadow of a change in the striatal territory belonging to a particular network, and equally conceivable that it reflects something unrelated to network organization at all, such as years of altered motor and habit learning in people who have rehearsed avoidance for decades. The adult-only pattern and the larger effects in later-onset illness are at least compatible with a structural consequence of living with the disorder rather than a cause of it.

What each reveals about the other, finally, is a choice the field has not yet admitted it has to make. The consortium model has delivered what it promised, honest estimates of small effects with properly sized confidence intervals, and the lesson of those estimates is that social anxiety disorder does not leave a large footprint on the coarse anatomy of subcortical structures. The precision model has delivered what it promised too, a map fine enough to reveal organization that averaging erased, and the lesson there is that the coarse anatomy was never the level at which the social brain is organized. Running ENIGMA at the resolution of amygdala nuclei would require segmentation tools that work reliably on heterogeneous clinical scans at 3 tesla, which the Edmonds paper itself suggests they do not, given that the authors needed hand-drawing to find the medial nucleus at 7 tesla. Running precision mapping in a thousand patients would require several hours of scanning per person, at a cost and a burden on anxious participants that no current funding model supports. The likely path forward lies somewhere between, perhaps dense sampling of a few dozen carefully characterized patients and controls, perhaps population cohorts with enough data per person to estimate individual networks, but neither paper tells us which.

That leaves the question this edition cannot answer and that, more than anything else currently on the table, would tell us whether the amygdala belongs at the center of social anxiety’s neurobiology at all. If one took twenty people with severe generalized social anxiety disorder, scanned each for several hours at 7 tesla, and mapped their DN-B networks into the amygdala the way Edmonds and Braga mapped six healthy volunteers, would the social cognitive network’s coupling to the medial and basolateral nuclei look different, perhaps reaching into the central nucleus where in healthy brains it does not, so that thinking about other minds became wired directly into the defensive output that thinking about other minds is supposed to leave alone? If it would, then the amygdala hypothesis survives, relocated from volume to wiring and from the whole structure to its nuclei, and ENIGMA’s null is explained. If it would not, then the field will have to take seriously the possibility that its founding image of social anxiety, a frightened amygdala overreacting to faces, has been a story told at the wrong scale about the wrong structure, and that the faint, strange, adult-only signal in the putamen and pallidum is where the real answer has been waiting.
