The Social Brain Dispatch

The Brain Looks the Same Until You Show It a Cartoon

In 2023 an international consortium pooled 3,890 brains from 37 samples and found that social anxiety barely changes the size of anything, a putamen smaller by about a tenth of a standard deviation and an amygdala that stays quiet across most of the data. Three years later a Dutch team sat 86 people in a scanner with a six-minute cartoon about a stork and a cloud and found the difference living in how far each anxious brain’s reading of the story drifts from every other anxious brain’s reading.

Edition No. 19 · Frontier Neuroscience · October 15, 2026

For a quarter century the neuroimaging of social anxiety has run on a promise that rarely gets said aloud. The promise is that the disorder has an address. Somewhere in the brain, in a structure or a circuit or a coupling between the two, the socially anxious differ from everyone else, and a sufficiently careful scanner will eventually find the place. This Dispatch has followed that promise from Birbaumer’s amygdala in 1998 through eighteen editions of circuits, connectivity maps, pattern classifiers and treatment trials, and the honest summary is a field that keeps relocating its own address. The amygdala is in, then out. Prefrontal coupling is disrupted, then turns out to be intact the moment a patient is told to use it. Edition 5 read a multicenter resting-state study under the title The Study That Lost the Amygdala, and the title has aged well.

The stakes of the relocation are practical. Every proposed biomarker, every neuromodulation target and every connectivity-based classifier assumes that patients share a lesion, or at least share a direction of departure from the healthy mean. If they do not, if each socially anxious brain departs from the norm in its own idiosyncratic way, then the group average that the field has been mapping since the 1990s describes a patient who does not exist. Two papers published three years apart, built on opposite philosophies of what a brain study is for, between them put that possibility on the table. One is the largest structural dataset anyone has assembled on the disorder. The other is a small naturalistic experiment in which the stimulus is a Pixar short film. Read together they ask whether social anxiety is a place or a style, and whether the field has been measuring the wrong one.

The structural paper belongs to the ENIGMA consortium, and the consortium is the story. ENIGMA grew out of Paul Thompson’s Imaging Genetics Center at the University of California Los Angeles and later the University of Southern California, where Thompson and Neda Jahanshad, who also appears on this byline, confronted a problem that genetics had already solved and imaging had not. Genome-wide association needs tens of thousands of participants because every common variant carries a sliver of effect, and no single scanner site could supply them. Their answer was federation. Each site runs one shared segmentation and quality-control protocol on its own data, and the pooled output behaves as though a single enormous study had been run on a single machine. The early triumphs were genetic, notably the hippocampal volume and intracranial volume discoveries, and the model then spread to disease working groups for schizophrenia, depression, bipolar disorder, obsessive-compulsive disorder, PTSD, autism and, eventually, the anxiety disorders.

The anxiety working group is the project of a transcontinental clinical-neuroscience network. The authors who open and close the byline, Nynke Groenewold and Nic van der Wee, together with Dan Stein, Daniel Pine, Paul Thompson and the consortium’s long list of site investigators, form a roster in which readers of this Dispatch will recognize some old acquaintances. Stein led the World Mental Health epidemiology we read in Edition 8, the paper that gave social anxiety its cross-national prevalence curve. Pine, of the National Institute of Mental Health, sat on the adolescent-circuitry papers of Edition 4 and wrote the longitudinal study of Edition 9 showing that childhood anxiety forecasts adult anxiety and depression. Janna Marie Bas-Hoogendam, second on the author list, contributed a Leiden sample recruited from multigenerational families genetically enriched for social anxiety. The pattern is of a field that spent decades producing underpowered studies in separate buildings and then, in a collective act of institutional humility, decided to stop. Groenewold et al. (2023) is the output of that decision.

Before the numbers, the concept of a mega-analysis deserves a sentence of precision, because it is easy to confuse with the meta-analyses this Dispatch has met since its first edition. A meta-analysis combines summary statistics, or in the coordinate-based case the peak locations, that each study chose to publish. A mega-analysis pools the participant-level measurements themselves, processed through one pipeline, and models the site as a random effect. The difference matters because the coordinate meta-analyses of social anxiety had sorted themselves into camps that could not be reconciled. A mega-analysis of 174 patients had found a larger right dorsal putamen, while a coordinate meta-analysis had reported a smaller left ventral putamen, and the smaller studies had variously found small amygdalae, large amygdalae, and no amygdala difference at all. Groenewold and colleagues set out to settle the matter with a sample that could not be dismissed as a fluke of recruitment.

The sample is 1,115 patients and 2,775 controls from 37 samples in ten countries across five continents, with segmentations of seven structures per hemisphere (thalamus, amygdala, hippocampus, putamen, pallidum, nucleus accumbens and caudate) produced by FreeSurfer under the ENIGMA protocol and visually inspected for failures. About 97.7 percent of volumetric observations survived quality control. Social anxiety disorder was established by structured diagnostic interview, with the SCID, MINI, CIDI and DAWBA all appearing across sites and a handful of samples using other procedures. Current diagnosis was assessed in 35 of the 37 samples, and only 2.5 percent of patients met criteria for lifetime but not current disorder. Patients with schizophrenia, bipolar disorder or autism were excluded. The models were linear mixed-effects regressions with a random intercept for scan site, adjusted for sex, age, age squared, their interactions and total intracranial volume, with family-wise error correction across the fourteen regions and effect sizes expressed as mixed-effects d. The sample spans childhood to late middle age, and the adolescent contingent includes the Philadelphia Neurodevelopmental Cohort with its 155 patients and 398 controls, which is why the paper could offer the first large mega-analysis of adolescents as their own stratum.

The headline is small, and the authors say so. Across the full sample, patients had smaller bilateral putamen volumes, with d of minus 0.077 on the left (corrected p = .037) and minus 0.104 on the right (corrected p = .001), and a significant diagnosis-by-age interaction on the left (r = minus 0.034, corrected p = .045). The age structure explains why. In adults the putamen reduction was larger, d of minus 0.141 on the left and minus 0.158 on the right, and was accompanied by larger pallidum volumes (left d = 0.129, right d = 0.099). In adolescents with social anxiety there was nothing, no difference in any of the fourteen regions after correction. The amygdala, the structure to which this Dispatch devoted its entire eighteenth edition, produced a signal only in a subgroup, a smaller left amygdala (d = minus 0.145, corrected p = .017) in patients with a comorbid anxiety disorder, which is to say a finding that cannot be attributed to social anxiety alone.

The subgroup analyses are where the paper becomes interesting rather than merely sober. Patients whose disorder began before age 13 showed a smaller right hippocampus (d = minus 0.194, corrected p = .009), and the effect strengthened to minus 0.326 within the adult early-onset group. Patients with later onset showed the largest effects in the entire paper, bilateral putamen reductions of minus 0.336 and minus 0.309, a smaller left nucleus accumbens (minus 0.171) and bilateral pallidum enlargements near 0.2. Patients on psychotropic medication at the time of scan showed no corrected differences at all, while medication-free patients retained the right putamen effect, a pattern that fits either a normalizing drug effect or a selection of milder cases into treatment and cannot distinguish between them. And none of it correlated with severity. In the 523 patients who had completed the Liebowitz Social Anxiety Scale, no subcortical volume survived correction, a null the authors rightly attribute to modest power but which remains a null.

Read critically, the paper offers a lesson about what a very large sample can and cannot buy. The effect sizes, in the neighborhood of a tenth of a standard deviation, are comparable to the hippocampal effects ENIGMA reported for obsessive-compulsive disorder, PTSD and depression, and a fraction of the minus 0.46 seen in schizophrenia. A true effect of that size is invisible in any study of fewer than a thousand people, which is the best available explanation for the contradictory literature the authors set out to resolve. But an effect of that size also cannot serve as a diagnostic marker, since the distributions of patients and controls overlap by well over ninety percent. The authors are alert to the limits that matter. Everything is cross-sectional, so the age interaction compares different people at different ages and cannot show that putamen volume declines within a person. Harmonized protocols did not erase differences in field strength, sequence and software version. The left putamen effect disappears when the analysis is restricted to current cases. And a volumetric measure, however precisely estimated, is silent about what the tissue does.

The second paper comes from Nijmegen, from a group whose institutional pedigree could hardly be more different. Saskia Koch, Margot Mangnus, Robin Devillers, Peter Hagoort, Jana Bašnáková and Arjen Stolk work at the Donders Institute at Radboud University, and the study was funded through the Language in Interaction consortium, a Dutch Gravitation program in which Hagoort, one of the field’s leading figures in the neurobiology of language, is a central figure. Stolk, the senior author, built much of his reputation on a question that sounds distant from anxiety and is actually adjacent to it: how two people who share no code manage to establish a new communicative signal and understand each other. Koch holds a European Research Council starting grant, titled SocialDynamics, that funds the continuation of exactly this line. The lab’s immediately previous paper, Mangnus et al. (2024), put autistic adults in a scanner with the same film and found preserved spontaneous mentalizing alongside increased similarity between viewers’ brains. The social anxiety study is the sequel, and it was built from the start as a comparison, since a sibling condition that shares some surface features with autism, social avoidance and awkward turn-taking among them, deserves a brain-level account of how it differs.

Their method needs two concepts. The first is the film. Partly Cloudy is a Pixar short of five minutes and forty-five seconds in which a stork who delivers difficult babies and a cloud who creates them move through cooperation, injury, rejection and reconciliation, without a word of dialogue. Rebecca Saxe’s laboratory validated it, in Jacoby et al. (2016), as a localizer for the theory-of-mind network, because specific scenes in which the viewer must infer a character’s thoughts reliably recruit the temporoparietal junction, the precuneus and the medial prefrontal cortex, while scenes of physical pain recruit a different set of regions. Koch et al. (2026) annotated four mental scenes totaling 44 seconds, seven pain scenes totaling 26 seconds, and three neutral scenes totaling 24 seconds, so that a mental-versus-pain contrast holds affective salience roughly constant while varying the demand to mentalize. The second concept is intersubject correlation, introduced by Hasson et al. (2004) in a study where people watched a Clint Eastwood western. If many brains watch the same stimulus, then the portion of each brain’s signal that tracks the other brains is stimulus-driven, and the remainder is the viewer’s own contribution. Reduced alignment indexes more idiosyncratic processing. Finn et al. (2018) showed that trait paranoia reduces alignment on an ambiguous narrative, and Camacho et al. (2024) reported higher intersubject variability in youth with higher social anxiety, which supplies the premise Koch and colleagues set out to test in adults.

The sample was 86 adults recruited through the Radboud research database and campus postings, half of them classified as socially anxious by a Liebowitz Social Anxiety Scale total of 30 or more and half below it. Mean scores were 51.42 (SD = 18.58) in the elevated group and 15.23 (SD = 7.57) in the controls. The groups, 43 each, were matched on sex, age (about 26 years), and verbal and nonverbal IQ, and Bayes factors favored the null of no difference on each of those. Participants taking psychotropic medication were excluded, and a mock scanner session was used in advance to reduce state anxiety. They watched the film once during multiband multiecho fMRI at 3 tesla (TR 1.5 s, 2.5 mm isotropic voxels) while heart rate and pupil diameter were recorded, and afterwards, unannounced, they wrote a plot summary. The design is deliberately low in evaluative threat. Nobody is watched, judged or asked to perform, which means that anything that distinguishes the groups cannot be attributed to the anticipation of being scrutinized.

The physiology came back empty, and the emptiness is the foundation of the paper. Mean heart rate was 65.45 against 64.11 beats per minute (Bayes factor for the null of about 3.5), the heart rates aligned across viewers equally in both groups, pupil dilation tracked event type strongly (largest for pain, then mental, then neutral scenes) with no main effect of group and no interaction (null Bayes factors of 6.2 and 6.7), and pupil alignment across viewers did not differ. Whatever the fMRI would show, then, was unlikely to be arousal or lapsing attention in disguise. The activation analysis found the canonical mentalizing network engaged across everyone, with peaks at the right temporoparietal junction (46, minus 52, 22), precuneus and medial prefrontal cortex, and a single focal group difference. The elevated-anxiety group showed reduced activation in the left posterior superior temporal sulcus during mental scenes, with a peak near (minus 44, minus 62, 18) for mental versus neutral scenes (t = 4.41, corrected p = .003), and the same region emerged for mental versus pain.

The alignment analysis is where the study justifies its place on this beat. Computing dynamic intersubject correlation for each participant against the leave-one-out average of their own group in thirty-second sliding windows, and testing the groups with cluster-based permutation, the authors found three spatiotemporal clusters. In the elevated-anxiety group alignment was reduced across the first two-thirds of the film in superior parietal and bilateral temporal cortex (cluster p = .003), and again in the final third in supplementary motor, precentral and medial prefrontal cortex (p = .02, medial prefrontal peak t = 6.77 at about minus 6, 50, 8). Alignment was increased in occipital, inferior frontal and superior temporal cortex across most of the film (p = .002). Early sensory cortex therefore behaved more uniformly among the anxious viewers, and association cortex more individually. No cluster overlapped the mentalizing network by more than 20 percent, and the alignment values showed no relation to posterior superior temporal sulcus activation (all p above .20), so the two findings are statistically independent. The authors read the pattern through Mesulam’s (1998) hierarchy from sensation to cognition as a redistribution, in which the anxious brain tracks the stimulus more tightly at the bottom and more idiosyncratically at the top.

Two further results complicate the story in productive ways. When the authors compared cluster 1 with the cluster that Mangnus et al. found for autism in the same film, the spatial overlap was roughly 60 percent, across supramarginal, inferior temporal, midcingulate and precuneus cortex, and the direction was reversed. Autistic viewers were more alike each other than controls, and socially anxious viewers were less alike each other, in nearly the same tissue. The groups were comparable in social anxiety severity, which sharpens the implication: two populations who look similar on a questionnaire can reach that similarity by opposite neural routes. And the plot summaries moved against the hypothesis. The socially anxious participants did not differ in mental-state vocabulary (proportions of .053 and .048), yet used a smaller share of negative emotion words (.65 against .85, p = .012). The authors suggest impression management in an offline explicit task, the film’s happy ending, or a context in which negativity bias requires the viewer to be a participant rather than an audience.

A critical reading finds real strengths and a few soft places. The strengths are the null-supported physiology, the Bayesian treatment of absence, the matched groups, the open data and scripts, and above all a design whose stimulus requires nothing of the participant. The soft places are specific. The claim that the mentalizing network is preserved rests on null Bayes factors of 4.08 for the right temporoparietal junction but only 1.22 for the precuneus and 1.42 for the medial prefrontal cortex, which are anecdotal evidence at best, so preservation is the safest reading rather than the established one. The posterior superior temporal sulcus difference is a single cluster from a whole-brain search in 86 people, with no replication yet, and the paper’s headline mentalizing result therefore rests on one region. Because each person’s alignment is computed against their own group’s average, reduced alignment in the anxious group means the anxious viewers resemble each other less than the controls resemble each other, which is a statement about dispersion and reveals nothing about direction. The cross-study autism comparison pairs different samples, with the overlap computed descriptively. And the participants are young volunteers who scored above a questionnaire threshold, with no diagnostic interview and no data on onset or comorbidity, a limitation the authors acknowledge.

Set the two papers side by side and the first thing to notice is that they agree on something neither was designed to test, namely that the amygdala-centered, threat-centered picture of social anxiety is a poor summary of where the signal sits. ENIGMA finds the strongest and most replicable structural effect in the putamen and pallidum, regions of the striatum that Edition 17 met in a different guise, when Jarcho and colleagues described a striatum that could not update on a peer’s unexpected kindness. Koch and colleagues find their effect under conditions designed to contain no threat, with flat heart rates and flat pupils, in the superior temporal and association cortex that read other people’s minds and in the individual manner in which brains interpret a shared story. Taken together they describe a disorder that is at least as much about how the world is weighted and learned as about how danger is detected.

The tension between them is more instructive than the convergence. ENIGMA treats heterogeneity as the enemy. The reason the literature was inconsistent, the authors argue, is that age, comorbidity, onset and medication scramble the signal, and their remedy is to stratify and subtract until a clean group-mean difference emerges. Koch et al. treat heterogeneity as the finding. In an intersubject correlation design, dispersion across brains is the dependent variable, and reduced alignment among anxious viewers means that the disorder expresses itself as a divergence between patients. Put the two logics together and the small mean effect in ENIGMA becomes predictable. If each patient departs from the normative brain in an individual direction, with the dispersion greatest in precisely the association systems that Mesulam placed at the top of the hierarchy, then averaging 1,115 of them yields a shift near zero, a tenth of a standard deviation, on a structure that happens to be shared. The statistic ENIGMA reports is the correct statistic for a disorder that is homogeneous and a conservative one for a disorder that is not.

Each paper also exposes the other’s blind spot. ENIGMA cannot see function. A volume is a static summary, and a null in the amygdala across 3,890 brains says nothing about what the amygdala does when a face appears, which is why the volumetric answer to the amygdala question never settles the functional one. Its design also cannot distinguish a vulnerability from a scar. The adult-only putamen effect, the larger effect in later-onset cases and the missing adolescent effect are all compatible with structural change accumulating over years of altered learning and avoidance, and a cross-sectional interaction cannot say which. The Nijmegen study cannot see the clinic. Its participants are dimensional, university-recruited, medication-free and mostly mildly to moderately symptomatic, and its central measure cannot say whether idiosyncrasy is a feature of the disorder, a response to years of social vigilance, or a trait of people who were always inclined to read scenes their own way. The one place the papers touch is revealing. Camacho et al. (2024), as Koch et al. cite them, found elevated intersubject variability in youth, an age at which Groenewold et al. find no volumetric difference at all. If both observations hold, idiosyncratic processing may arrive first and structural change later, a sequence a longitudinal dataset could test directly and that no existing dataset has.

That sequencing question matters, and a larger one sits inside Koch et al.’s own results. Reduced alignment reports that anxious brains diverge from each other and is silent on where they go. The theoretical models that organize this field, laid out in Edition 15, predict that the divergence runs toward threat, a shared negative self-image distorting each person’s reading of the same scene. The one behavioral measure in the study, the plot summaries, points the other way, and the authors’ impression-management explanation could be right or could be an attempt to rescue a hypothesis. If the direction of drift is toward threat, then idiosyncrasy is the neural face of an interpretive bias the cognitive models already describe, and the field has a mechanism. If the drift has no consistent direction, then something stranger is going on, and the biomarkers, targets and treatments built on the average anxious brain are aiming at a patient whose defining feature is that no two of them look alike. Which of those is true, and whether each person’s particular drift persists across films, across months, and across the years between adolescence and the adult putamen, is the experiment the field has not yet run.
