The Social Brain Dispatch

The Average Brain Nobody Has

In 2023 a consortium pooled 3,890 scans from thirty-seven sites and found that social anxiety shrinks the putamen by about a tenth of a standard deviation, an effect so small it could describe almost no one in particular. In 2025 a Tübingen team scanned 120 adults at rest and found that a history of childhood emotional maltreatment flips the sign of the disorder’s connectivity signature, which means the consortium may have averaged two opposite brains into one nearly empty one.

Edition No. 19 · Frontier Neuroscience · October 12, 2026

There is an embarrassment at the center of social anxiety neuroimaging that the field discusses mostly in the passive voice. Three decades of scanning have produced a literature in which the amygdala is too active, or not too active, or too coupled to prefrontal cortex, or too decoupled from it, in which the default network is hyperconnected in one sample and hypoconnected in the next, and in which every review concludes that the findings are “inconsistent” and that larger, better harmonized samples will settle matters. This Dispatch has watched that sentence recur for eighteen editions. The multicenter resting-state study of Langhammer and colleagues in Edition 5 found small and diffuse differences even with a carefully coordinated German consortium, and the circuit papers of Editions 10 and 16 kept disagreeing about which direction the amygdala and its prefrontal partners were broken in. The standard diagnosis of this failure is noise. Underpowered studies, sloppy regions of interest, medicated patients, comorbid depression. The standard prescription is scale. This edition reads the two most instructive frontier responses to that prescription, and they turn out to diagnose the failure differently. One says the signal is real but tiny and lives in a place the field was not looking, in the striatum rather than the amygdala, and that it depends on how old you are and when your disorder began. The other says the signal is large but its sign depends on something almost no one measured, whether the patient was emotionally mistreated as a child. If the second account is even partly right, then the first is a heroic and rigorous measurement of an average that corresponds to no actual brain.

The first paper belongs to the ENIGMA-Anxiety Working Group, and to understand why its design looks the way it does you have to understand what ENIGMA is. The Enhancing Neuro Imaging Genetics through Meta-Analysis consortium grew out of Paul Thompson’s group at the University of Southern California, whose Imaging Genetics Center set out in 2009 to answer a problem from the genetics side of neuroscience. Variants that influence brain structure have effects so small that no single laboratory’s sample can see them, and so the only honest strategy was to pool hundreds of laboratories under one pipeline. The consortium’s founding insight was procedural. Each site keeps its own raw data and runs the same FreeSurfer segmentation, the same visual quality control, and the same statistical script, so that the pooled result inherits the power of thousands of subjects without anyone having to argue about how a hippocampus gets traced. Working groups for schizophrenia, bipolar disorder, major depression, OCD, and PTSD followed, and their results established a sobering and by now familiar pattern, which is that psychiatric disorders differ from health in subcortical and cortical structure by effects in the range of one tenth to one half of a standard deviation, with schizophrenia at the high end. The anxiety group was assembled to bring that discipline to a literature that badly needed it. Its first author on this paper, Nynke Groenewold, worked alongside Dan Stein’s South African group, which has been one of the field’s anchoring forces in anxiety neurobiology. Its second author, Janna Marie Bas-Hoogendam, comes out of the Leiden Family Lab on Social Anxiety Disorder, a program built around the idea that the disorder should be studied in multigenerational pedigrees where it runs in families, and its last author is Nic van der Wee, the Leiden psychiatrist whose department supplied the clinical backbone of that program. The senior bench behind them includes Thompson himself and Daniel Pine of the National Institute of Mental Health, whose 1998 longitudinal work on adolescent anxiety readers met in Edition 9. The paper is therefore the output of two lineages that rarely meet, a family-genetic tradition that treats the disorder as a heritable trait with a developmental course and a consortium tradition that treats the brain as a very large sample from which the weak signals of disease can be extracted by brute coordination.

The design deserves exact description because its choices carry its conclusions. Groenewold and colleagues (2023) assembled structural scans from 37 samples drawn from ten countries on five continents, totaling 1,115 people with social anxiety disorder and 2,775 healthy controls. Diagnosis rested on structured interviews in every sample, lifetime or current, and only two samples did not assess current status, which meant that across the other 35 just 2.5 percent of patients met lifetime but not current criteria. Patients with schizophrenia spectrum disorder, bipolar disorder, or autism were excluded, and controls were free of lifetime major psychiatric diagnoses and, where known, of psychotropic medication. Scans came at 1.5 or 3 tesla and were segmented with FreeSurfer into fourteen subcortical volumes, thalamus, amygdala, hippocampus, putamen, pallidum, accumbens, and caudate in each hemisphere, then checked by eye, with 97.7 percent of observations surviving quality control. The statistical model was a linear mixed-effects regression with a random intercept for scan site and covariates for sex, age, age squared, their interactions, and total intracranial volume, and the authors tested whether adding a random slope for diagnosis by site improved fit and found, for nine of the fourteen regions, that it did not, which licensed the simpler model. Effects are reported as mixed-effects d, a Cohen’s d analogue corrected for the clustering of subjects within sites, and significance was set at a family-wise error threshold across fourteen regions, corresponding to an uncorrected p of 0.00357. Every one of those choices is defensible and consequential. The harmonized pipeline is why the result can be trusted as a measurement. The restriction to variables every site collected is why the result cannot say anything about childhood adversity, which only some sites measured and none measured in a common way.

What the pooled sample showed was a small and specific thing. Across all participants, people with social anxiety disorder had smaller bilateral putamen volume, with a left effect of d = −0.077 and a right effect of d = −0.104. No amygdala difference survived correction and no hippocampal difference did either. The left putamen finding was robust to excluding patients with comorbid OCD or PTSD, with effect sizes between −0.074 and −0.094, but when the comparison was restricted to patients who currently met criteria it fell to d = −0.074 with a corrected p of 0.156, a reminder of how thin these margins are. The first interesting structure appears when age enters. A diagnosis-by-age interaction was significant for the left putamen (r = −0.034), and splitting the sample showed why. In adults, aged 22 to 69, patients had smaller putamen (left d = −0.141, right d = −0.158) and larger pallidum (left d = 0.129, right d = 0.099) than controls. In children and adolescents, aged 8 to 21, there was nothing, no difference in any of the fourteen regions. The second structure appears when clinical history enters. Patients with early onset, defined by the sample median of before age 13, showed a smaller right hippocampus (d = −0.194), a difference that sharpened to d = −0.326 among adults with early onset. Patients with later onset showed no hippocampal effect at all but the largest putamen deficits in the paper (left d = −0.336, right d = −0.309), a smaller left accumbens, and a larger pallidum. Patients with a comorbid anxiety disorder showed a smaller left amygdala (d = −0.145), while those without comorbidity showed the putamen pattern, and the effect of medication was simply that medicated patients showed nothing and unmedicated patients showed the right putamen deficit (d = −0.100), a pattern the authors rightly treat as possible selection bias given how few patients were medicated. Finally, no volume tracked symptom severity on the Liebowitz scale in the 523 patients who had it, nor trait anxiety, nor depressive symptoms.

That last null deserves emphasis before any story is built. A structural signature that does not scale with how socially anxious a person is cannot be the structural substrate of social anxiety in any straightforward sense. It may be a scar, or a precursor, or a correlate of treatment history, or a marker of a subgroup. The authors read the age pattern as a possible progression, volumes that diverge from normal only after years of illness, and they are candid that cross-sectional pooling cannot distinguish progression from cohort differences or from the survival of one kind of patient into adulthood. Their discussion also does something unusual for a consortium paper and places the effect in comparative context. The pairing of smaller putamen with larger pallidum has not appeared in other ENIGMA disorder groups, which sets social anxiety apart from the enlarged putamen reported by the group for generalized anxiety and from the larger pallidum without putamen change in OCD, and the magnitude of d near 0.1 matches the order of the hippocampal effects in depression and PTSD at roughly 0.15, against 0.46 in schizophrenia. This is a reasonable place to ration the interpretation of the putamen result. A striatal finding invites a reward-and-habit narrative, an inhibited temperament that processes social incentives abnormally, and the authors gesture at exactly that, citing work on striatal activity in behaviorally inhibited adolescents. But a volume difference of a tenth of a standard deviation in a cross-sectional sample is a hypothesis generator for that narrative and nothing more.

The second paper arrives from a small clinical group in southwestern Germany and asks a question the first could not. Melina Leypoldt, Ariane Wiegand, Matthias Munk, Sanja Drohm, Andreas Fallgatter, Vanessa Nieratschker, and Benjamin Kreifelts (2025) work in the Department of Psychiatry and Psychotherapy at the University of Tübingen, a department Fallgatter heads and one with a long reputation in functional imaging of emotion. Kreifelts, the corresponding author, built his earlier career in the Tübingen emotional-perception tradition around Dirk Wildgruber, studying how the brain integrates voices and faces, which helps explain why this paper pays unusual attention to visual cortex. Nieratschker works on epigenetics, which gives the group a standing interest in how early experience gets written into later biology, and Wiegand, who is now also affiliated with the Max Planck Institute of Psychiatry’s fellow group for precision psychiatry, carried out the earlier task-fMRI study from this same sample that reported something odd and is the seed of this paper. In that study, patients with social anxiety and low adversity showed increased activation of the left dorsolateral prefrontal cortex during fear-related stimuli, while patients with high adversity did not. A pattern like that implies that whatever the field had been calling the neural correlate of social anxiety was in part a correlate of who had been mistreated as a child. The 2025 paper asks whether that interaction also lives in the resting brain. It is worth noticing that Kreifelts also appears in the author list of the ENIGMA paper, and that the same German Research Foundation grant, KR 4398/5-1, is acknowledged in both. The two papers share an investigator and a funding line, and they differ only in what they chose to measure.

The sample was 120 German-speaking adults of European descent, aged 19 to 48, diagnosed with the structured clinical interview for DSM-IV, 49 of them with social anxiety disorder. Adversity was measured with the Childhood Trauma Questionnaire, and a participant counted as high-adversity if any of its five subscales crossed the published severity cutoff, 15 for emotional neglect, 13 for emotional abuse, 10 for physical abuse or neglect, or 8 for sexual abuse. That crossing of an any-of-five rule produced four groups, 49 low-adversity controls, 22 high-adversity controls, 30 low-adversity patients, and 19 high-adversity patients. Everyone completed ten minutes of eyes-closed resting-state multiband fMRI on a 3 tesla Prisma, 405 volumes at a repetition time of 1.5 seconds with 2 mm isotropic voxels, preprocessed in SPM12 and CONN with motion scrubbing at 0.9 mm framewise displacement, aCompCor-style denoising of white matter and CSF, and band-pass filtering between 0.008 and 0.09 Hz. The central statistical object was the interaction contrast, the difference between adversity effects in controls and adversity effects in patients, tested in an ANOVA with age and gender as covariates. The authors ran three analyses in a deliberate cascade. A literature-derived ROI-to-ROI analysis among fourteen regions with replicated connectivity alterations in either condition found no interaction at all. A seed-based analysis from sixteen seeds, including the left dorsolateral prefrontal cluster from the earlier task study, found a rich interaction only for that task-derived seed, ten clusters spread across visual, sensorimotor, and inferior parietal cortex, while the amygdala seeds yielded nothing even with small-volume correction. And a whole-brain connectivity multivariate pattern analysis, which compresses each voxel’s connectivity profile to the rest of the brain into four principal components and tests those for the interaction, found three clusters: left calcarine fissure extending into precuneus (MNI −26, −60, 12; 70 voxels), subgenual anterior cingulate cortex (6, 10, −12; 65 voxels), and left middle frontal gyrus (−34, 8, 36; 42 voxels), each with a cluster-level FDR p of 0.016 or below.

Using those three clusters as seeds, the authors mapped where their connectivity showed the interaction and took the conjunction of the three maps, which yielded a network of 36 regions spanning visual cortex, pre- and postcentral cortex, temporal and inferior parietal cortex, and, tellingly, hippocampus, thalamus, and bilateral caudate. Within it they found five interaction patterns, and the common thread is the headline. In every one of them the effect of adversity ran in opposite directions in patients and controls. The subgenual cingulate was positively coupled to much of the network in low-adversity controls and became negatively coupled in high-adversity controls, while patients with high adversity showed positive coupling, so that high-adversity patients were hyperconnected relative to high-adversity controls and indistinguishable from low-adversity controls in most pairs. The calcarine and precuneus node went the other way, with high-adversity patients showing near-absent coupling, which the authors call a disconnection of visual cortex. Another observation they flag with caution is that high-adversity controls resembled low-adversity patients, as though a history of mistreatment moved a healthy brain toward the pattern the disorder produces without adversity. All 36 connections kept their interaction after controlling for state and trait anxiety and for depressive symptoms, with F(1,116) of at least 14.2, so the effect is not a depression artifact. And when adversity was treated as a continuous severity, the association of connectivity with CTQ score differed between patients and controls in every one of the network’s connections, with the effect carried almost entirely by emotional abuse (29 of 36 connections) and emotional neglect (29 of 36), less by physical neglect (21), and essentially not at all by physical abuse (0) or sexual abuse (1).

The paper is more cautious than its framing sounds, and it should be read with the same caution and a little more. The interaction results from the MVPA are followed by seed-based tests and then by t-tests on values extracted from clusters that were selected for showing the interaction, which means the shape of the five patterns, particularly the crossover forms, is partly an artifact of selection. The authors are quick to emphasize that the opposing signs are consistent across the network, but a crossover interaction is exactly what a selection procedure applied to noisy cell means will produce, and a cell of 19 people is a thin foundation for a pattern one hopes to carry to other samples. The any-of-five dichotomization of adversity throws away exposure severity and timing, and the retrospective questionnaire is vulnerable to the recall biases that anxious and depressed people are known to show. The sample is young, mostly female by the authors’ own admission, and was scanned without physiological monitoring in a population whose resting heart rate variability is known to differ. The ROI analysis returning nothing is itself informative, since it implies that a conventional region-of-interest strategy would have missed the effect entirely, a point the authors turn into a recommendation that researchers publish the masks of their significant clusters. And the whole inference rests on an interaction, which is the most underpowered kind of statistical test there is.

Set the two papers side by side and the first thing to see is that they agree about the cause of the field’s inconsistency while disagreeing sharply about its location. Groenewold and colleagues argue that heterogeneity in age and clinical history explains why earlier studies, which pooled adolescents with adults and early-onset with late-onset patients, found contradictory structure. Leypoldt and colleagues argue that heterogeneity in adversity explains why earlier connectivity studies found contradictory function. Each paper offers a variable that, once conditioned on, makes a null or a contradiction intelligible, and neither paper conditions on the other’s variable. That is where the pairing begins to teach. The ENIGMA design cannot see adversity because its scope was fixed by the lowest common denominator across 37 samples. The Tübingen design cannot see onset or age because its sample is narrow, 19 to 48 years, and nearly all of its participants were presumably well past the developmental window in which the ENIGMA adolescents showed nothing.

Then consider what each result would mean if the other were true. If adversity reverses the sign of the connectivity signature, as Leypoldt reports, then pooling patients with and without adversity produces an average in which the sign-opposed contributions cancel, which is precisely what a very small pooled effect size looks like. An effect of d = −0.10 is what you would expect to see if a real difference of larger magnitude existed in each of two subpopulations and pointed in different directions. It is a conjecture, and the ENIGMA structural data and the Tübingen functional data are different measures, since a reversal of resting connectivity need not imply a reversal of gray matter volume. But the conjecture generates a clean prediction. Adversity is associated, as Leypoldt and colleagues note in their introduction, with earlier onset, and early onset is where ENIGMA found the hippocampal deficit that scales sharply in adults. Hippocampal vulnerability to early stress is among the oldest findings in neurobiology. If early-onset patients are disproportionately the adversity-exposed ones, then the early-onset structural signature, hippocampal and not striatal, may be the structural face of the adverse-childhood subtype, while the late-onset putamen and pallidum signature, the larger of the two in magnitude, may belong to patients whose disorder arrived without that history. Under that reading the two subgroups of the ENIGMA analysis would be two brain disorders sharing a diagnostic label and differentiated by developmental origin.

Now run it the other way, because the Tübingen paper has its own blind spot, and the ENIGMA paper exposes it. The Leypoldt network contains the caudate bilaterally, and the striatum is where ENIGMA found its only consistent structural signal, yet the Tübingen group had no region-of-interest evidence in the striatum, their putamen seed returned nothing, and their subcortical story runs through hippocampus and thalamus. Meanwhile ENIGMA’s null for adolescents is a warning that whatever the Tübingen network shows in adults may be the product of years of living with the disorder rather than a trace of its origin. The cross-sectional adult design cannot say whether the opposite connectivity patterns precede the disorder, as a vulnerability that adversity carves, or follow it, as an adaptation. Leypoldt and colleagues state this honestly and lay out the two possibilities, that adversity shapes a variable neural correlate of an otherwise constant disorder, or that the disorder has a constant correlate while adversity’s sequelae differ in who develops it. Their most provocative observation is that high-adversity controls look like low-adversity patients. If that holds, then the disorder in its adversity-free form may be a state that resilient, mistreated people share without ever becoming symptomatic, which would make the connectivity pattern a risk-adjacent trait rather than a disease signature, and would explain why the ENIGMA volumes never tracked symptom severity.

Both papers, in the end, are held back by the same limit of the cross-sectional adult scan, and both end their discussions with the same plea for longitudinal samples that begin in childhood. That plea is correct and is also not enough. The deeper difficulty is that the field has two instruments, one with enormous power and almost no phenotypic depth, and one with phenotypic depth and almost no power, and nothing connecting them. The consortium can detect an effect of a tenth of a standard deviation but cannot say whose brain it describes. The clinical sample can say whose brain it describes but cannot say whether a sign reversal among 19 people would survive a second sample of 19. Consider what it would take to join them. A consortium dataset in which every site collected one common adversity measure at a common severity threshold, along with age of onset, would let the interaction be tested at ENIGMA scale, and the working group has announced cortical analyses as its next step. A prospective cohort that recruited children at high adversity and followed their connectivity into the age of onset would let the Tübingen interaction be observed forming rather than inferred after the fact. Until one of those exists, the central claim of three decades of social anxiety neuroimaging, that there is a brain of social anxiety to be found, rests on an unexamined assumption.

So here is the question this edition leaves open, and it matters for every study the Dispatch will cover from now on. Is social anxiety disorder one neural condition measured badly, or at least two neural conditions that converge on a single clinical description, one arising from a history of emotional mistreatment and expressed as hyperconnected cingulate and disconnected visual cortex, the other arising without that history and expressed as a late-onset shrinking of the striatum? If the answer is two, then the effect-size ceiling that ENIGMA has now measured at roughly one tenth of a standard deviation is a property of the diagnostic category and never of the brain, and the next decade of biomarker searches will keep failing for a reason no increase in sample size can fix.
