The Social Brain Dispatch

The Putamen Problem

The largest brain-structure study of social anxiety ever assembled, 1,115 patients scanned at sites on five continents, found its clearest signal in the putamen rather than the amygdala, at a magnitude near a tenth of a standard deviation. A Tübingen team scanning 120 people at rest suggests that the smallness of that number may be the arithmetic signature of averaging over a childhood the big study never asked about.

Edition No. 19 · Frontier Neuroscience · October 1, 2026

A field earns the right to build on its findings only after its findings have been made to stand still. For roughly three decades, structural and functional imaging of social anxiety disorder has produced a literature in which almost every result arrives with a rival. The amygdala is larger in one report and smaller in the next. The hippocampus shrinks in a sample of young adults and holds steady in a sample of adolescents. Connectivity between the anterior cingulate and the visual system goes up in one resting-state study and down in another, and the prefrontal cortex is invoked in both directions so freely that a reader could be forgiven for suspecting that the brain of the socially anxious person is whatever the most recent paper says it is. Eighteen editions of this Dispatch have walked through the architecture that this literature was used to justify, the amygdala that fires too hard at an angry face, the prefrontal brake that fails to engage, the salience and default networks that bind self-focus to threat. The stakes of the contradictions are therefore larger than tidiness. If the base rates of the brain differences themselves are unstable, then every mechanism stacked on them, and every hope of a neural marker that could predict who will respond to which treatment, rests on sand. The two papers in this edition are the frontier’s two most serious answers to that instability, and they answer it in opposite directions. One says the way out is more people, pooled and harmonized until the noise averages away. The other says the noise is not noise at all, that it is two or more true signals with opposite signs being averaged into confusion by a variable almost nobody records. Both are right about something, and the place where they cannot both be fully right is the most useful thing in this field to look at today.

Start with the pooled answer, because its scale is the argument. The paper is a mega-analysis of subcortical brain volumes in social anxiety disorder published in Molecular Psychiatry in 2023 under the name of the ENIGMA-Anxiety Working Group, with Nynke Groenewold of the University of Cape Town as first author and Dan Stein of Cape Town and Nic van der Wee of Leiden at the senior end of a byline that runs to ninety-nine names (Groenewold et al., 2023). Anyone who has followed this publication from its first edition will find the author list a roll call of the field’s own history. Tomas Furmark, whose Uppsala group produced the PET study of speaking stress that this Dispatch read in Edition 3, is there. So is Murray Stein, whose 2002 study of amygdala responses to angry and contemptuous faces anchored Edition 10, and Heide Klumpp, who appeared in Editions 10 and 12 with insula and amygdala-prefrontal connectivity work, and Daniel Pine of the National Institute of Mental Health, whose developmental fingerprints were on Editions 4 and 9. The consortium is a device for making the field’s competing laboratories contribute their scans to a single table. It descends from ENIGMA, the Enhancing Neuro Imaging Genetics through Meta-Analysis network that Paul Thompson’s group at the University of Southern California built to bring meta-analytic scale to brain imaging, and the working group on anxiety carries that discipline into a disorder family whose imaging literature had been assembled one small sample at a time. The author list of the 2017 Leiden-led mega-analysis, for what it is worth, already contained Furmark, Klumpp, and Stein, so this is a reunion as much as a debut. The people most identified with this particular analysis come from two traditions that complement each other. Stein runs the Cape Town psychiatric neuroscience program whose cross-national epidemiology of social anxiety, read here in Edition 8, established how common and how culturally patterned the disorder is. Janna Marie Bas-Hoogendam, the second author, brought the other half of the lineage from Leiden, where her doctoral work on the Leiden Family Lab study scanned more than a hundred members of eight families genetically enriched for social anxiety, and where she had already led a voxel-based mega-analysis of 174 adults that pointed at the putamen (Bas-Hoogendam et al., 2017). The 2023 paper is, in that sense, her own earlier result handed a much larger test.

The concepts that make the design matter are worth stating carefully, because the design is the paper’s main claim to authority. A meta-analysis combines published summary statistics. A mega-analysis, which is what this is, pools the raw participant-level data and fits a single model to all of it, which allows covariates to be treated identically across sites and allows effects of age and clinical subgroup to be estimated within the data rather than argued about across papers. The price is harmonization. Every site ran its own T1-weighted acquisition at 1.5 or 3 tesla, but every scan was segmented with FreeSurfer under the ENIGMA protocol, fourteen subcortical volumes were extracted (thalamus, amygdala, hippocampus, putamen, pallidum, nucleus accumbens and caudate, bilaterally), and each segmentation was inspected visually for failure. Post-quality-control inclusion was 97.7 percent of observations. The statistics were linear mixed-effects models with a random intercept for scan site, including sex, age, age squared, their interactions, and total intracranial volume as covariates, with effect sizes reported as mixed-effects d, a standardized difference scaled like Cohen’s d but corrected for the non-independence of observations nested in sites. The authors tested whether a random slope for diagnosis per site improved fit and found it did not for nine of the fourteen regions, so they kept the simpler model. Significance was set at p below .05 family-wise corrected across the fourteen regions, an uncorrected threshold of .00357. A reader with a methods background will find nothing exotic here, and that is deliberate. The paper is engineered so that its result cannot be dismissed as an analytic choice.

The sample was 1,115 patients and 2,775 healthy controls from 37 samples in ten countries. Social anxiety status came from diagnostic interview, lifetime or current, and only 2.5 percent of patients in the 35 samples that assessed current status met lifetime but not current criteria; two samples, the Philadelphia Neurodevelopmental Cohort and the Study of Health in Pomerania, could not distinguish. Patients with schizophrenia-spectrum disorder, bipolar disorder, or autism were excluded, controls had no lifetime major psychiatric diagnosis, and the age range ran from 8 to 69, split at 21 and 22 into adolescent and adult subsamples. The result in the full sample is the headline everyone will quote and no one should quote without the number attached. Patients showed smaller bilateral putamen volume, with a left d of −0.077 (corrected p = .037) and a right d of −0.104 (corrected p = .001). Those are tenths of a standard deviation. The finding survived exclusion of patients with comorbid obsessive-compulsive disorder or posttraumatic stress disorder, though the left putamen slipped to non-significance when the contrast was restricted to patients with current disorder (d = −0.074, corrected p = .156), an effect size essentially unchanged but with fewer participants behind it. A diagnosis-by-age interaction appeared for the left putamen (r = −0.034, corrected p = .045), and the age split told the developmental story the authors had hoped to find. In adults, putamen was smaller (left d = −0.141, right d = −0.158, both corrected p below .001) and pallidum was larger (left d = 0.129, corrected p = .006; right d = 0.099, corrected p = .046). In adolescents, nothing survived correction. No interaction with sex emerged, and none of the three severity measures (the Liebowitz scale in 21 samples and 523 patients, trait anxiety in 19 samples, depressive symptoms in 19) correlated with any volume after correction.

Then the subgroups, which are where the paper gets interesting and where it is hardest to read cleanly. Patients with a comorbid anxiety disorder had a smaller left amygdala than controls (d = −0.145, corrected p = .017), the only place in the entire analysis where the structure that has carried this field’s theory for thirty years separated from controls, and it separated only in patients who carried another anxiety diagnosis. Patients without comorbid anxiety showed the putamen effect (left d = −0.091, right d = −0.097). Patients taking psychotropic medication at scan, including those on serotonergic agents, showed no corrected differences, while unmedicated patients showed a smaller right putamen (d = −0.100, corrected p = .007). Splitting at the median onset age of 13 years yielded the most structured pattern in the paper. Early-onset patients had a smaller right hippocampus (d = −0.194, corrected p = .009), stronger in adults (d = −0.326, corrected p below .001), and no putamen finding. Later-onset patients had the striatopallidal signature at far greater magnitude than the full sample, with putamen d around −0.31 to −0.34, a smaller left accumbens (d = −0.171), and larger pallidum (d up to 0.240). The discussion places these results beside other ENIGMA disorders. The pairing of smaller putamen with larger pallidum was not seen in the other working groups, generalized anxiety disorder showed a subthreshold enlargement of the putamen, obsessive-compulsive disorder showed larger pallidum, autism showed smaller putamen and pallidum, and the magnitude of the social anxiety effects sits near the hippocampal effects of about d = −0.15 in obsessive-compulsive disorder, trauma, and depression, and well below the roughly −0.46 seen in schizophrenia.

A critical reading begins by admitting how honest the authors are, and then asking what the honesty costs. The putamen finding is real in the sense that it replicates the direction of one earlier meta-analysis and is the best-powered estimate of its kind ever produced, yet it is a difference of one-tenth of a standard deviation in a variable measured with an automated segmentation whose reliability for small deep structures is itself imperfect. Cross-sectional data cannot tell development from cohort. The adolescent null and the adult effect might be an effect that accumulates with years of illness, which is the authors’ reading, or an effect that exists only in people who stay ill into adulthood, or a difference in which kinds of patients get recruited into adult versus adolescent studies. The subgroup contrasts are not analyses of the same participants under different labels. Onset, comorbidity, and medication were available only in the subset of samples that recorded them, so the later-onset putamen effect of −0.34, four times the full-sample effect, probably reflects a composition of samples as much as a biology of lateness, and the authors concede that variables had to be restricted to those consistently collected. Medication is the starkest case. Few patients were medicated at scan, so the observation that medicated patients showed no putamen difference has almost no power to distinguish a normalizing drug effect from the absence of one. Severity measures exist for a minority of patients, and a structural volume that does not track the Liebowitz score at n of 523 is telling the field something about what the volume is, which makes it a trait-like or history-like quantity that behaves nothing like a gauge of current suffering. What the paper does not contain, and what its authors could not have added, is any measure of the childhood in which most of these patients developed the disorder.

That absence is the doorway to the second paper. It comes from Benjamin Kreifelts and colleagues at the University of Tübingen, published in Translational Psychiatry in 2025 with Melina Leypoldt as first author (Leypoldt et al., 2025). Kreifelts leads the anxiety research group in the department of psychiatry and psychotherapy there, a program whose thematic center is social anxiety and emotional communication, studied with functional imaging and behavioral measurement, and whose intellectual signature is the study of nonverbal signals in the voice and face. His 2017 habilitation dealt with the multimodal perception of laughter in social anxiety, a topic that shows a group treating the disorder as a disturbance of ordinary social exchange, with threat as one ingredient among several. The senior psychiatrist Andreas Fallgatter and the epigenetics researcher Vanessa Nieratschker share the byline, and Ariane Wiegand, the group’s earlier first author, supplies the lineage that matters here. In an earlier report drawn from a subsample of the same participants, Wiegand and colleagues found that patients with social anxiety and low early adversity showed increased activation in left dorsolateral prefrontal cortex and left medial superior frontal gyrus while processing fear-related stimuli, whereas patients with high early adversity did not. That finding is the direct ancestor of the 2025 paper, and its logic is the paper’s whole premise. If the neural signature of social anxiety depends on whether you were maltreated or neglected as a child, then a literature that pools patients without recording that history will produce exactly the contradictions with which this edition began. Kreifelts also appears among the contributing authors on the ENIGMA paper, which means that the same laboratory is on record on both sides of the argument this edition stages, as a contributor of scans to the average and as the author of a study asking what the average erases.

The concepts the Tübingen paper needs are an interaction, a resting-state network, and a multivariate way of searching for one. The design crosses diagnosis with adversity, and the quantity of interest is the interaction, formally the contrast (low-adversity controls minus high-adversity controls) minus (low-adversity patients minus high-adversity patients). A main effect of social anxiety would say patients differ from controls. An interaction says the direction or size of that difference depends on history, and it is the only statistical object that can explain why two studies of the same disorder disagree. Resting-state functional connectivity, the correlation over time between the spontaneous BOLD signals of two regions, is the dependent measure. The search strategy is the one familiar to anyone who reads this Dispatch’s methods beat. The authors first tested fourteen regions of interest chosen from the literature, regions in which resting connectivity had been altered in both social anxiety and early adversity, then ran seed-based maps from those regions plus two seeds taken from Wiegand’s task study, and then turned to connectome-wide multivoxel pattern analysis, in which each voxel’s whole-brain connectivity profile is reduced by principal component analysis to a few components and tested for group structure without any seed. This approach is hypothesis-free about where the effect lives, which is precisely what a first look at a new interaction requires.

The sample was 120 German-speaking adults of European descent, aged 19 to 48, diagnosed with the structured clinical interview for DSM-IV, of whom 49 had social anxiety disorder. Early adversity was measured with the Childhood Trauma Questionnaire, and a participant counted as high-adversity when any one of its five subscales exceeded the Bernstein and Fink severity threshold, which sorted people into four cells of unequal size. Low-adversity controls numbered 49 and high-adversity controls 22, low-adversity patients 30 and high-adversity patients 19. The Liebowitz scores sorted the groups cleanly (about 16 and 23 in controls, 68 and 75 in patients). Scanning was ten minutes of eyes-closed rest on a 3-tesla Prisma, multiband acquisition with a 1.5-second repetition time and 405 volumes, preprocessed in SPM12 and the CONN toolbox with aggressive denoising, scrubbing of volumes beyond 0.9 millimeters of framewise displacement, and bandpass filtering between 0.008 and 0.09 hertz. The region-of-interest analysis found nothing. Of the seed analyses, one produced a result that survived correction across all sixteen seeds, the left dorsolateral prefrontal seed borrowed from the task study, whose connectivity to ten clusters in visual, pre- and postcentral, and inferior parietal cortex showed a diagnosis-by-adversity interaction. The multivariate analysis found three clusters, in left calcarine fissure extending into precuneus (MNI −26, −60, 12; 70 voxels; corrected p = .002), in subgenual anterior cingulate (6, 10, −12; 65 voxels; p = .002), and in left middle frontal gyrus (−34, 8, 36; 42 voxels; p = .016). Seeding from those three and intersecting the maps produced a network of 36 connections with a significant interaction, anchored on six regions that interacted with all three nodes, in visual cortex, pre- and postcentral cortex, and middle and inferior temporal gyrus. The middle frontal node sits where Wiegand’s task activation had been, which is the paper’s tidiest convergence.

Within that network the direction of the effects is what matters, and the direction is consistent in a way the authors identify as five patterns that share one logic. Whenever adversity changed connectivity in controls, it changed it in the opposite direction in patients. In low-adversity controls the subgenual cingulate was positively coupled to most of the network and the calcarine node negatively coupled. In high-adversity controls both flipped toward negative. Patients with low adversity looked like high-adversity controls. Patients with high adversity looked like neither, with what the authors call hyperconnectivity of the subgenual cingulate and an almost complete absence of resting connectivity in visual cortex. The categorical result was supported by a dimensional one, in that correlations between connectivity and CTQ total score had opposite signs in patients and controls across the whole network, and the subscales decomposed it. Emotional abuse and emotional neglect each carried the interaction in 29 of 36 connections, physical neglect in 21, physical abuse in none, and sexual abuse in one. The interaction stayed significant when state and trait anxiety and depressive symptoms were entered as covariates (all F(1,116) of 14.2 or more, p below .001), which the authors take to mean that something specific to the social form of anxiety is moving the network. The discussion is candid about three readings of why high-adversity controls resemble low-adversity patients. A shared neural resource, a compensation that fails, or a subclinical vulnerability. Their reductionist two-hypothesis summary is worth remembering, since it will govern the next decade of this question: either the neural correlate of the disorder varies with adversity, or the neural sequelae of adversity differ between those who go on to develop the disorder and those who do not.

The critique of this paper should be as plain as the praise for it. It is a cross-sectional study with 19 patients in its pivotal cell, in which the network was found by a search that had four free components and a voxel threshold of p below .001 uncorrected before cluster correction, and in which the post-hoc t-tests that sort the 36 connections into five tidy patterns are run on clusters selected for showing the interaction. The five patterns are therefore a description of one interaction contrast, and cannot be counted as five independent findings, and the effect sizes within them will be inflated by selection. The null region-of-interest analysis, which the authors explain by the mismatch between anatomical masks and the real location of effects, could equally say the effect is small, and an amygdala-focused small-volume analysis was also empty. Eyes-closed rest makes the visual cortex “disconnection” in high-adversity patients hard to interpret, because occipital coupling under closed eyes is exquisitely sensitive to arousal and vigilance state, and the authors acknowledge that heart rate variability, lower at rest in social anxiety, was not monitored. High-adversity participants were older by about three years on average (F = 6.72, p = .011) and reported more depressive symptoms and higher trait anxiety, and adversity was measured retrospectively by a self-report instrument whose subscale threshold is a clinical convention. The sample is 70 percent female among patients and almost entirely German of European ancestry. None of this lowers the importance of the idea. It means the idea is at the stage of a well-motivated first look, and the authors say so, closing with a call for longitudinal work beginning in childhood.

Place the two papers on the same table and the first thing that appears is a shared diagnosis of the field’s disease, with opposite prescriptions. Both begin from contradiction, the ENIGMA group from contradictory volumes and the Tübingen group from contradictory connectivity, and both identify heterogeneity as the culprit. The ENIGMA paper’s own data show it. The full-sample putamen effect of about −0.1 turns into −0.34 in later-onset patients and vanishes in early-onset ones, who show a hippocampal effect instead. A pooled estimate near a tenth of a standard deviation, composed of subgroups with effects of opposite structure, is what an interaction looks like when the moderator is unmeasured. The Tübingen paper supplies the mechanism that arithmetic requires. If the sign of a connectivity difference between patients and controls depends on a history of emotional maltreatment, then a sample that is 30 percent high-adversity and a sample that is 60 percent high-adversity will report different, even opposite, group effects from the same disorder, and a consortium that pools across a hundred samples with unknown adversity mixtures will converge on a number near zero with a very good standard error. The consortium’s tenth of a standard deviation may be the average of footprints, some of which are large.

The tension is just as clear and more interesting. The two papers measure different things. One is structure, one is function, and the ENIGMA finding sits in the striatum, pallidum, hippocampus and amygdala, while the Tübingen network sits in cingulate, prefrontal, precuneus and sensory cortex with caudate and thalamus at its edges, and the regions barely overlap. Structure is slow and accumulates over years, while resting connectivity is a fast state that can shift with a night’s sleep. The ENIGMA severity null says volumes are indifferent to how anxious a patient is now. The Tübingen paper reports a functional signature that tracks the dose of past emotional abuse and neglect. These could be one story at two timescales, in which adversity during development reshapes cingulate-sensory coupling early and the structural residue appears in the striatum and hippocampus later, but nothing in either paper licenses that claim, and the adolescent null in ENIGMA is a mild counterweight, since a developmental story in which adversity-shaped circuitry precedes disorder predicts differences in the young that the big study did not find. Each paper also exposes the other’s blind spot. The pooled analysis has the power and cannot see the moderator, since the harmonized dataset contains no adversity measure and the paper reports none. The Tübingen design sees the moderator and lacks the power to know whether its five patterns would survive a second sample, a different scanner, or an open-eyed acquisition. Neither paper’s favorite region is the other’s. The amygdala that organized the first eighteen editions of this publication has a minor role in one and none in the other.

The most productive way to join them is a prediction that neither paper makes and that the ENIGMA data may already contain. Early-onset anxiety is clinically associated with a history of adversity, a link the Tübingen introduction cites, and the hippocampus is the structure the broader stress literature has most often tied to early adversity. The early-onset hippocampal effect, concentrated in adults at d = −0.326, may be the structural shadow of the same high-adversity subgroup whose resting connectivity Leypoldt and colleagues found inverted. In that case the later-onset putamen and pallidum signature would belong to the low-adversity patients, a group whose connectivity in Tübingen looks like that of ordinary high-adversity controls, and the field’s inconsistencies would resolve into at least two kinds of social anxiety disorder that share a diagnosis, a symptom checklist, and a treatment manual and differ in the neurobiology that carries them. This is a hypothesis constructed from two papers that did not coordinate, and it could be wrong in every part. Onset age and adversity are correlated without being interchangeable, retrospective onset reports are unreliable, and the Tübingen participants are adults aged 19 to 48 with no onset analysis. But it is falsifiable with data that exist. A consortium with the ENIGMA group’s harmonization and a minimum dataset including a validated adversity measure could test whether the diagnosis-by-onset pattern collapses once adversity is entered, and whether the Tübingen network replicates across scanners, in open-eyed rest, and in adolescents before onset.

What remains unresolved is whether social anxiety disorder has a neural identity at all or only a set of neural identities that the diagnosis hides. If adversity inverts the sign of the circuit-level correlate, then the single biomarker for which clinical neuroscience has been searching may be unfindable in principle, and the right question for the next generation of studies is which developmental history a given circuit pattern encodes. If instead the interaction proves to be an artifact of small cells and flexible searching, the consortium’s tenth of a standard deviation stands as the honest measure of the disorder’s structural footprint, and the field must accept that its most cited brain differences are small enough that no scanner will ever read them off an individual patient. Which of these is true decides whether the next ten years of this field are spent building stratified models or abandoning the search for single-region markers. The data to choose between them require only that someone ask, in every future scan, about the childhood of the person in the scanner.
