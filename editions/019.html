The Social Brain Dispatch

The Sign Flips Where Nobody Was Looking

A Tübingen team scanned 120 adults at rest and found that the connectivity signature of social anxiety reverses direction depending on whether the patient was emotionally maltreated as a child, while a Münster team pooling 160 people across the diagnostic map recovered a faint structural signature that points at the same visual cortex. Read together, they suggest that the field’s averaged brain signature for social anxiety is small because it is the sum of two opposite ones.

Edition No. 19 · Frontier Neuroscience · October 19, 2026

For twenty years the neuroimaging of social anxiety disorder has been a hunt for a signature, some pattern of amygdala, insula, cingulate and prefrontal activity or coupling that separates patients from controls reliably enough to be read off a scan. This Dispatch has followed the hunt through meta-analyses, face paradigms, resting-state seeds and machine-learning classifiers, and the honest summary of what it has found is that the effects point in different directions in different hands. Amygdala coupling goes up in one sample and down in the next. Prefrontal connectivity does the same. The largest mega-analysis of subcortical volumes in the disorder, the ENIGMA-Anxiety effort that Meinert and colleagues (2026) cite in their introduction, found no robust difference at all. The standard explanations are small samples, loose diagnostic boundaries and methodological drift, and every one of them is probably true. Two papers published in the last eighteen months, from two German groups working in different imaging modalities with different statistical philosophies, offer a more unsettling one. The signature may be inconsistent because the diagnostic label hides more than one of them, and because at least one of the hidden variables flips the sign. If that is right, the problem with biomarker research in this disorder runs deeper than power. Every case-control contrast, every classifier trained on pooled patients, averages across opposing effects and reports the residue.

The first paper comes from the Department of Psychiatry and Psychotherapy at the University of Tübingen, and its corresponding author is Benjamin Kreifelts. To understand why this group would be the one to ask about childhood adversity, it helps to know the program Kreifelts works in. His published record, much of it with Dirk Wildgruber in the same department, has been about how the socially anxious brain handles the raw materials of social signals, the voice and the face. A 2019 fMRI study of fifty participants found that sensitivity of the left temporal voice area and left amygdala to voices, and of the right fusiform and posterior superior temporal face areas to faces, scaled with social anxiety, and that the amplification was not confined to threatening expressions. The same year the group published a resting-state study of markers of biased perception in social anxiety. That is a lab organized around the question of what the socially anxious brain does with other people. The paper at hand is a pivot to an earlier question, which is whose brain is being asked. The senior author list around Kreifelts includes Andreas Fallgatter, whose department houses the work, and Vanessa Nieratschker, whose own research concerns epigenetic regulation, and that connection to epigenetics matters to the design. The immediate predecessor of this paper, a 2021 study in the Journal of Psychiatry and Neuroscience led by Ariane Wiegand, who appears here with a Max Planck Fellow Group affiliation in precision psychiatry, scanned a subsample of the same participants during a task with anxiety-relevant stimuli and examined both early-life adversity and DNA methylation. It found that left dorsolateral prefrontal cortex and left medial superior frontal gyrus responded more strongly in socially anxious people with low adversity, and did not do so in socially anxious people with high adversity. Leypoldt and colleagues (2025) take that task-evoked interaction and ask whether it survives at rest, and, more ambitiously, whether it explains why the resting-state literature on the disorder contradicts itself.

The sample is 120 German-speaking adults of European descent between 19 and 48, scanned in Tübingen. Diagnosis came from the German SCID for DSM-IV, which identified 49 participants with social anxiety disorder, and severity was indexed by the Liebowitz Social Anxiety Scale. Early-life adversity was measured retrospectively with the Childhood Trauma Questionnaire, and a participant counted as high-adversity if they crossed the Bernstein and Fink severity threshold on at least one of five subscales, at 15 or more for emotional neglect, 13 for emotional abuse, 10 for physical abuse or neglect, and 8 for sexual abuse. Crossing the two factors yielded four cells, 49 low-adversity controls, 22 high-adversity controls, 30 low-adversity patients and 19 high-adversity patients. The cells behave as the design requires on the dimensions that define them. Liebowitz scores averaged 16.2 and 22.9 in the two control cells and 67.6 and 74.7 in the two patient cells, with no effect of adversity on symptom severity (p = .109) and no interaction. Childhood Trauma Questionnaire totals ran from 29.2 in low-adversity controls to 56.3 in high-adversity patients, and patients scored higher than controls overall (F = 16.51), which means adversity and diagnosis are correlated in the sample, as they are in the world. Age differed by adversity group (27.8 and 27.6 versus 25.0 and 24.3, p = .011) and was modeled along with sex.

Acquisition was ten minutes of eyes-closed rest on a 3 tesla Siemens Prisma, 405 multiband volumes at a repetition time of 1.5 seconds and 2 mm isotropic voxels. Preprocessing ran in SPM12 and the CONN toolbox, with outlier scans flagged at framewise displacement above 0.9 mm or global signal change above five standard deviations, and denoising by five white matter and five cerebrospinal fluid components, twelve motion regressors, outlier-scan regressors and a 0.008 to 0.09 Hz band-pass, without global signal regression, and 6 mm smoothing. The analysis was staged. First came a literature-derived set of fourteen regions, among them the bilateral amygdala, hippocampus and anterior cingulate, chosen because they had altered resting connectivity in both social anxiety and adversity research, tested in a ROI-to-ROI design for the interaction contrast, (control low minus control high) minus (patient low minus patient high). Nothing survived correction. Second came seed-based maps from those fourteen regions plus the two prefrontal regions from the Wiegand task study, thresholded at voxelwise p < .001, cluster-level FDR p < .05 and a further Bonferroni correction across sixteen seeds. The amygdala yielded nothing, even with small-volume correction. The hippocampus gave a right superior and middle frontal cluster (MNI 30, 66, 12) that failed Bonferroni. The left dorsolateral prefrontal seed from the earlier task study, however, produced ten clusters of interaction, mostly in visual cortex, pre- and postcentral cortex and inferior parietal cortex.

The third stage was hypothesis-free. A connectivity multivoxel pattern analysis, which compresses each voxel’s whole-brain connectivity profile by principal component and then tests the interaction on the resulting low-dimensional representation, returned three clusters. They sat in the left calcarine fissure extending into precuneus (MNI −26, −60, 12; 70 voxels; FDR p = .002), the subgenual anterior cingulate (6, 10, −12; 65 voxels; p = .002) and the left middle frontal gyrus (−34, 8, 36; 42 voxels; p = .016). Seeding each of these in turn and intersecting the resulting interaction maps by minimum-statistic conjunction gave a network of 36 regions, with six clusters interacting with all three seeds, concentrated in visual cortex, pre- and postcentral gyri and middle and inferior temporal cortex. The left middle frontal node overlapped the dorsolateral cluster from the task study, which is the authors’ bridge between evoked and intrinsic activity.

What the interactions look like is the substance of the paper. In low-adversity controls the subgenual cingulate was mostly positively coupled to the rest of the network, and the calcarine node mostly negatively. In high-adversity controls that arrangement shifted, with subgenual and calcarine coupling both predominantly negative and the middle frontal node predominantly positive. Patients with low adversity resembled high-adversity controls. Patients with high adversity departed from everyone, with positive subgenual hyperconnectivity and an almost complete absence of visual cortex coupling, which the authors call disconnection. Five distinct interaction motifs were catalogued, and in every one the adversity-related difference ran in opposite directions in patients and controls. The effect held for all 36 connections after adjusting for state and trait anxiety and Beck depression scores (every F(1,116) at or above 14.2, p < .001), which speaks against the obvious reading that general distress is doing the work. Treating adversity as continuous, the slope of connectivity on Childhood Trauma Questionnaire score differed between patients and controls for every connection, and the subscale breakdown is the most striking result in the paper. Emotional abuse and emotional neglect each produced significant slope differences in 29 of 36 connections, physical neglect in 21, physical abuse in none and sexual abuse in one. The neural moderation of social anxiety, in this sample, tracks the emotional forms of maltreatment, the ones that most directly concern how a child is regarded by the people around them.

The result deserves both its force and its audit. The audit begins with circularity. The three central seeds were selected because they showed the interaction, and the seed-based maps were then tested for the same interaction, so the number of connections that pass and the sheer regularity of the crossover pattern are partly guaranteed by the selection. Thirty-six connections radiating from three seeds are not thirty-six independent confirmations. The cell of high-adversity patients has nineteen people in it, and a descriptive taxonomy of five motifs drawn from cell means in a design that size is a map of this sample before it is a map of the disorder. The fourteen-region literature analysis, which had every reason to find an amygdala or cingulate interaction, found none, and the authors attribute that candidly to anatomical ROIs fitting poorly with the real location of effects. The sample is young, mostly female, entirely of European descent and scanned with eyes closed, which raises the question of what a calcarine seed means when the retina is dark. And adversity is measured by retrospective self-report on a checklist that cannot capture timing, duration or exposure to the family circumstances that sit outside it, a limitation the authors state themselves. The causal story is also left open on purpose. High-adversity controls look like low-adversity patients, so the authors raise the possibility that the connectivity pattern is an adaptation to adversity that protects some people from the disorder, and that the patient pattern in the high-adversity group is that adaptation failing. They also raise the alternative that the correlates of the disorder are constant and the correlates of adversity vary between individuals. Cross-sectional data distinguish none of these.

The second paper comes from Münster, from the Institute for Translational Psychiatry that Udo Dannlowski directs, a group known for assembling large phenotyped cohorts and using them to ask what childhood maltreatment and affective illness do to brain structure. Dannlowski co-led the DFG research group FOR2107 on the neurobiology of affective disorders from 2014 to 2022. The first author is Hannah Meinert, and the senior authorship is shared between Joscha Böhnlein and Elisabeth Leehr, who is the corresponding author. This is a clinical-psychology and translational-psychiatry lab by temperament, preregistering its analyses and treating dimensional psychopathology as the default. Its method carries two lineages. Connectome-based predictive modeling descends from the Yale work of Emily Finn, Xilin Shen and colleagues on connectome fingerprinting and its 2017 protocol paper, in which edges are selected for their association with a behavior, summarized, and used to predict that behavior in held-out participants. The network-based statistic that Meinert and colleagues use to pick those edges descends from Zalesky and colleagues, and the toolbox that fuses the two, NBS-Predict, was built by Serin, Zalesky and others in 2021. The Münster group had already used it to ask how childhood maltreatment and social support are encoded in structural networks. Social anxiety is a natural next target because the cohort, the Transdiagnostic Investigation of Emotional Processing in Depression and Anxiety, was designed to cut across diagnoses.

Meinert and colleagues (2026) start from a conceptual wager that the Tübingen paper, in its way, tests from the opposite direction. Trait social anxiety is continuous, they argue, and distributed across the whole mental-health spectrum, so a signature worth the name should be found by regressing symptom severity on the brain across diagnoses rather than by contrasting patients with controls. Their sample is 160 right-handed German-speaking adults between 18 and 65. Sixty-four met DSM-5 criteria for social anxiety disorder, and 49 of those also had acute or remitted major depression. Of the 96 without the disorder, 49 had never had any mental disorder and 47 had a lifetime history of depression. The Liebowitz total averaged 40.1 overall, 21.5 in the group without the disorder and 68.0 in the group with it. The groups differed in age (29.5 versus 25.1 years), and the sample is 64 percent female. Diffusion data came from a 3 tesla Prisma with 2.5 mm isotropic voxels, sixty gradient directions at b = 1000 and five b0 images. Tensors were estimated with the RESTORE outlier-rejection algorithm and connectomes built with deterministic tractography in the CATO toolbox, on 114 nodes from the Cammoun subdivision of the Desikan-Killiany atlas, keeping only edges supported by at least three streamlines.

The predictive pipeline used repeated nested cross-validation, with ten inner and one hundred outer folds, edge selection at p = .01 (stricter than preregistered), connected-component detection by the network-based statistic, a choice between linear and support-vector regression with regularization tuned in the inner loop, and age and sex as covariates. Performance was the correlation between concatenated out-of-sample predictions and observed Liebowitz scores, tested against permuted labels. The headline result is a network of 46 edges predicting symptom severity with r = .141 (CI .12 to .2, p = .003), concentrated in visual and temporo-occipital cortex, with its most prominent positive edge between left lateral occipital and lingual cortex and several negatively weighted edges among fusiform, inferior temporal and occipital regions. Restricting to the 111 participants with any current or past disorder produced a larger network of 59 edges and r = .172 (CI .14 to .2, p < .001), and analysis in the plain Desikan-Killiany atlas gave a similar pattern. Whole-brain graph metrics added little. The number of modules was higher in patients (F(1,156) = 6.03, p = .015, partial η² = .037) and no other global metric differed by diagnosis. Hub analysis under the preregistered definition, the top 15 percent of both degree and betweenness centrality, found no hubs in the symptom network at all. A degree-only exploratory criterion found them in left and right precuneus and right superior parietal cortex.

This paper is more candid about its own weakness than most in the field, and the candor is the right place to start. An r of .14 means that out-of-sample predictions explain about two percent of the variance in symptom severity. The authors themselves describe the network as of limited stability, the hub findings as candidate regions only, and the whole as unfit to be called a biomarker, a position consistent with the large-sample brain-wide association literature that they cite for the observation that reliable effects in psychiatric neuroimaging are small. A few details deserve a reader’s sharper pencil. The abstract names hubs in left pericalcarine, inferior parietal, superior parietal, right lateral occipital cortex and pars orbitalis, while the Results report none under the preregistered definition and a different set under the lenient one, so the abstract and the body disagree. The percentages of the connectome claimed for the networks, 0.18 percent for 46 edges and 0.62 percent for 59, cannot both be right, and with 114 nodes there are 6,441 possible edges, so 46 edges would be about 0.7 percent by my arithmetic. The module-count difference at p = .015 is one of twelve global metrics tested and would not survive correction for them. Table 2 reports degrees of freedom of 79 for the continuous-severity regressions in a sample of 160, and partial η² values between .49 and .82 that are implausibly large for single Liebowitz regressions in this sample, which means that its associations between severity and path length and efficiency should be set aside until corrected. The cortical-only node set also means that the amygdala is absent from the analysis by construction. And the clinical group is two-thirds comorbid with depression, so the network may track depressive or general internalizing severity, a specificity problem the authors raise but cannot resolve without a covariate they did not report.

Place the two papers side by side and the first thing to notice is where they converge. Both were built in a field whose textbook circuit runs through the amygdala and prefrontal cortex. Neither found it. Leypoldt and colleagues’ amygdala and cingulate regions of interest, chosen from the literature, produced nothing after correction, and the unbiased multivariate search landed on the calcarine fissure, the subgenual cingulate and the middle frontal gyrus, with visual cortex as the dominant partner in nearly every interaction. Meinert and colleagues’ predictive network is anchored in lateral occipital, lingual, fusiform and inferior temporal cortex and its exploratory hubs sit in precuneus and superior parietal cortex. A functional interaction and a structural prediction, in different samples, with different methods, point at posterior visual and parietal territory. Each team reaches for the same interpretation, which is that the signal reflects altered processing of socially relevant visual information and the hypervigilance for social cues that the cognitive literature has long reported. That interpretation is plausible and also the one most easily written after the fact. The more sober reading is that posterior cortex is what two unbiased searches returned and that the field has not yet built the task paradigms, eye-tracking controls and structural-functional pairings needed to say what it is doing there.

The tension between the papers is the interesting part, and it is more informative than the convergence. Meinert and colleagues fit one linear relation between connectivity and symptom severity across a sample that is diagnostically heterogeneous and for which no adversity measure appears among the covariates, only age and sex. Leypoldt and colleagues show, at the functional level, that the relation between connectivity and the disorder can reverse in sign depending on a variable of exactly that kind. If something like that holds in the structural domain, and nothing in either paper shows that it does, then a main-effect model is the wrong instrument for the job. It would return a small, diffuse and unstable network, and the explained variance would be low regardless of sample size, because the model fits the average of two opposite slopes. A small r, a network the authors themselves call unstable and a lack of hubs under a rigorous definition are what such a signal would look like when regressed on its marginal. The inference runs only as far as a hypothesis, since the modalities differ and a sign reversal in resting coupling implies no such reversal in white matter streamlines. But it is a hypothesis with a clean test. A group that has already used connectome-based prediction on childhood maltreatment can ask whether the social anxiety network differs by maltreatment history, or train separate models within strata.

The reverse critique is also fair, and it lands on Tübingen. Meinert and colleagues went looking for a signature that generalizes and found one that barely does. Leypoldt and colleagues found an interaction in 120 people, with 19 in the critical cell, selected and then characterized in the same data, and offered no held-out test of whether the pattern predicts anything in anyone. A crossover interaction is the most seductive form a finding can take, and small samples with flexible analysis produce them more often than they should. Cross-validated prediction is exactly the discipline the Tübingen analysis lacks, and an adversity-stratified predictive model is exactly the extension the Münster analysis lacks. Neither paper can speak to what the other has. The functional paper has no tractography, and the structural paper has no resting dynamics and no developmental history.

Both teams also share a blind spot that neither paper can see from inside. The entire design of each is cross-sectional, and the most important variable in the argument, the order in which adversity, circuitry and symptoms arrived, is the one thing neither can supply. Adversity is correlated with diagnosis in the Tübingen sample, patients with the disorder scored higher on the trauma measure overall, and the Münster authors invoke adolescence as the period when structural networks consolidate and the early forms of anxiety take root. The long-term implication of the Tübingen paper is that a neuromarker for susceptibility after adversity would have to be built in children before the disorder exists, which the authors say outright when they call for longitudinal work starting in childhood. The implication of the Münster paper is that such a marker, if it exists in the structure of the brain, is modest enough that it will only be seen with samples much larger than 160.

That leaves a question this Dispatch will carry forward into its developmental editions. Two people with the same Liebowitz score, 68 in one and 75 in the other, the same diagnosis, the same SCID, and the same clinical presentation, may differ in resting coupling between subgenual cingulate and visual cortex by a sign, depending on whether someone made them feel unwanted as a child. If that is the structure of the disorder, then what is the unit that treatment acts on? Cognitive therapy that works equally well in both groups would be acting on a downstream node that two different circuits share, and cognitive therapy that works differently would be the first evidence that the two circuits are really two disorders. I know of no treatment trial in this literature that stratifies by childhood emotional maltreatment and scans both arms. Until someone does, the field is estimating one effect from two populations whose effects cancel.
