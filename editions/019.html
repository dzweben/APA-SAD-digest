The Social Brain Dispatch

The Small Signal Below the Amygdala

Pooling 3,890 brains from five continents, the largest structural study of social anxiety ever run finds a difference of about a tenth of a standard deviation, in a basal ganglia structure the amygdala-centered field barely mentions, and only in adults. A family study of eight pedigrees saw the same pallidum years earlier and called it an endophenotype with nothing surviving correction, and between them they leave open whether this signature is a risk the brain brings to the disorder or a scar the disorder leaves.

Edition No. 19 · Frontier Neuroscience · November 2, 2026

There is a particular embarrassment waiting for any discipline that spends thirty years scanning one disorder, and social anxiety imaging has now reached it. The disorder is among the most common in psychiatry, its onset is anchored in a developmental window that the field understands unusually well, and its heritability has been argued over since Kagan’s inhibited toddlers. Yet when the structural question is finally put to the largest dataset anyone has assembled, the brain answers in a whisper. The question is whether people with social anxiety disorder have brains whose gross anatomy differs from the brains of people without it. The honest answer, as of the paper this edition begins with, is that the differences are real, tiny, confined to a structure the field has barely discussed, and absent in the age group where the disorder is born. That should unsettle everyone who has read the preceding eighteen editions of this publication, because those editions have been, almost without exception, about the amygdala and the cortex above it, and the single most reproducible structural signal in the disorder sits in the putamen and the pallidum below. This edition pairs that mega-analysis with a smaller, stranger, older study that arrived at the same corner of the brain by a completely different road, and the argument between them is about what a brain measure has to do before it earns the word endophenotype.

The mega-analysis belongs to the ENIGMA-Anxiety Working Group, and the group’s lineage is worth setting out, because the architecture of the paper is the architecture of the movement that made it. ENIGMA began as a bet that the small-sample, single-site neuroimaging literature in psychiatry could be rescued by standardizing the processing pipeline rather than the scanners, so that every site runs the same FreeSurfer segmentation and the same quality control and sends home summary statistics or raw regional volumes instead of images. Paul Thompson’s group at the University of Southern California built the original machinery for imaging genetics, and the disorder-specific working groups for schizophrenia, major depression, obsessive-compulsive disorder and others followed the template. The anxiety group assembled its social anxiety contingent under the coordination of Nic Van der Wee at Leiden University Medical Center, who is the senior author, and Nynke Groenewold, then working out of the University of Cape Town, who is the first. Cape Town matters in this story. The psychiatric genetics and neuroimaging community around Dan Stein there has spent two decades insisting that a science of mental illness built entirely on North Atlantic samples is a science of one part of the species, and the Groenewold paper carries that conviction in its sampling, thirty seven samples from ten countries on five continents. I would not overstate what a Cape Town postdoc and a Leiden psychiatrist share, but it is hard not to notice that the paper reads like the work of people who had been burned by small studies and decided to stop arguing from them.

Here is what they did, at the resolution the result demands. The mega-analysis pooled individual participant data from 1,115 patients with social anxiety disorder and 2,775 healthy controls, although the demographic table lists 1,120 and 2,781 and the manuscript does not reconcile the difference. Patients were 61.5 percent female with a mean age of 26.9 years (standard deviation 12.3); controls were 55.2 percent female with a mean age of 31.9 (15.6), a mismatch the models had to absorb. Diagnosis rested on whatever structured or semi-structured interview each site used, the SCID, MINI, CIDI, DAWBA or KSADS, current or lifetime depending on the sample, with two cohorts, the Philadelphia Neurodevelopmental Cohort and the Study of Health in Pomerania, contributing lifetime diagnoses only. Patients with schizophrenia spectrum, bipolar or autism spectrum diagnoses were excluded; controls had no lifetime major psychiatric diagnosis and, where recorded, no psychotropic medication. Everyone received a T1-weighted scan at 1.5 or 3 tesla, and FreeSurfer produced volumes for seven subcortical structures per hemisphere, thalamus, amygdala, hippocampus, putamen, pallidum, nucleus accumbens and caudate, with visual quality control and an inclusion rate of 97.7 percent. The authors split the data at 21 years into an adult stratum (22 to 69 years, 668 patients and 1,984 controls across 30 samples) and an adolescent stratum (8 to 21 years, 437 patients and 788 controls across 29 samples).

The analytic choices deserve more attention than they usually receive, because they are what make a number this small credible. The authors fit linear mixed-effects models with volume as the outcome, diagnosis as the predictor, and sex, age, age squared, the sex by age terms and total intracranial volume as covariates, with a random intercept for sample or scan site. They tested a random slope for diagnosis, found with likelihood ratio tests that it failed to improve fit for nine of fourteen regions, and kept the simpler model throughout. Effect sizes were mixed-effects d values derived from the t statistics, scaled like Cohen’s d but corrected for the non-independence of participants within sites, and significance was family-wise corrected across the fourteen regions, so that the uncorrected threshold was roughly p of .0036. This is a conservative design for a field that has historically reported uncorrected peaks in regions chosen after the fact, and it is precisely why the headline findings are modest.

In the full sample, patients had smaller putamen volumes than controls, with d of negative .077 on the left (corrected p of .037) and negative .104 on the right (corrected p of .001). Nothing else survived correction. The age by diagnosis interaction was significant for the left putamen (r of negative .034, corrected p of .045), which anticipates the stratified result. In adults the putamen reduction roughly doubled, d of negative .141 on the left and negative .158 on the right, both with corrected p below .001, and the pallidum was larger rather than smaller, d of positive .129 on the left (p of .006) and positive .099 on the right (p of .046). In adolescents, no region differed at all. Medication did not explain the adult pattern, since adults who were not taking psychotropic drugs showed d of negative .154 and negative .157 in the putamen and positive .170 and .132 in the pallidum. Comorbid anxiety did not explain it either, because adults without a comorbid anxiety disorder had the largest pallidal enlargement in the dataset, d of positive .214 on the left. Comorbid anxiety did add something, a smaller left amygdala (d of negative .145, corrected p of .017), which is the one place where the amygdala literature’s expectation shows up, and it shows up as a feature of comorbidity rather than of social anxiety as such.

Age of onset sharpened the picture further. Splitting patients at the median, onset before age 13 versus after, early onset patients showed a smaller right hippocampus (d of negative .194, corrected p of .009, rising to negative .326 in adults), while later onset patients carried the putamen and pallidum signature at much larger magnitude, d of negative .336 on the left putamen and negative .309 on the right, with the pallidum positive at .240 and .195. Symptom severity measured by the Liebowitz Social Anxiety Scale in 21 samples (n of 523), trait anxiety in 19 samples, and the Beck Depression Inventory in 19, produced nothing that survived correction. The authors take care to say that these effects are small by any standard, about negative .10 overall and perhaps negative .30 in the late onset subgroup, comparable with what ENIGMA reports for OCD, PTSD and depression and well below the hippocampal effect of roughly negative .46 in schizophrenia. They also note that the putamen smaller, pallidum larger pattern does not appear in the other ENIGMA disorders they compare against, the contrast with generalized anxiety disorder, where the putamen is enlarged, being the most provocative.

I have three reservations about reading this paper at face value, and they are worth stating because the sample is large enough that readers will be tempted to stop worrying. The first is that segmentation of the pallidum and putamen by FreeSurfer is not independent. The two structures abut, the pallidum is rich in iron and its T1 contrast differs by field strength and sequence, and a bias that nudges a boundary a fraction of a millimeter shows up as one structure shrinking and its neighbor growing. The authors acknowledge the possibility that the pallidal increase reflects a putamen extension, and the opposite signs of the two effects in adults, together with the failure of the pallidal enlargement to appear in adolescents, are exactly what a boundary artifact interacting with age would look like, although they are also what a real reorganization would look like. The second is the stratification. Adolescence in this dataset begins at eight and ends at twenty one, the adult stratum begins at twenty two, and the adult group includes samples recruited from patient populations with long illness histories, so that the age effect is confounded with duration, with cohort, and with the very different sampling frames that produce clinical adults and community adolescents. The third is that every inference here is cross-sectional. A mega-analysis can establish that a difference exists, and it can scarcely say whether the difference was there before the disorder, grew with it, or was produced by decades of avoidance and the particular life it builds.

The second paper arrives from the opposite end of the scale, and its authors made a choice that the consortium literature has no way to replicate. Janna Marie Bas-Hoogendam trained at Leiden under Van der Wee and the developmental psychologist Michiel Westenberg, and the Leiden Family Lab study on social anxiety disorder, whose first major structural report appeared in 2018, is the project on which her early career was built. The design was a deliberate rejection of the case-control logic that ENIGMA later scaled up. Rather than comparing unrelated patients to unrelated controls, the group recruited families, each one centered on a parent aged 25 to 55 with social anxiety disorder and a child at home aged 8 to 21 with clinical or subclinical social anxiety, then scanned as many first-degree relatives across two generations as they could. Nine families were recruited and one was dropped because the proband had a contraindication to MRI, leaving 110 participants (56 male) from eight families. The first generation had 51 members with a mean age of 46.5 years, the second 59 with a mean age of 18.1. Thirty nine had clinical or subclinical social anxiety, seventeen clinical and twenty two subclinical, while 62 did not. All scans were acquired on a single 3 tesla Philips system and processed with FreeSurfer 5.3 using the Desikan-Killiany atlas.

The logic behind the family design is the endophenotype concept as Gottesman and Gould framed it, and it is what separates the Leiden paper from a small case-control study. A candidate brain measure earns endophenotype status if it is heritable and if it co-segregates with the illness within families, meaning that within a pedigree the relatives with more symptoms carry more of the brain feature. It does not need to differ between patients and the public, and in principle it can track genetic liability in unaffected relatives. The Leiden analysis tested the amygdala, hippocampus, pallidum and putamen as subcortical regions of interest plus nineteen cortical regions, for thickness and for surface area separately, using linear mixed models with a random effect of family and covariates for age, sex and global measures. Heritability was estimated within the same random-effects framework, and associations with self-reported social anxiety and fear of negative evaluation were tested against a false discovery rate correction at five percent.

Then comes the part that makes the paper worth reading closely rather than citing in a sentence. Nothing survived the false discovery rate correction. The authors report this plainly and then, in the abstract and the conclusion, describe several measures as promising candidate endophenotypes anyway, on the basis of uncorrected p values at .05 combined with moderate to high heritability. Left pallidum volume rose with social anxiety and fear of negative evaluation, and had a heritability of .28 (p of .038). Right fusiform surface area fell with social anxiety, with heritability of .33. Several cortical thickness measures showed uncorrected associations in both directions, with heritabilities that were genuinely high for the left superior temporal gyrus (.74) and the left transverse temporal gyrus (.64), and moderate to high for the left medial orbitofrontal cortex and the rostral anterior cingulate. Sensitivity analyses excluding comorbid participants (leaving 70) or adjusting for depressive symptoms gave broadly similar patterns. The authors list their own limitations without evasion, namely eight families, a cross-sectional design, comorbidity, no control families from the general population, genetic data not yet analyzed, and the absence of correction survivors.

It would be easy to dismiss this as a pilot, but I think that reading misses what the paper is for. Heritability in a family study is estimated from covariance between relatives, and a trait such as superior temporal gyrus thickness can be highly heritable without having anything to do with social anxiety, as the anatomy of the brain is heritable nearly everywhere. The first endophenotype criterion is therefore cheap, and the second, co-segregation, carries the entire burden, which is exactly the criterion the data fail to support under correction. The authors are on the right side of the epistemic line when they flag the preliminary status, and on the wrong side when the word promising appears in the abstract above a table with no corrected survivors. Still, the single recurring finding across both datasets is the pallidum. A left pallidum that grows with symptoms inside eight families is the same structure, with the same sign, that the consortium finds enlarged in adults, and the early Leiden hint reads, in retrospect, as a prediction that a sample thirty five times larger would later vindicate in direction while shrinking it in magnitude.

Put together, the two papers converge on a small piece of anatomy that the field’s dominant circuit model has no place for. The classic account described in this publication’s previous editions, from the amygdala hyperreactivity of Birbaumer through the amygdala-prefrontal connectivity papers, runs through limbic and cortical territory and treats the striatum, when it appears at all, as a reward appendix. The pallidum is the output nucleus through which the basal ganglia gate approach and avoidance behavior, and the putamen is its dorsal-lateral input, associated with habit and action selection. The Groenewold team gestures toward the older proposal that social anxiety involves a motivational problem, a desire to avoid social failure, rather than only a threat detection problem, and the structural signal would fit that account, with the important proviso that fit is cheap. An enlarged pallidum and a shrunken putamen could mean that the avoidance loop has been used so heavily that it has hypertrophied, which would be an experience-dependent change and an effect of the disorder. They could equally mean that the loop was built differently to begin with, which would make the signal a cause, or a marker of risk, and the age pattern in the consortium data cuts in a direction that quietly favors the first reading, since nothing is visible in the adolescents who are closest to onset.

This is where the two papers expose each other’s blind spots. The Leiden family design is the only one of the pair that could, in principle, tell a cause from a consequence, because relatives who carry genetic liability without having years of illness would show the feature if it were a risk marker. But it is underpowered to the point that its answer is uninterpretable, and it never actually tested unaffected relatives against unrelated controls, a point the authors concede when they list the absence of general population control families. The ENIGMA data have the power to detect a difference of one tenth of a standard deviation, and no capacity to say where the difference came from. The mismatch is almost comic. One study has the right design and the wrong sample size, and the other has the right sample size and the wrong design, and neither will be repaired by a bigger version of itself. There is also a quiet tension in how each treats age. For the Leiden team, a combined generation sample of mean age 46.5 and 18.1 is a feature, because heritability is estimated across a lifespan. For the consortium, the sharp separation of adults from adolescents is the main discovery, and an effect that appears only after twenty one cannot be the stable, trait-like, genetically fixed marker that the word endophenotype promises, since a trait marker should be visible in the eight year old who will go on to develop the illness.

That last implication is the one I would press hardest on a reader who plans to work in this area. If the pallidal and putaminal differences are absent in adolescents with social anxiety and present in adults, then the candidate endophenotypes from the Leiden family study that rest on the pallidum are in trouble by the consortium’s own logic, and the conclusion that the putamen change is a scar of the disorder is in trouble by the Leiden logic if the families ever show it in unaffected young relatives. The two cannot both be right in their strong forms, and the data to adjudicate between them would be a longitudinal scanning program that follows genetically enriched, behaviorally inhibited children through the age window in which the adult signature appears. Nobody has that dataset at the scale required. Until someone does, the field is left with a question that carries real weight for how social anxiety should be conceptualized and treated. Is the striatopallidal signature of the disorder something the disorder does to a brain over a decade of avoidance, in which case early intervention is neuroprotective and the structure is a biomarker of illness burden, or something a brain brings to the disorder, in which case the signature is a window into risk that closes the moment the first symptoms have been treated?
