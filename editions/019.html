The Social Brain Dispatch

The Signal Under the Noise Floor

The largest structural brain study of social anxiety ever assembled, 1,115 patients and 2,775 controls from 37 samples on five continents, found a putamen smaller by about a tenth of a standard deviation and nothing at all in the adolescents among whom the disorder is supposed to begin. A Münster team’s white-matter model, published a week ago, predicts symptoms from wiring at r = .14 after summarizing that first study as a null, and between them the two papers leave the field holding effects too small to build a biomarker on and too consistent to dismiss.

Edition No. 19 · Frontier Neuroscience · October 5, 2026

Every structural neuroimaging study of psychiatric illness eventually has to confront the same arithmetic. If a disorder changes the brain, the change has to be large enough to survive the noise of scanners, segmentation software, medication histories, comorbid diagnoses, and the plain biological variability among healthy people, and the history of the field is a long record of that arithmetic being ignored by studies of thirty patients. Social anxiety disorder has been unusually bad on this front. Smaller studies reported a shrunken amygdala, an enlarged amygdala, a shrunken hippocampus, an enlarged hippocampus, a thin thalamus, and a fat putamen, often within a few years of each other. The recurring impression, across the imaging papers this Dispatch has read since edition one, is of a literature that learned how to generate findings long before it learned how to generate facts. The question that gives this week’s edition its stakes is therefore a plain one. If you stop trying to be clever, assemble every scanner you can get access to, and measure the brain of a socially anxious person as precisely and as unglamorously as current methods allow, is there a difference at all, and if there is, how large, where, and in whom?

Two papers, written three years apart by overlapping sets of people, answer that question from opposite ends. The first is the ENIGMA-Anxiety Working Group’s mega-analysis of subcortical volumes in social anxiety disorder (Groenewold et al., 2023), the biggest coordinated structural dataset the disorder has ever had. The second is a preregistered study from the University of Münster that appeared on September 28 of this year (Meinert et al., 2026), which gives up on regions entirely and asks whether the pattern of white-matter connections across the whole brain can predict how socially anxious a person is. One paper is built on the premise that the disorder lives in particular structures and that enough statistical power will reveal them. The other is built on the premise that structures were always the wrong unit. Read in sequence, they turn out to share more than either admits, including a quiet disagreement about what the first one found.

The people matter here, because the first paper is a product of an institution as much as of a team. ENIGMA, the Enhancing NeuroImaging Genetics through Meta-Analysis consortium that Paul Thompson and Neda Jahanshad built at the University of Southern California, began as a way of pooling genetic and imaging data across sites too small to do anything alone, and it has since grown into a federation of working groups, one per disorder, each following a shared image-processing and quality-control protocol so that results can be compared across illnesses. The anxiety disorders working group is led from two places. In Cape Town it runs through Dan Stein, whose World Mental Health analysis of social anxiety across countries this Dispatch read in edition eight, and Nynke Groenewold, who is first author here. In Leiden it runs through Nic van der Wee and Janna Marie Bas-Hoogendam, whose Leiden Family Lab on Social Anxiety Disorder scanned multigenerational families genetically enriched for the disorder and Bas-Hoogendam was first author of the earlier voxel-based mega-analysis of 174 patients that this paper extends by an order of magnitude. The author list runs to dozens of principal investigators, from Murray Stein and Luan Phan and Daniel Pine in the United States to Qiyong Gong in Chengdu and Tomas Furmark in Uppsala, whose PET work on social phobia this Dispatch read in edition three. This is what the discipline looks like when it has decided that the era of the single-lab finding is over, and the paper is as much a statement about method as about the brain. Its authors set out to learn whether a field can know anything at all about structure, and mechanism comes second.

The design is a mega-analysis in the strict sense, meaning that individual participant data rather than site-level summary statistics were pooled. Thirty-seven samples from ten countries contributed 1,115 patients and 2,775 healthy controls. The patients were 61.5 percent female with a mean age of 26.9 years (SD 12.3), the controls 55.2 percent female with a mean age of 31.9 (SD 15.6). Social anxiety disorder was established by structured interview, but the interview differed by site, among them the SCID, the MINI, the CIDI, the DAWBA, and the K-SADS, and two samples, the Philadelphia cohort (PNC) and the population-based SHIP study in Pomerania, assessed lifetime but not current diagnosis. Scans were 1.5 or 3 tesla T1-weighted images, all run through FreeSurfer under the ENIGMA protocol, which segments seven structures per hemisphere (thalamus, amygdala, hippocampus, putamen, pallidum, accumbens, and caudate) and requires visual inspection of every segmentation, after which 97.7 percent of observations survived quality control. Each volume was modeled with a random intercept for scan site and fixed effects of diagnosis, sex, age, age squared, their interactions, and intracranial volume. The authors tested a random slope for diagnosis by site and found it improved fit for only five of the fourteen regions, so they settled on the simpler model. Effects are reported as mixed-effects d, a standardized difference that corrects for the non-independence introduced by pooling, and the significance threshold was a Bonferroni correction across the fourteen regions, which sets the bar at an uncorrected p of roughly .0036.

The headline result is a single structure. Patients showed smaller bilateral putamen volume than controls, with d = −0.077 on the left (corrected p = .037) and d = −0.104 on the right (corrected p = .001). Nothing else survived in the full sample, not the amygdala, not the hippocampus, and not the thalamus that several earlier studies had pointed at. The effect was robust to excluding patients with comorbid obsessive-compulsive disorder or PTSD, although the left putamen slipped under the threshold when the comparison was restricted to current rather than lifetime diagnosis. A diagnosis-by-age interaction in the left putamen (r = −0.034, corrected p = .045) sent the authors into the age strata, and this is where the paper becomes interesting. Among adults, ages 22 to 69 (668 patients, 1,984 controls, 30 samples), the putamen effect roughly doubled to d = −0.141 on the left and −0.158 on the right, and the pallidum, which had shown nothing in the pooled data, turned out to be larger in patients, with d = +0.129 on the left (corrected p = .006) and +0.099 on the right (corrected p = .046). Among children and adolescents aged 8 to 21 (437 patients, 788 controls, 29 samples), no region showed a difference. The authors read this as meaning that adolescent patients are structurally unremarkable and that the adult pattern emerges with time, and they propose that it might aggravate with illness duration.

That reading deserves some resistance, and the resistance is arithmetic. A difference of d = 0.15 estimated from 668 patients and 1,984 controls has a standard error near 0.045, which puts it at a z of about 3.5. The same true difference estimated from 437 patients and 788 controls has a standard error near 0.060 and would sit at a z of roughly 2.5, short of the 2.9 or so that the fourteen-region correction demands. This is a back-of-envelope calculation that ignores the mixed-model adjustment, but it shows that an adolescent effect the size of the adult one would be missed more often than not. The adolescent null is therefore close to uninformative, and what the data actually support is the weaker statement that no adolescent effect larger than the adult one is visible. The interaction itself, which is the only formal test of the developmental claim, is significant for one hemisphere at a corrected p of .045. A developmental story built on that number is a hypothesis worth following and falls well short of a finding.

The clinical subgroup analyses are where the paper tries to explain why earlier studies disagreed, and they produce the most intriguing and least stable results. Patients with a comorbid anxiety disorder (404 patients, 29 samples) showed a smaller left amygdala, d = −0.145, corrected p = .017, while patients without one (615 patients) showed the bilateral putamen reduction at around −0.09 to −0.10, and among adults without comorbidity the pallidum enlargement reached d = +0.214. Medicated patients (246 patients, 20 samples) showed no corrected difference anywhere, whereas unmedicated patients (850) showed a smaller right putamen at d = −0.100. Age of onset split the sample at 13 years. Early-onset patients (240 patients, 23 samples) showed a smaller right hippocampus at d = −0.194, which grew to about −0.33 in the adult early-onset subsample, and later-onset patients showed the largest effects in the entire paper, with bilateral putamen at d near −0.31 to −0.34 and pallidum enlargement at +0.20 to +0.24. Symptom severity on the Liebowitz scale, available in 21 samples and 523 patients, correlated with no volume after correction. So the amygdala appears only in the comorbid subgroup, the hippocampus only in the early-onset subgroup, and the striatum in nearly everyone else, with no relationship to how ill a person is.

Two features of those subgroup results should make a careful reader slow down. The first is that the authors interpret the age pattern as accumulation with illness duration, yet the largest putamen effects belong to the late-onset group, whose members at any given age have by definition been ill for less time than the early-onset group. If duration drove the putamen, the effect should be bigger where duration is longer, and it is bigger where it is shorter, unless late-onset patients are substantially older, which the main text does not say. The second is that the subgroups were each compared with the same pool of controls, so the findings are not independent of one another, and the direct contrasts between patient subgroups are relegated to supplementary notes. The paper is candid about its limits, noting cross-sectional and partly retrospective designs, residual heterogeneity in field strength and FreeSurfer version, and few medicated or remitted patients. It is also candid that the effects are small. Mixed-effects d of about −0.10 in the main analysis sits beside the hippocampal effects of roughly −0.15 that the authors cite from ENIGMA work on PTSD and depression, and far below the −0.46 reported for schizophrenia. The authors note that smaller putamen with larger pallidum has not been seen in any other ENIGMA disorder, and that the larger right putamen reported in the earlier 174-patient voxel-based mega-analysis may in fact have been pallidal signal, or putamen extending across a boundary that voxel-based methods blur, and they propose vertex-wise shape analysis to settle it.

The Münster paper starts from a different diagnosis of the field’s problem. Its group sits at the Institute for Translational Psychiatry under Udo Dannlowski, an institute that has built its reputation on large, deeply phenotyped cohorts of affective disorder, and the senior author, Elisabeth Leehr, leads a research group there and is also affiliated with Marburg. The recent work of that group has concentrated on what depression and anxiety share. A 2021 systematic review by Sindermann, Redlich, Opel, Böhnlein, Dannlowski, and Leehr, which the ENIGMA paper cites, examined MRI findings in depression, anxiety, and their co-occurrence, and the present study is the empirical continuation of that framing. The Münster group is also, it should be said, a contributor to the ENIGMA paper. Dannlowski, Grotegerd, S. Meinert, Winter, Böhnlein, Schrammen, Leehr, and Sindermann all appear on the 2023 author list, and a German sample labeled TIP, the name of the cohort that supplies the 2026 data, appears in the ENIGMA sample table with 14 patients and 23 controls. The second paper is therefore a critique and an extension written from inside the consortium it is reacting to, which makes its summary of the first paper’s result all the more surprising.

The design uses 160 adults from the Transdiagnostic Investigation of Emotional Processing in Depression and Anxiety cohort, aged 18 to 65, German-speaking, right-handed, and of Caucasian descent by the study’s exclusion criteria. Sixty-four met DSM-5 criteria for social anxiety disorder, 49 of them with comorbid acute or remitted major depression, and 96 did not, of whom 49 had no lifetime psychiatric history and 47 had lifetime depression. Diagnoses came from the SCID, severity from the Liebowitz scale (mean 67.98 in the patient group, 21.45 in the others), and the patient group was significantly younger (25.1 against 29.5 years) and more depressed on the Beck inventory (14.4 against 8.4). The imaging was a 3 tesla Prisma acquisition with a 1 mm T1 and a diffusion sequence of 2 × 30 directions at b = 1000 plus five unweighted volumes, processed with the CATO toolbox using RESTORE tensor fitting and deterministic tractography on 114 nodes defined from each participant's own cortical parcellation, using the Cammoun subdivision of the Desikan-Killiany atlas. Edges required at least three streamlines. This is a respectable but not a modern diffusion protocol, a single shell and a tensor model with deterministic tracking, and anyone who has tried to resolve crossing fibers with sixty directions will know what that does to the long association tracts.

The analysis is connectome-based predictive modeling in the form implemented by NBSPredict, which couples the network-based statistic to a cross-validated machine-learning pipeline. In each of 100 outer folds, edges whose strength correlated with Liebowitz score at p < .01 were grouped into their largest connected component, the edge values in that component trained either a linear regression or a support vector regression with hyperparameters tuned in 10 inner folds, age and sex entered as covariates, and performance was the Pearson correlation between concatenated out-of-sample predictions and observed scores, tested against a permutation null. The result is a network of 46 edges, which the paper reports as 0.18 percent of the connectome, predicting symptoms with r = 0.141 (interval 0.12 to 0.20, p = .003). In the 111 participants with a psychiatric diagnosis, excluding the 49 healthy controls, the network grew to 59 edges and the correlation to r = 0.172. The network is described as a visual-temporal core, with its strongest positive edge between left lateral occipital and lingual cortex, negative edges linking fusiform, inferior temporal, and occipital regions, and weaker frontal and parietal contributions. The preregistered hub definition, requiring a node to sit in the top 15 percent for both degree and betweenness, found no hubs. A relaxed degree-only definition pointed to bilateral precuneus and right superior parietal cortex. Whole-brain graph metrics showed that patients with the diagnosis had more modules than the others (F = 6.03, p = .015, partial η² = .037), the only one of twelve global metrics to differ by group and a result that would not survive correction for twelve. Across the whole sample, higher Liebowitz scores went with more connections, longer path length, and greater small-worldness, and fewer streamlines per connection.

The honest summary is that the network explains about two percent of the variance in symptoms, and the authors say so, describing the effects as small, the network as of limited stability, and the findings as exploratory. A few details sharpen the picture. A correlation of .14 in 160 people carries a plain sampling interval running from approximately zero to about .29, and the narrower interval reported in the paper reflects the variability of repeated cross-validation within this one sample and says nothing regarding how the model would fare in a new one. The percentages for network size do not reconcile with the stated node count, since 114 nodes give 6,441 possible edges, of which 46 would be about 0.7 percent and 59 about 0.9 percent. The regression columns of the graph-metric table contain degrees of freedom and effect-size values that cannot be what the headings say. None of this is damning, and the supplement may resolve it. It does mean that the paper’s own sensitivity analysis, which concedes it could detect only medium effects (f² = .15), describes a study operating below the resolution of the effects it reports.

The more interesting issue is what the two papers say about each other. In their introduction, Meinert et al. state that large-scale ENIGMA mega-analyses found no robust evidence for alterations in subcortical volume in social anxiety disorder, and they use that to motivate a switch to networks. The 2023 abstract states that patients showed smaller bilateral putamen volume, survived family-wise correction in two hemispheres, and recovered a pallidum difference in adults, concluding that subtle alterations were detected. One could defend the Münster summary as a statement about effect size, since d of −0.10 is hardly robust in the clinical sense, but the sentence as written describes a null that the first paper does not report. The slip matters because it is the premise of the second study’s rationale. A reader who takes the introduction at its word will believe that regional structure was tried and failed, and will therefore regard a network result of r = .14 as a rescue. In fact, the regional approach returned a replicable, if tiny, striatal signature, and the network approach returned a tiny, unstable visual-temporal one, and the two do not overlap anywhere on the map.

That absence of overlap is itself the most informative fact in either paper. ENIGMA’s signal sits in the putamen and pallidum, structures long linked to reward, motivation, and habit, and the paper’s own discussion ties the putamen to reward processing in inhibited temperament and adolescent anxiety, where aberrant activation has been read as an intense desire to avoid failure in social settings. Münster’s signal sits in occipital, fusiform, and inferior temporal cortex, regions that encode faces and bodies and that the authors link to hypervigilance for social cues, with the precuneus and superior parietal cortex as attentional hubs. Neither paper reports a result in the amygdala-prefrontal circuit that anchored editions one, ten, and sixteen of this Dispatch, and neither can be said to contradict that literature, since volume and tract strength are not activation or coupling. What they do jointly suggest is that the mechanism-first logic of the functional literature has had little purchase on structure, and that the structural differences that exist, wherever they are, are not located where the functional ones are. A reasonable person could conclude that structure is simply irrelevant to the disorder. A more careful one would notice that each study is nearly blind to the other’s territory. ENIGMA’s FreeSurfer pipeline measures fourteen volumes and no connections. The Münster network is built from 114 cortical nodes, and nothing in the paper indicates that it can see whether the cortical edges it flags reach the striatum at all.

Here the conjecture the Dispatch is prepared to make, and it is only a conjecture, concerns an anatomical fact that neither paper uses. The posterior putamen and the tail of the caudate receive dense input from visual association and temporal cortex, and a network that runs through lingual, fusiform, and inferior temporal cortex is anatomically upstream of exactly the striatal territory in which ENIGMA saw its effect. If that wiring carries the disorder’s structural signature, one would expect the cortical-striatal projections to be the place where the two datasets meet, a prediction that would be tested by repeating the Münster analysis with the fourteen ENIGMA nodes added to the parcellation, or by correlating putamen volume with the visual-temporal edge strengths in the same participants. The TIP cohort has both measurements already. The two signals could just as easily be unrelated, one reflecting the volumetric consequences of years of avoidance and the other a trait-level difference in how visual information is routed, and the point of the experiment would be to say which.

The papers also differ in what each assumes about when the difference arises, and neither tests it. The ENIGMA authors, observing that adult patients differ and adolescent ones do not, lean toward a consequence account, in which the structure changes as the illness persists. The Münster authors, speculating about their network, offer both possibilities, that certain connectivity configurations predispose individuals to social sensitivity or result from repeated engagement in socially anxious thought. Both are compatible with their data because both designs are cross-sectional, and both papers end by asking for longitudinal work. That request has been made so often it has lost its force, so it is worth saying what the data would have to look like. The age at which social anxiety disorder typically begins, which in the pooled ENIGMA patients averaged around 15 years, lies in the very window where the pooled adolescent comparison found nothing. A design that scanned several thousand children before onset and followed them through it would show whether the putamen difference precedes the disorder, appears with it, or follows years behind. No such cohort has been analyzed for this question, and the adolescent null in the largest structural dataset ever assembled is, as the arithmetic above suggests, mostly an artifact of how few young patients there are.

The deeper tension between the papers is about what an effect of this size is for. ENIGMA’s answer is that a d of −0.10 is a real property of a population, to be catalogued beside the hippocampal effect in depression and PTSD and compared across disorders, and its authors have the humility to note how small the differences are. Münster’s answer is the one the biomarker literature has been giving for fifteen years, that predictive modeling with cross-validation enforces generalizability and so ought to find what group comparisons miss. Convert the two sets of numbers to a common scale and the change of method turns out to have bought very little. A pooled d of 0.08 to 0.16 corresponds to a correlation of roughly .04 to .08 and well under one percent of variance. ENIGMA’s strongest subgroup effects, near 0.3, correspond to a correlation near .15. The Münster network’s r of .141 to .172 corresponds to a d of about 0.28 to 0.35 and to two or three percent of variance, which looks larger than the pooled ENIGMA effect until one remembers that it comes from 160 people, from a pipeline tuned and tested within one sample, and from an outcome that spans health and illness at once. Whether one counts volumes in the striatum across 3,890 people or edges in a white-matter graph across 160, the structural brain of social anxiety presents as a difference that needs a large sample to see and cannot yet be used for anything clinical. The question of whether the effect is small because the disorder barely touches brain structure, or small because the disorder is several conditions whose signatures cancel in the pooled average, is the one the comorbidity and onset subgroups in ENIGMA hint at and neither study can resolve.

Which leaves a question that this Dispatch cannot answer from the literature it has read and that bears on everything in the structural half of the field. The ENIGMA data show effects that differ in sign and location depending on whether a patient has a comorbid anxiety disorder, began before or after thirteen, takes medication, or is an adult, and the Münster data show that a pooled network predicts symptoms across patients and healthy people alike with the mixed diagnosis of the sample baked in, 49 of the 64 patients having depression as well. If the disorder is heterogeneous in the way those subgroups imply, then a single pooled putamen effect and a single pooled predictive network are both averages over populations with different underlying neuroanatomy, and the small effect sizes are artifacts of the averaging. If it is homogeneous, the structural signal is genuinely tiny and the brain of social anxiety is, anatomically, almost indistinguishable from a healthy one, which would be a finding with sobering implications for two decades of structural biomarker research. Distinguishing the two would require a study with the sample size of ENIGMA, the within-person network measures of Münster, and enough clinical detail to test whether subgroups carry different structures, something no one has yet assembled. Until someone does, every structural result in this field will be consistent with both readings, and it will not be possible to say whether the field has found a signal or merely learned to measure its own noise.
