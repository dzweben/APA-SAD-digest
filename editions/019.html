The Social Brain Dispatch

The Self That Only Shows Up Under Review

A Melbourne team scanning thirty-eight young patients found that the default mode network distinguishes social anxiety from health only when people are asked what others would say about them, and a Victorian team scanning 135 adults at rest found no diagnostic difference anywhere, only a severity gradient in the same medial wall. Neither found the amygdala-prefrontal deficit this field was built on, and each paper’s design quietly explains the other’s result.

Edition No. 19 · Frontier Neuroscience · October 26, 2026

Social anxiety disorder is defined by a belief about other minds. The feared event in the diagnostic criteria is scrutiny, and the cognitive models this Dispatch read in edition 15, Rapee and Heimberg (1997) and the architectures that followed, put a mental representation of how one appears to an observer at the center of the maintaining loop. Yet more than two decades of neuroimaging have mostly asked the disorder a different question. They showed patients faces, scanned the amygdala, and counted how loudly it answered. Edition 18 followed that habit to its roots. Two recent papers from Australia change the question. Both go looking for the social anxious self in the medial cortex that builds self-representations, and they reach conclusions that look opposed on the surface and turn out to be rivals only in part. One finds a clean diagnostic signature, but only when patients are asked to imagine what other people would say about them. The other finds no diagnostic signature at all in a larger, quieter experiment, and a dose-response relation with symptom severity that appears wherever the authors did not think to expect it.

The stakes are taxonomic and practical. If social anxiety is a category with a circuit-level lesion, then the field’s old program of locating that lesion and building a treatment to repair it is on track, and the failures of replication in amygdala work are noise around a real signal. If it is a dimension, a tail of a continuous distribution of self-monitoring and sensitivity to evaluation, then group comparisons between patients and controls are a statistically awkward way of sampling the same continuum, and the field has been asking a binary question of a graded quantity for forty years. The Research Domain Criteria initiative bet on the second view, and a mechanism paper that sides with it, or with the first, shifts how every later case-control study should be designed. Edition 16 found a prefrontal deficit that vanished the moment patients were told to regulate. These two papers are the next installment in that argument, and the first genuinely different method brought to it.

The first comes from Melbourne, and from a research tradition that has spent years on a single large question, which is how the brain constructs the sense of being a particular person. Alec Jamieson, Ben Harrison, Christopher Davey and colleagues, with Lianne Schmaal, Kim Felmingham and Lisa Phillips, work between the University of Melbourne’s Department of Psychiatry and Orygen, the youth mental health research centre. Harrison and Davey belong to a group that has treated the default mode network as a mechanistic problem instead of a resting-state curiosity, and they have applied one analytic instrument repeatedly across disorders. The instrument is dynamic causal modeling, which fits a generative model of how activity in each region of a small network drives activity in the others, and asks which directed couplings differ between groups or conditions. Their earlier papers used it to describe the healthy core-self network and to describe disturbed self-appraisal in depression, and the 2023 paper in Translational Psychiatry (Jamieson et al., 2023) is the latest in that sequence, the one that moves the same machinery to social anxiety. Schmaal’s presence matters for a reason beyond authorship. She leads work in the ENIGMA consortium, the large-sample mega-analytic enterprise that has increasingly set the standard for what counts as a replicable psychiatric imaging effect, and set against that standard the DCM approach plays a different role. It resolves mechanism within a model, and it leaves to larger samples the question of how common an effect is in the population.

The experiment itself deserves a careful description, because the conclusion rests on the design. The sample was thirty-eight unmedicated people with social anxiety disorder, aged sixteen to twenty-five, recruited from outpatient clinics in Melbourne, from forty-two originally scanned, with one lost to head motion and three to poor performance on the control task. Diagnosis was made with the SCID-5 research version, and symptom severity sat in the moderate to severe range, with a mean Liebowitz Social Anxiety Scale score of 84.5 against 23.2 in controls. The comparison group was seventy-two healthy controls drawn from 122 recruits and matched on age and sex by variable-ratio matching, with no differences in IQ. The group is clinically honest in one respect that matters for interpretation, since 65.8 percent of the patients also met criteria for major depression, which is what youth clinics look like and which the authors report plainly instead of excluding.

Participants performed a self-appraisal paradigm in a 3T GE scanner, with a field of view angled to cover ventral prefrontal cortex. In blocks of five trait adjectives, each shown for five seconds, they answered one of three questions. In the direct condition the question was whether they would use the word to describe themselves. In the reflected condition it was whether other people would use it to describe them. In the baseline external-attention condition they judged whether the word contained four or more vowels. Words were taken from Anderson’s likeability norms and chosen from near the median, neither flattering nor insulting, and lists were matched and counterbalanced. The choice is revealing. It strips the stimulus of valence and isolates the act of self-reference, which is exactly what a first mechanistic study of this circuit needs and exactly what a clinical researcher might fear loses the disorder, since the patient’s pain lives in the valence. The authors concede the point in their limitations, and it recurs below.

Behaviorally the groups were close. Reaction times differed across conditions and not between groups, with reflected appraisal slower than direct appraisal and the vowel task slowest, and accuracy on the control task was about 94 percent in both groups. Patients endorsed more adjectives as self-descriptive (t(108) = -2.68, p = .008), with the reflected-condition difference borderline. In a task built on near-neutral words, a bias to say yes to the self is itself a small and interesting finding, since it is the opposite of what a global negative self-image would predict, though without valence coding the paper cannot say what the extra endorsements were.

The standard activation analysis found a default mode core in both groups, with peaks in medial prefrontal cortex at (-10, 54, 10), posterior cingulate at (-6, -50, 26), and left inferior parietal lobule at (-48, -60, 24), identified by a conjunction of self-appraisal activation and rest-fixation activation. No group difference survived whole-brain family-wise correction. At an uncorrected threshold of .001 the patients showed lower activity in left pars triangularis and premotor cortex during direct appraisal and in right pars opercularis during reflected appraisal, which the authors rightly treat as hints. This null is the setup for the paper’s argument. A mass-univariate analysis sees no diagnostic difference in how hard this network works.

The dynamic causal model then looked at how the three nodes talk to each other. Each participant’s model contained bidirectional connections among the three regions, modulation of every connection by both appraisal conditions, and a driving input into posterior cingulate. Subject-level estimates were combined in a parametric empirical Bayes model with regressors for the group mean, the effect of diagnosis, and age, followed by Bayesian model averaging, with effects retained at a posterior probability above .95. Two intrinsic differences emerged, meaning couplings that differed regardless of condition. The excitatory drive from posterior cingulate to medial prefrontal cortex was weaker in the patients, by about 0.09 Hz, and the inhibitory influence of inferior parietal cortex on medial prefrontal cortex was also weaker, by about 0.08 Hz. During reflected self-appraisal, and only then, both couplings were modulated in the opposite direction, with posterior cingulate to medial prefrontal drive stronger by 0.05 Hz and parietal inhibition of medial prefrontal cortex stronger by 0.06 Hz. Direct self-appraisal modulated nothing differently between groups. Leave-one-out cross-validation then asked whether the two intrinsic couplings carried clinical information, and they predicted trait anxiety on the STAI (r = .25, p = .004), while predictions of LSAS and state anxiety failed.

The headline reading, which the authors favor, is that the diagnosis becomes visible exactly where the disorder’s cognitive core sits. Being asked what others think of you is the condition that cognitive models such as Rapee and Heimberg’s place at the center of the pathology, and it is the only condition in which the network behaves differently. Their account of the mechanism is plausible. Posterior cingulate contributes a narrative self-representation, parietal cortex supplies perspective-taking and episodic retrieval that reflected appraisal depends on, and medial prefrontal cortex gates representations into awareness. In patients, the reflected-appraisal modulation of the posterior cingulate to medial prefrontal route resembles what the same patients show in the direct condition, which the authors read as evidence that when socially anxious people simulate another person’s view of them, they lean on their own constructed self-narrative harder than controls do.

The arithmetic invites a second, more skeptical reading, and it is worth stating because the paper’s own numbers support it. The intrinsic effects are negative by 0.09 and 0.08 Hz. The reflected-condition modulations are positive by 0.05 and 0.06 Hz. In patients, then, the task partially cancels the baseline deficit. The couplings that are weak at baseline are pulled back toward the controls’ values when the question turns to other people’s opinions. That is compatible with the authors’ story, but it is also what one would expect if the patients’ baseline were set by something like a chronic trait, and the condition-specific effect were a shift toward normal. In a task-based DCM the intrinsic parameters and the modulatory parameters are estimated jointly, and they can trade off. A diagnosis-specific finding that lives in the modulatory term alone is consistent with a trait-level difference expressed at baseline and then unevenly compensated, a reading reinforced by the fact that the only clinical variable the intrinsic couplings predicted was trait anxiety. The authors point toward this themselves, linking the intrinsic alterations to the Topography of the Anxious Self account and suggesting they may be shared across anxiety disorders. The effect sizes then sit where they must, small but real, with an out-of-sample correlation of .25 meaning roughly six percent of variance in trait anxiety, honest evidence of signal and nowhere near clinical utility.

The second paper arrives from a different Australian city and a different philosophy of what to ask of a scanner. Simone Mizzi, Mangor Pedersen, Susan Rossell, Peter Rendell, Gill Terrett, Markus Heinrichs and Izelle Labuschagne published in Translational Psychiatry in 2024 (Mizzi et al., 2024), from a collaboration across RMIT, Auckland University of Technology, Swinburne, Australian Catholic University, Queensland and Freiburg. The program belongs to Labuschagne, whose earlier work in Chicago with Luan Phan, using oxytocin to quiet amygdala reactivity to fear in generalized social anxiety, with K. Luan Phan and colleagues (Labuschagne et al., 2010), established her as a researcher interested in pharmacological levers on the social brain, and to Heinrichs at Freiburg, who built the human oxytocin and social-stress literature. Read in that light, a large resting-state study is groundwork, an attempt to decide which circuits in social anxiety merit a pharmacological probe before anyone spends a dose. The authors’ earlier systematic review of resting-state studies supplied the starting problem, small samples, short scans, ad hoc processing, and the reported direction of amygdala to frontal connectivity flipping between studies, with amygdala to frontal differences reported in nine of the eighteen studies reviewed.

The design answers that problem directly. One hundred and thirty-eight community and clinic-recruited adults were scanned and 135 retained, forty-two with social anxiety disorder and ninety-three controls, after three were excluded for mean framewise displacement above 0.5 mm. Participants were aged eighteen to fifty-five, right-handed, and free of psychotropic medication, substance misuse and neurological illness. Diagnosis required the MINI together with an LSAS of at least thirty, social anxiety had to be the primary condition, and acute depression, bipolar disorder and psychosis were exclusions, with only five patients carrying a secondary diagnosis, so the cleaner sample is the mirror image of Melbourne’s. On a Siemens 3T Trio, a multiband sequence with acceleration factor five delivered 500 volumes at a repetition time of 1020 ms over about eight and a half minutes, at 2 mm isotropic resolution, a long, fast scan chosen because test-retest reliability of connectivity improves with duration. Preprocessing used fMRIPrep, with white matter, CSF and six motion parameters regressed out, bandpass filtering at 0.01 to 0.08 Hz and 8 mm smoothing. The authors then chose eighteen seeds, the whole amygdala and its four anatomically defined subregions in each hemisphere, plus bilateral subgenual cingulate, ventromedial prefrontal cortex, precuneus and temporoparietal junction seeds, and correlated every seed with 173,843 gray matter voxels.

The group comparison was clean and null. With threshold-free cluster enhancement and 5,000 permutations, covarying age, sex and motion, no seed showed a significant difference between patients and controls, whether or not framewise displacement was included. The sub-threshold peaks, with t values of about 3.4 to 4.5, were reported anyway, among them a right basolateral amygdala to left cerebellum connection at (-18, -28, -26), t = 4.51, and a left temporoparietal junction to right anterior insula connection, t = -4.32. The motion confound is real, since controls moved more than patients (mean framewise displacement 0.20 against 0.16 mm), and the authors handled it by covariation and a rerun without it.

The dimensional analysis is where the paper earns its title. Across all 135 participants, using partial Spearman correlations with the Social Interaction Anxiety Scale and a permutation-based minimum-p threshold at .001, severity tracked connectivity in two places. Left whole-amygdala connectivity with right supramarginal gyrus at (44, -44, 36) rose with social anxiety (rho = .389), as did connectivity with left cerebellar crus II (rho = .360) and lobule VIIb (rho = .372). The same supramarginal association appeared for the left superficial amygdala subregion (rho = .361), and for no other subregion. Meanwhile connectivity between the precuneus, on both sides, and perigenual anterior cingulate cortex at (4, 38, -10) fell with severity, with rho of about -.39 and -.40. No amygdala to frontal association appeared anywhere. The authors read the superficial amygdala result as enhanced surveillance of self-relevant social information and the precuneus to cingulate result as weaker regulation of negative self-relevant emotion, and they argue that a dimensional view is supported by the pattern of continuous associations without categorical differences. They note candidly that the amygdala to parietal finding runs opposite to what Brühl and colleagues (2014) predicted in their neurofunctional model, which expected reduced rather than increased amygdala to parietal coupling.

Each of these results deserves the same critical handling as the first paper. The strongest objection to the claim of dimensionality comes from the statistics. The group test used threshold-free cluster enhancement on a two-group contrast with a significance level of .05, and the severity analysis used a different permutation scheme with a threshold of .001 and a ten-voxel minimum cluster. These are different instruments with different sensitivities, and the authors themselves warn that the first may be too stringent to detect medium effects. Further, a correlation with severity computed across a sample that mixes forty-two clinical cases with ninety-three controls is partly a group contrast in disguise, because the scale scores separate the groups by an enormous margin, with a mean SIAS of 51.6 against 18.3 and t = 17.2. The authors lean on the overlap of the two distributions, from 28 to 72 in patients and 1 to 48 in controls, to claim a continuum, but the correlation could reflect the group structure or the continuum or both. A within-group analysis, or a test of whether the slope is the same in patients and controls, would separate them, and the paper’s main analyses report none.

The subregion claim faces a resolution problem. The seeds were three-millimeter spheres placed at right-hemisphere centers such as (19, -8, -14) for the superficial subregion and (26, -5, -19) for the basolateral, which sit about nine millimeters apart, with the centromedial center only about six millimeters from the superficial. Data acquired at 2 mm and smoothed at 8 mm FWHM are blurred across exactly that distance. The authors acknowledge the overlap in their limitations, and it makes the double dissociation between subregions, with only the superficial one showing the association, a result to hold loosely until a replication that uses high-resolution acquisition and little smoothing. The comparison of subregion maps that opens the results was likewise made by eye, without a formal test. The finding that remains steady across such caveats is the precuneus to perigenual cingulate association, because it replicates across both precuneus seeds and sits inside a model the literature had already anticipated.

Now bring the two papers together, because their relation is more interesting than either conclusion alone. They converge on a place. Both land in the medial cortex that assembles representations of the self, Jamieson on the three-node default mode core and the weakened drive from posterior cingulate into medial prefrontal cortex, Mizzi on the weakened coupling between precuneus, the posteromedial neighbor of the posterior cingulate, and the perigenual cingulate, a ventral stretch of the same medial wall. Neither paper finds the amygdala to prefrontal deficit that Etkin and Wager (2007) made the canonical story of the anxiety disorders and that edition 16 interrogated. In a carefully matched study with a fast scan and a clinically clean sample, the amygdala connects to prefrontal cortex in patients about as it does in controls. Mizzi’s authors suggest the amygdala to prefrontal effect may need a threat on the screen to appear, which would make it a task-evoked circuit that rest cannot reveal. Taken together, the two papers move the locus of the disorder’s measurable difference from where danger is detected toward where the self is represented, a shift that Koban and colleagues (2023), whose work on brain mediators of biased self-perception edition 2 read, had already begun from the learning side.

They are in tension over where that difference lives. Jamieson reports a diagnostic effect, statistically confined to one task condition and arguably an echo of a baseline trait, and Mizzi reports no diagnostic effect and a severity gradient. Read together the findings suggest that the network under study carries a graded trait, the intrinsic coupling that predicted trait anxiety in Melbourne and the precuneus to cingulate gradient in Mizzi’s sample, and that the apparent diagnostic specificity appears only when a task forces the system to operate under social threat. The categorical story needs a task, and the dimensional story needs rest. That is a sharp, testable claim, and no one has tested it. It predicts that the precuneus to perigenual connectivity of Mizzi’s sample should correlate with the intrinsic parameters of Jamieson’s model when the same people supply both, and that the reflected-appraisal modulation should be the only parameter that discriminates patients from controls once the intrinsic trait component is regressed out.

Each paper also exposes the other’s blind spot. Jamieson’s sample, young, clinic-recruited and two-thirds comorbid with depression, carries the disorder as it is lived, and the intrinsic default mode alterations it reports are exactly what the depression literature from the same laboratory would predict, which leaves open whether the drive deficit belongs to social anxiety or to the depression riding along with it. Mizzi’s sample, cleaned of comorbidity and medication, is the sample in which that confound cannot operate, and it still finds a medial-wall effect, which is the best argument that the circuit is not merely a depression artifact. In the other direction, Mizzi’s resting design cannot ask what the network does under evaluation, and its own authors say that the absence of threat may hide fear-circuit effects, while Jamieson’s design supplies the evaluative condition but with deliberately neutral words, so that neither paper has put a valenced judgment from an imagined critic into a scanner and watched this circuit. The adjectives were chosen to sit at the median of likeability. The disorder’s fear lives in the tails.

That leaves a question that matters for everything the field does next. If the medial self-network carries a trait-like gradient that only becomes a diagnostic difference when the self is placed under imagined evaluation, then what is it that treatment changes? Edition 13 ended on the observation that nobody has scanned the therapy that works with the method that watches the brain change. Jamieson and colleagues propose that default mode effective connectivity could predict response to cognitive behavioral therapy and normalize with success. The two papers imply a more demanding version. Therapy might shift the baseline coupling, which would make it a trait intervention, or it might shift only the task-evoked modulation, leaving the baseline gradient intact, in which case the patient gets better at the moment of evaluation while remaining, in the circuit’s own terms, the same person. Those are different diseases and different treatments, and so far the field has never had a design capable of telling them apart.
