The Social Brain Dispatch

The Dread Before the Face

In 2019 a Münster group pointed a dummy camera at nineteen patients and found the bed nucleus of the stria terminalis firing harder in the first second of waiting. Six years later a Maryland team put sixty-six teenagers through a far stricter version of the same wait and found the same structure doing less with threat and more with safety as symptoms climbed. The second amygdala has finally entered the social anxiety literature, and its first two reports point in opposite directions.

Edition No. 19 · Frontier Neuroscience · November 12, 2026

Almost everything this Dispatch has read about the socially anxious brain was measured at the wrong moment. Edition three’s Birbaumer and Tillfors studies, edition ten’s Stein and Klumpp papers, edition sixteen’s connectivity work, all of them scanned people while a face was on the screen or a speech was under way. The clinical phenomenon lives somewhere else. Ask a patient where the disorder costs them and the answer is the three days before the presentation, the walk down the corridor toward the seminar room, the decision on Thursday not to go on Saturday. Avoidance, the behavior that turns a temperament into an impairment, is by definition a response to an encounter that has not happened. A neuroscience of social anxiety that only images the encounter has been studying the part of the illness the patient has already organized a life around escaping.

The reason for the gap is partly technical and partly inherited. Edition eighteen traced how LeDoux’s rat circuit gave the field an amygdala built for a discrete cue that predicts an imminent shock. That preparation has a sibling the clinical literature mostly ignored. Michael Davis, working at Yale and then Emory on fear-potentiated startle, spent the 1990s showing that lesions of the central nucleus abolished startle potentiation to a short, explicit cue and left intact the slower potentiation produced by bright light or by long, diffuse contexts, while lesions of the bed nucleus of the stria terminalis did the reverse. With David Walker and, on the human side, Christian Grillon at the National Institute of Mental Health, Davis turned this into a doctrine of two systems, a phasic one for fear centered on the central amygdala and a sustained one for anxiety centered on the bed nucleus. The Research Domain Criteria matrix later enshrined the split as acute threat versus potential threat. If the doctrine is right, then a disorder defined by dread of what might happen should be a bed nucleus disorder, and twenty years of amygdala imaging in social anxiety has been looking one synapse upstream of the action.

The bed nucleus is a miserable structure to image. It sits at the head of the stria terminalis, wrapped around the anterior commissure, bordered by the caudate, the nucleus accumbens, the internal capsule, and the lateral ventricle, and its supracommissural portion occupies something on the order of a tenth of a cubic centimeter per hemisphere. At conventional three millimeter voxels with a six millimeter smoothing kernel, a bed nucleus signal is mostly a blend of its neighbors. The human literature became possible only when Jennifer Blackford’s group at Vanderbilt published a manually segmented probabilistic mask (Theiss and colleagues, 2017), and it is a small, telling fact that both papers in this edition rest on that same mask. They share an anatomical definition, a paradigm lineage, and a clinical target. They share almost nothing else, including their answer.

The first paper comes out of Thomas Straube’s Institute of Medical Psychology and Systems Neuroscience at the University of Münster. Straube made his name in Jena in the early 2000s with a run of tightly designed fMRI studies of spider phobia and social phobia, the best known of which showed that amygdala and insula responses to angry faces in social phobics depended on whether the task made the emotion relevant. He is a clinician-experimentalist of a recognizably German type, loyal to diagnosed patient groups, disorder-specific provocation, and modest samples run with care. By the mid 2010s his laboratory had committed to a program that was almost taxonomic in ambition. Take the Davis dichotomy, build an fMRI model with one regressor for the first moments after an anticipation cue and another for the whole anticipation interval, and run it through the anxiety disorders one diagnosis at a time. Anna Luisa Münsterkötter did spider phobia, Leonie Brinkmann did panic disorder and post-traumatic stress disorder, Christine Buff did generalized anxiety disorder. In each, patients showed more sustained bed nucleus activity than controls while waiting for something aversive. Benedikt Figel’s 2019 paper, with Brinkmann and Buff as second and third authors, is the social anxiety entry in that series. It was meant to complete a set.

Figel and colleagues recruited 22 patients and 22 controls and analyzed 19 patients (12 women, mean age 29.5) and 21 controls (13 women, mean age 27.1) after excluding three patients with severe comorbid depression and one control with elevated questionnaire scores. Diagnosis was by structured clinical interview with social anxiety disorder as the primary diagnosis; five patients had current major depression, three were on antidepressants, and the patient group averaged 71 on the Liebowitz scale against 9 for controls. The provocation was simple and clever. Participants were told that a camera mounted on the head coil, in fact a dummy, would at certain times record their faces so that two experimenters could judge how much they blushed. A triangle or circle at trial onset announced whether the camera would come on at some point or stay off. The wait then lasted three, five, ten, or sixteen seconds, unpredictably, before a symbol declared the camera live or dead for ten seconds. There were eleven trials of each kind, seven of them at the full sixteen seconds.

The manipulation worked on experience. In ratings collected after the scan on nine-point scales, patients found the camera cue more arousing and more anxiety-provoking than the neutral cue and more so than controls did, with group by condition interactions of F(1,38) = 14.00 for arousal and 16.77 for anxiety, while controls did not distinguish the cues at all. The imaging model put two regressors on the anticipation interval, a phasic one confined to the first second after cue onset and a sustained one spanning the whole wait, and extracted mean estimates from a cytoarchitectonic centromedial amygdala mask and the Theiss bed nucleus mask, each split by hemisphere.

The predicted result did not appear. On the sustained regressor, both the left and the right bed nucleus responded more to camera anticipation than to neutral anticipation across everyone (F(1,38) = 4.67, p = .037, and F(1,38) = 12.79, p = .001), which is a respectable confirmation that the human bed nucleus tracks uncertain social threat over seconds. Patients and controls did not differ. The group by condition interaction was null in all four regions, and the one diagnosis in Straube’s series most obviously defined by anticipatory dread turned out to be the one in which sustained bed nucleus activity failed to separate patients from the healthy. What separated them was the phasic regressor. In that first second, patients showed a larger camera-minus-neutral difference than controls in the left central amygdala (F(1,38) = 5.67, p = .022) and the left bed nucleus (F(1,38) = 8.27, p = .007). Those convert to partial eta squared values of roughly .13 and .18. Whole-brain permutation analysis found no group differences on either regressor, and within patients the neural differences did not correlate with the ratings.

The authors read this as evidence for a profile specific to social anxiety, a fast salience response in both divisions of the extended amygdala without the prolonged bed nucleus engagement seen in other diagnoses. That reading deserves more suspicion than the paper gives it. The analysis ran eight group by condition tests, four regions by two regressors, each at an uncorrected alpha of .05. A Bonferroni threshold for eight tests is .00625, and the strongest finding in the paper misses it. The effects are left-lateralized with no right-sided companion and no hypothesis that predicted laterality. The phasic regressor is a one-second event estimated from eleven trials per condition at a repetition time of 2.08 seconds, in the same model as a sustained regressor that begins at the identical moment, and after a temporal low-pass filter. The data were smoothed at six millimeters before a mask smaller than the kernel was applied, so the “bed nucleus” estimate carries caudate and accumbens signal by construction. And the phasic bed nucleus effect was the hypothesis nobody wrote down. The introduction predicted phasic amygdala and sustained bed nucleus differences, and the paper obtained one of two. A forgiving reader calls the phasic bed nucleus finding a discovery. A stricter one calls it the survivor of a forking analysis in nineteen patients. I think the durable contributions of Figel and colleagues are the paradigm, which is the best social-evaluative anticipation probe the field had at that date because it involves a real threat of scrutiny of the participant’s own body, and the null, which the authors report honestly and then spend their discussion trying to explain away.

The second paper comes from a laboratory that would have predicted that null. Alexander Shackman trained at Wisconsin with Richard Davidson and then worked with Ned Kalin on the largest nonhuman primate imaging studies of anxious temperament ever run, the fluorodeoxyglucose PET series in which hundreds of young rhesus monkeys faced a human intruder’s averted profile and in which metabolism in both the central nucleus and the bed nucleus tracked freezing and cortisol. Monkeys do not respect the Davis dichotomy, and Shackman came out of that work convinced the dichotomy was wrong. His 2016 essay with Andrew Fox in the Journal of Neuroscience argued that the central nucleus and the bed nucleus are better understood as one functional unit, the central extended amygdala, with both divisions responding to both certain and uncertain threat, and that the clean double dissociation in the rodent startle literature had hardened into a human taxonomy on thin evidence. His Affective and Translational Neuroscience Laboratory at the University of Maryland then built the instrument to test the claim, the Maryland Threat Countdown, a paradigm that crosses threat against safety and temporally certain against uncertain anticipation with shock-reinforced trials, analyzed with spatially unsmoothed data in anatomically defined regions. Juyoen Hur, then a postdoctoral fellow in the lab and now on the faculty at Yonsei University in Seoul, was first author of the 2020 adult paper showing that in healthy adults the two divisions were statistically indistinguishable in their response to uncertain threat.

Hur and colleagues (2025) is that program’s move into developmental psychopathology, and the author list shows how it was made. Andres De Los Reyes, whose Maryland clinic has spent fifteen years on the measurement of adolescent social anxiety and on the meaning of disagreement among informants, supplied the assessment tradition. The paper’s stance is dimensional on principle. Sixty-six adolescents aged 13 to 17 (mean 15.4, 61 percent female, half of them adolescents of color) were recruited through separate advertisements aimed at shy and at untroubled teenagers so as to fill the whole range of the abbreviated Social Phobia and Anxiety Inventory for Children. Scores ran from 0 to 21.3; half the sample exceeded the screening cutoff for probable disorder, and 24 met criteria for social anxiety disorder on the MINI interview administered by a masters-level clinician without the parent in the room. Fourteen of those 24 carried another diagnosis, most often generalized anxiety (12) or major depression (8). All were unmedicated. Seventy-eight were enrolled and twelve were lost to early termination, poor rating compliance, or motion.

The task is a social translation of the countdown. A background color told participants throughout each trial whether the trial would end in threat or safety. On certain trials a descending count of integers ran for eighteen seconds; on uncertain trials a random stream of integers ran for between eight and thirty seconds, averaging eighteen. Threat trials ended with a 1.8 second photograph of an angry young adult paired with a hostile recorded remark from a voice actor matched for sex and race, lines such as a flat statement that nobody wants the listener there. Safety trials ended with a happy face and an innocuous sentence about soccer or the weather. Images were acquired at a one second repetition time with multiband acceleration and 2.2 millimeter slices, registered with field-map correction, and analyzed without smoothing in the central nucleus and the Theiss bed nucleus masks. Anticipation was modeled with variable-duration boxcars, and physiological and independent-component nuisance signals were regressed out. Certain-safety anticipation served as the implicit baseline, a choice that will matter shortly.

Three results carry the paper. First, the task produced distress where it should. Ratings were higher for threat than safety (F(1,64) = 47.39, partial eta squared .43) and for uncertain than certain anticipation (F(1,64) = 18.18, partial eta squared .22), and more anxious adolescents showed a larger threat-minus-safety gap (interaction F(1,64) = 7.81, partial eta squared .11). Second, when the faces and voices actually arrived, the extended amygdala responded more to hostile than to benign stimuli (F(1,64) = 8.03, partial eta squared .11) and that response had no relation to social anxiety whatever. In a sample enriched for diagnosed adolescents, with clean anatomy and a multimodal threat, the canonical finding of this field, an extended amygdala that overreacts to hostile faces in proportion to symptoms, was absent. The authors note the precedent of Ziv and colleagues’ null in 67 unmedicated adult patients and move on. I would linger. Every edition of this Dispatch that leaned on symptom-proportional amygdala hyperreactivity should register that the most careful region-of-interest study yet run in adolescents could not find it.

Third, the anticipation data. Across the sample, uncertain threat engaged the extended amygdala more than certain threat (t(64) = 2.56) or uncertain safety (t(64) = 2.07), with no difference between divisions. Social anxiety then entered through a condition by region by anxiety interaction (F(2,128) = 4.75, p = .01, partial eta squared .07). The central nucleus was unrelated to symptoms in every condition. In the bed nucleus, more severe social anxiety went with less activation during uncertain threat anticipation (t(62) = −2.52, p = .01) and more activation during uncertain safety anticipation (t(62) = 2.34, p = .02), and both associations survived control for central nucleus activity. Expressed as a single number, the bed nucleus difference between uncertain threat and uncertain safety correlated with symptom severity at r = −.38 (p = .002). The association held after partialling out in-scanner distress ratings and after partialling out diagnostic status. Voxelwise analyses found nothing related to symptoms anywhere in the brain.

The concept this result invokes is one the Dispatch has met from another direction. Edition seven followed Lissek’s and then Duits’s meta-analyses of fear conditioning to the conclusion that anxious patients are distinguished less by excessive responding to the danger cue than by a failure to withhold responding to the safety cue. Edition two read Grupe and Nitschke’s uncertainty model, which Hur and colleagues cite as their interpretive frame, and which holds that uncertainty inflates threat expectancy and that anxious people discriminate threat from safety poorly. The Maryland finding is that conditioning result relocated to the bed nucleus and to social anticipation in adolescents, and it carries a specific and somewhat unsettling claim. The anxious teenager’s problem, on this account, is a bed nucleus that treats waiting for a smile much as it treats waiting for an insult.

Before accepting that, three features of the design need pressure. The first is the baseline. Each anticipation estimate is a contrast against certain-safety anticipation, so a positive association between symptoms and “uncertain safety” activation is equally an association between symptoms and a larger uncertain-minus-certain difference on safe trials. That is a narrower statement than heightened reactivity to safety, and it cannot be separated from individual differences in the baseline condition itself. The second is the nature of safety. A safety trial here ends with a stranger smiling and speaking. For a field that has taken fear of positive evaluation seriously since Weeks and Heimberg, an approaching friendly stranger of unknown arrival time is hardly a neutral event for a socially anxious sixteen-year-old. The authors anticipate the objection and answer that symptom severity was unrelated to the extended amygdala response once the happy face appeared. That answer assumes anticipation and receipt share a mechanism, which is the very assumption the paper’s own central finding contradicts. The third is the size and provenance of the effect. The study was not preregistered, the critical interaction sits at p = .01 in 66 participants, the confidence interval on the headline correlation runs from .15 to .57 in absolute value, and the blunted response to uncertain threat is the opposite of what the introduction’s review of the preclinical literature would lead a reader to expect. An honest summary is that Hur and colleagues have a well-measured, theoretically legible, unreplicated moderate effect whose direction surprised them.

Set the two papers side by side and the first thing to say is that they agree on more than their abstracts suggest. In both, the bed nucleus responds over many seconds to uncertain social threat in everyone, patient or control, anxious or calm. In both, the size of that sustained threat response fails to increase with social anxiety. Figel reports it as a null group interaction and treats it as a puzzle; Hur reports a negative slope and treats it as half of a discrimination deficit. Hur and colleagues, summarizing the Münster study in their introduction, mention that its patients showed numerically smaller sustained responses and that no group differences were found. They omit the phasic effect entirely, which is the only positive patient finding Figel reported. The omission is defensible on the statistical grounds I gave above, yet it means the two papers have never actually been made to confront each other.

The confrontation is instructive because each design is blind exactly where the other sees. The Münster model can ask about the first second and the Maryland model cannot, since a single boxcar across an eighteen second countdown assigns onset transients and sustained plateaus to one number. If socially anxious people mount a brief orienting burst to any cue that announces social evaluation and then fail to sustain a differentiated response, a boxcar would register the combination as blunting. Hur’s one second sampling rate is fast enough to test this in the existing public data, and nobody has. The Maryland design, for its part, can ask what the safety condition is doing, and the Münster design cannot, since its neutral condition was a camera that stays off and it reported only the difference score. Figel’s patients might have shown an elevated bed nucleus response to the neutral cue that the subtraction concealed, which is precisely what Hur’s adolescents showed. The two findings that look contradictory, more phasic activity in adult patients and less differentiated sustained activity in anxious adolescents, could be two partial views of one response profile. They could equally be a developmental difference between a fifteen-year-old early in the course of the disorder and a twenty-nine-year-old with a decade of avoidance behind them, or a difference between threat to the self, a camera on one’s own blushing face, and threat from another, a hostile stranger on a screen. Three variables changed at once between these studies and neither can say which mattered.

What the pair does settle, I think, is the fate of the tidy version of the Davis doctrine in this disorder. The prediction that social anxiety would show up as exaggerated sustained bed nucleus activity during uncertain social threat has now been tested twice, in a diagnosed adult sample and in a dimensional adolescent one, with the same anatomical mask, and it failed both times. Shackman’s unitary extended amygdala fares only a little better, since his own laboratory’s data dissociate the two divisions the moment symptoms enter the model, with the central nucleus silent and the bed nucleus carrying the whole effect. The field is left with a structure that plainly matters to social anticipation and a clinical signal in it that looks like lost contrast and bears little resemblance to the excess gain everyone went looking for. That shifts the question from how loud the alarm is to what the system has learned about which waits are safe, and learning is a longitudinal matter.

Which leads to the question neither cross-section can touch. An adolescent whose bed nucleus fails to distinguish an approaching insult from an approaching greeting might have arrived with that indifference, as temperament, the inhibited toddler of edition six grown into a teenager for whom every unfamiliar person was always a possible threat. Or the indifference might be the residue of avoidance itself. A person who declines most uncertain social encounters never collects the evidence that most of them end benignly, and a circuit denied that evidence has no reason to tell the two kinds of waiting apart. On the first account, diminished discrimination precedes the disorder and could predict it in the inhibited children Nathan Fox, thanked in the Maryland acknowledgments, has followed for decades. On the second, it follows the disorder and should recover with exposure, which is at bottom a procedure for forcing the collection of safety evidence. The two accounts imply different studies, different treatment targets, and different ages at which to intervene. Until someone images the wait before the face in the same children twice, a few years apart, with avoidance measured in between, the most interesting new finding in the neuroscience of social anxiety will remain a correlation that cannot say whether it is describing a cause or a scar.
