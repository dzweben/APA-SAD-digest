The Social Brain Dispatch

The Network That Remembers, and the Control That Cannot Look Away

Two recent studies, one scanning the idle brain of adults with and without a history of childhood maltreatment and one scanning the choosing brain of students facing a human opponent, converge on the same strip of left prefrontal cortex and disagree about almost everything else, and the disagreement shows that a social anxiety group effect may be the average of two opposed ones.

Edition No. 19 · Frontier Neuroscience · November 9, 2026

Every social anxiety imaging paper this Dispatch has read since June eventually asks what the amygdala did. Edition one opened with Etkin and Wager’s (2007) meta-analysis and edition three with the small, mismatched studies behind the amygdala’s founding claim (Birbaumer et al., 1998). The question has been productive and it has also been a kind of anchor. It assumes that the disorder has a signature, a stable difference between the brain of a person with social anxiety disorder and the brain of a person without it, which a sufficiently large sample will eventually resolve. Two papers published in the last two years put pressure on that assumption from opposite directions, and neither of them is mainly about the amygdala. One is a resting-state study from Tübingen that finds the signature changes sign depending on what happened to a person in childhood. The other is a decision-making study from Shenzhen that finds the prefrontal cortex of socially anxious people working at a different task than the one the experimenters thought they had set.

The stakes are practical. If the neural signature of social anxiety disorder is conditional on developmental history, then case-control contrasts are averaging over subgroups whose brains differ in opposite directions, and the field’s long record of contradictory resting-state results stops being noise and becomes data. If the prefrontal system that is supposed to regulate social threat is instead busy monitoring other minds, then the standard model of the disorder as a failure of top-down control has the arrow pointing the wrong way. Both claims are strong, both papers are modest in sample size, and reading them side by side is the fastest way to see where each one is exposed.

Begin with Tübingen. The paper by Leypoldt, Wiegand, Munk, Drohm, Fallgatter, Nieratschker, and Kreifelts (2025) comes out of the department of psychiatry and psychotherapy at the University of Tübingen, a center for neuroimaging in psychiatry whose chair, Andreas Fallgatter, is among its senior authors, and which has become a partner site of the German Center for Mental Health. Benjamin Kreifelts, the corresponding author, is a psychiatrist best known for earlier work on how the brain fuses voices and faces into a single emotional judgment, which is to say that his training is in how social signals are integrated across senses rather than in the amygdala-threat tradition. Vanessa Nieratschker leads work on the molecular embedding of stress, and her presence on the paper marks the group’s larger project, which is to ask how adversity in childhood gets written into the biology of anxiety. Ariane Wiegand, who now also holds a Max Planck fellowship in precision psychiatry, led the precursor study that this paper explicitly extends, an activation experiment in an overlapping sample showing that early life adversity moderated left dorsolateral prefrontal responses to anxiety-relevant stimuli. The present paper is the resting-state sequel, and it was built to answer a specific complaint. Resting-state studies of social anxiety disorder have reported both higher and lower connectivity in amygdala, dorsolateral prefrontal cortex, anterior cingulate, and precuneus, and the authors, citing a systematic review of that literature, propose that no one has measured the variable that decides which direction the effect takes.

The sample was 120 German-speaking adults of European descent, aged 19 to 48, scanned at 3 tesla on a Siemens Prisma for ten minutes of eyes-closed rest with a multiband sequence at 2 mm isotropic resolution and a repetition time of 1.5 seconds. Forty-nine met DSM-IV criteria for social anxiety disorder on the German structured clinical interview, with severity indexed by the Liebowitz Social Anxiety Scale. Early life adversity was defined retrospectively from the Childhood Trauma Questionnaire, with participants counted as high-adversity if any single subscale exceeded its threshold, at 15 for emotional neglect, 13 for emotional abuse, 10 for physical abuse or neglect, and 8 for sexual abuse. Crossing diagnosis with adversity produced four groups: 49 low-adversity controls, 22 high-adversity controls, 30 low-adversity patients, and 19 high-adversity patients. The Liebowitz means for those groups were about 16, 23, 68, and 75, so the high-adversity patients were slightly but not dramatically more symptomatic than the low-adversity ones. The cell of 19 is the one on which the paper’s most arresting claims rest, and it deserves to be kept in view.

The analytic design is where the paper earns its seriousness. The authors did what the field usually does and ran region-to-region connectivity among fourteen literature-derived regions of interest, and found no diagnosis-by-adversity interaction at all. They then ran seed-based correlations from sixteen seeds with a cluster-forming threshold of p below .001 and Bonferroni correction across seeds, and again found essentially nothing beyond a left dorsolateral seed carried over from the precursor study, with the amygdala negative even under small-volume correction. What worked was a voxel-wise multivariate pattern analysis of whole-brain connectivity, which does not require anyone to say in advance where the interaction lives. It returned three clusters, a left calcarine and precuneus cluster at MNI coordinates of about −26, −60, 12 (70 voxels, FDR-corrected p of .002), a subgenual anterior cingulate cluster at 6, 10, −12 (65 voxels, p of .002), and a left middle frontal cluster at −34, 8, 36 (42 voxels, p of .016). Post hoc seed analyses from those three nodes produced a network of 36 connections, concentrated in visual, pre- and postcentral, temporal, and inferior parietal cortex. The interaction survived adjustment for trait anxiety and depression in every one of the 36 connections, with F(1,116) of at least 14.2.

The pattern across the four groups is the substantive result. Low-adversity controls showed mostly positive subgenual connectivity and negative calcarine connectivity. High-adversity controls flipped that, with mostly negative subgenual and calcarine connectivity and positive middle frontal connectivity. Patients with low adversity looked like low-adversity controls, which is to say that in the absence of maltreatment the disorder left almost no mark on the resting network. Patients with high adversity departed from their matched controls in the opposite direction from the way adversity had moved controls, showing subgenual hyperconnectivity and an almost complete absence of visual cortex coupling. The same logic held when adversity was treated as a continuum, with the slope of connectivity on Childhood Trauma Questionnaire severity differing in sign between patients and controls for every connection, driven by emotional abuse and emotional neglect (29 of 36 connections each) rather than physical abuse (none) or sexual abuse (one). The authors read the controls as compensated and the patients as decompensated, and read the visual-precuneus disconnection as possibly related to dissociation. That reading is interpretive and the paper does not test it, but the descriptive result is clear enough to be uncomfortable. Adversity and diagnosis do not add. They cross.

There are real reasons for caution, and the authors list most of them. Maltreatment is measured retrospectively by questionnaire, with no information on timing or duration, and nothing about loss, economic hardship, or parental illness. The regions of interest were chosen from a small literature. The sample is young and not balanced by sex. Heart rate variability, which is lower in social anxiety and could contaminate BOLD connectivity, was not recorded. The paper reports F statistics and p values without standardized effect sizes, so the reader cannot judge how much variance an interaction in a cell of 19 actually carries. A further problem is structural. The multivariate pattern analysis found its clusters by searching for the interaction, and the post hoc network was then assembled from seeds placed at the winning clusters, so the clean five-pattern taxonomy of the results describes a map drawn after the territory was selected. The overlap with the precursor study’s dorsolateral finding is reassuring, but the two samples overlap, so it is not independent replication.

Now Shenzhen. Li, Van Dam, Wang, Luo, and Xu (2026) come from a tradition in which the social animal is studied in the economic laboratory. The last two authors, Yuejia Luo and Pengfei Xu, work in a Chinese social neuroscience program that has, among other things, mapped how prefrontal cortex gates social influence on risky choice (Lin et al., 2022), and their paper inherits that program’s question and tools. Nicholas Van Dam is a clinical neuroscientist whose career has been spent on the measurement problem in anxiety and depression, and his name on the paper signals concern about what a self-report cutoff actually samples. The paper itself says little about its authors, so the standing sketched here is drawn from their publication record. The conceptual lineage is clear from the citations. Klucharev and colleagues (2009) showed that conformity is carried by a reinforcement-learning prediction-error signal, Chung and colleagues (2015) that social signals of safety and risk shift observers’ choices asymmetrically, and in the anxiety literature Feng and colleagues (2018) reported aberrant conformity in social anxiety disorder while Schmid, Kleiman, and Amodio (2015) described reactive rather than proactive prefrontal control in socially anxious people.

The sample is fifty-five adults, mean age 20.2, selected from 346 sign-ups by extreme Liebowitz scores. Those above 60 formed the high social anxiety group (n of 28) and those below 30 the low group (n of 27), with no psychiatric or neurological history reported, so this is a dimensional extreme-groups design in a student population, not a clinical one. Two participants did not finish and six were dropped for saying they did not believe the other players were real, a manipulation check that deserves credit because the entire design depends on the opponent being believed to be human. Participants made 216 choices between a safe payoff and a gamble that paid 64 yuan or nothing, played against one of four faces, each carrying a mild twenty-five percent disgust expression, or against a labeled computer. On every trial four red dots showed what four other players had chosen, which could be mostly safe, mostly risky, or split two and two. One randomly drawn trial determined payment. The factorial structure, group by competitor type by others’ choices, was scanned at 3 tesla in four runs.

The behavioral story is consistent and specific. Using a fitted certainty equivalent, the authors define social approach as the difference in indifference point between human and computer opponents. Low-anxiety participants gambled more against humans, with indifference points of 32.4 against 30.2 for computers (F of 5.60, p of .022), while high-anxiety participants did not differ across opponents (F of 1.53, p of .221), and the group by opponent interaction was significant (F of 6.53, p of .014). Safe choices showed the same shape, with a group by opponent interaction of F of 8.24 (p of .006) and a between-group difference only against humans (t of −2.02, p of .049), none against computers (t of .58). That last result is the paper’s answer to the obvious objection that socially anxious people are simply risk averse. They are risk averse in the presence of a person. Conformity was the third outcome. A three-way interaction (F of 7.75, p of .007) arose because high-anxiety participants conformed less than low-anxiety ones when the majority chose risky against a human (.46 against .545, p of .026), and within the high group conformity to safe majorities was strong while conformity to risky majorities was not, a difference of .670 against .460 (F of 15.99).

A hierarchical drift diffusion model then asked where in the decision process the difference lives. The best-fitting model had a starting-point bias by others’ choices and group, with a deviance information criterion of 14,058.71, ahead of competitor-by-group (14,396.06), the full three-way model (14,079.26), and any model allowing drift rate to carry the effect (30,849.68 for drift alone). A decision starting closer to the safe boundary, rather than a slower accumulation of evidence, is what separated the groups, and in the risky-majority human condition the posterior probability was .999 that the high-anxiety starting point sat closer to safe. The reading is that the socially anxious participant arrives at the choice already leaning, before the evidence about the majority has had any chance to speak. The interpretive weight on a starting point is large, and a skeptic will note that z can absorb prior beliefs, response biases, and expectations about evaluation, which the model cannot tell apart.

The imaging result is the odd one, and the paper is straightforward about it. Whole-brain contrasts of human against computer opponents recruited the expected social machinery, with fusiform gyrus, amygdala, precuneus, and dorsolateral prefrontal cortex stronger for humans at confirmation of the opponent, superior parietal lobule and temporoparietal junction at decision, and amygdala and orbitofrontal cortex at feedback. The group-sensitive effect was a three-way interaction in left dorsolateral prefrontal cortex, significant after small-volume correction (peak FWE p of .006, cluster p of .001). Reading the betas against the behavior is the instructive step. In low-anxiety participants facing a human, dorsolateral activity was lowest when the majority chose safe (.715) and higher with mixed and risky majorities (1.193 and 1.216, F of 4.57, p of .010), so the region tracked the direction of the group. In high-anxiety participants facing a human it tracked nothing. The same tracking appeared in the high-anxiety group, but against the computer, where activity rose from .863 to 1.213 to 1.556 across safe, mixed, and risky majorities (F of 5.03, p of .010). Put plainly, the high-anxiety prefrontal cortex did the computation about the group’s choices for the machine and declined to do it for the person.

The connectivity analysis supplies the second half of the story. Psychophysiological interaction with the dorsolateral cluster as seed showed that in human trials the coupling with temporoparietal junction was positive in the high-anxiety group and negative in the low-anxiety group, with values of 1.377 against −.618 under safe majorities (F of 7.12, p of .010) and 2.365 against −.311 under risky majorities (F of 10.62, p of .002), and no difference when the majority was split. The authors take this to indicate an overengaged mentalizing system coupled to an inefficiently engaged control system. That is a coherent story, and the paper reports honestly where it frays. The correlation between dorsolateral betas and the diffusion-model starting point was .25 (p of .193), and exploratory correlations between connectivity and conformity were near −.3 and not significant in either group. The behavior, the model, the activation, and the coupling all sit in one paper pointing in one direction, yet none of the links among them has been shown within participants. With 55 people, the sensitivity analysis the authors give, 80 percent power at f of about .14, applies to the omnibus tests, not to the brain-behavior bridge the story requires.

Set the two papers next to each other and the first convergence is anatomical and slightly eerie. Leypoldt’s middle frontal node at −34, 8, 36 and Li’s dorsolateral cluster are both left lateral prefrontal territory, and both papers locate the group difference in how that territory couples with posterior regions, visual and sensorimotor cortex in one case and the temporoparietal junction in the other. Neither found the amygdala to carry the effect. Both also find that the aggregate disorder label leaves something on the table. Leypoldt’s result is that diagnosis predicts connectivity only conditionally on adversity. Li’s is that diagnostic-level anxiety predicts prefrontal tracking only conditionally on whether the opponent is a person. In each case the main effect that case-control logic hunts for is the wrong object, and the interaction is the finding.

The tension is equally instructive. Leypoldt finds that the nominal disorder in the absence of maltreatment is nearly invisible in the resting network, with low-adversity patients resembling low-adversity controls. Li finds large behavioral and neural group differences in a sample that has no reported history measure and that was screened only on a questionnaire. If Leypoldt is right, a good fraction of Li’s high-anxiety group should have a resting architecture indistinguishable from their low-anxiety peers, and the dorsolateral-temporoparietal hypercoupling Li reports should be concentrated in whichever subset carries adversity. Li had no way to check, since adversity was never measured. Conversely, Leypoldt’s resting network, however sharply it separates the cells, has nothing to say about what the system does when a person confronts a human face and a risky offer, and a resting-state interaction located partly in visual cortex is a long way from conformity. Each paper’s blind spot is the other’s method. One has a variable that organizes the brain and no task that reveals function, the other a task that reveals function and no variable that organizes the brain. There is also a population gap that neither paper can close, with a mixed-age German clinical sample on one side and Chinese university students on extreme questionnaire scores on the other, and the Dispatch’s cross-cultural beat has made plain how much a social threat construct can shift across such lines.

A third consideration is the direction of causation, which both papers raise and neither settles. Leypoldt’s authors propose that adversity might leave a stable, variable neural trace on which the disorder acts, or that the disorder has a stable correlate which adversity modulates. Li’s authors raise a parallel question about whether less conformity reflects avoidance of a social interaction or an adaptive response to a hostile one, since the task cannot measure motivation. In both papers the correlational cross-section is asked to carry a developmental argument, and the evidence offered, a sign reversal in one cell and a null brain-behavior correlation in the other, is thinner than the narratives built on it. They are the right hypotheses and the wrong designs to confirm them, which in a young field is an acceptable place to be.

What deserves to be asked next is whether the two findings are the same finding. Suppose the left prefrontal node that Leypoldt finds rewired by adversity in patients is the node that Li finds uncoupled from group information when the group is human and hypercoupled to temporoparietal cortex. Then the picture is a developmental one, in which a history of emotional abuse or neglect, the subscales that drove the parametric results, installs a prefrontal system that treats other people as an object of vigilance rather than as a source of information, and the behavioral signature of social anxiety, a skew toward safety before evidence arrives, is its output. Testing that requires a design neither group built, a task like Li’s run in a sample characterized like Leypoldt’s, with adversity measured by something better than a retrospective checklist. It would show whether anxious social caution is one mechanism or a convergent endpoint of several, and that is the question on which everything a clinician might do with these maps depends. If dorsolateral hypercoupling to the temporoparietal junction appears only in anxious people with a history of emotional maltreatment, then the next trial of a prefrontal-targeted treatment should be stratified by that history, and an unstratified result would be uninterpretable either way.
