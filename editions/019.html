The Social Brain Dispatch

The Average Socially Anxious Brain Does Not Exist

A Tübingen team scanned a hundred and twenty people at rest and found that the connectivity signature of social anxiety reverses its sign depending on whether the patient was emotionally maltreated as a child, which means two decades of conflicting group averages may have been honest measurements of different populations. A Nijmegen team then put eighty-six people in a scanner with a cartoon about a stork and a cloud and found that socially anxious viewers drift apart from one another in the brain’s association cortex, which means the field’s next problem is a heterogeneity that sits inside the signal rather than around it.

Edition No. 19 · Frontier Neuroscience · October 29, 2026

Every reader of this Dispatch who has followed the resting-state literature on social anxiety disorder has met the same embarrassment. One laboratory reports that the dorsolateral prefrontal cortex is hyperconnected in patients, another reports that it is hypoconnected. The anterior cingulate is up in one sample and down in the next. The precuneus, that hub of the default network where self-referential thought is thought to live, is described as both too coupled and too decoupled to its neighbors. Meta-analysts have responded with the usual remedies of larger samples, stricter thresholds, and carefully harmonized preprocessing, and the contradictions have survived all of them. The standard diagnosis is that the studies are underpowered and the analytic pipelines are too diverse. That diagnosis is probably correct as far as it goes, but it has a quiet corollary that deserves to be said aloud. If the contradictions are noise, then enough data should dissolve them, and the field has been waiting for that dissolution for fifteen years. This edition reads two recent papers that offer a different and more unsettling reading, in which the contradictions are signal. They come from opposite ends of the methodological spectrum, one a ten-minute eyes-closed resting scan in Tübingen and the other a six-minute cartoon in Nijmegen, and they arrive at the same structural conclusion from entirely different directions. Social anxiety disorder does not have a single neural signature that the right average will reveal, because the people who share the diagnosis are not neurally interchangeable, and the axes along which they differ can be named.

The stakes are larger than they first appear. A neuroscience of a psychiatric disorder is a promissory note. It promises that a biological marker, once found, will stratify patients, predict who responds to which treatment, and eventually point at a mechanism worth targeting. Every one of those promises depends on the marker being stable across the patients it is meant to describe. A connectivity difference that flips sign in a third of the population does not merely add variance to a group comparison. It guarantees that a classifier trained in one clinic will fail in another whose patients have different childhoods, and it guarantees that a mechanistic story told about the average patient will be wrong about almost every actual patient. The largest multicenter effort this Dispatch has covered, the German anxiety consortium resting-state study from edition five, found modest and diffuse effects, and the natural reading of that result is that the effect is small. The reading these two papers invite is that the effect, in part, is large and heterogeneous and gets cancelled by pooling.

The Tübingen paper comes from a group whose program is easy to describe and harder to execute. Melina Leypoldt is the first author, Benjamin Kreifelts the senior and corresponding author, and the department at the University of Tübingen, the Department of Psychiatry and Psychotherapy under Andreas Fallgatter, supplied the infrastructure and, with Matthias Munk and Vanessa Nieratschker, the grant support from the German Research Foundation. Ariane Wiegand, who is affiliated both with Tübingen and with the Max Planck Fellow Group for Precision Psychiatry in Munich, is the link to the project’s earlier work, which drew on the same Tübingen sample of people with and without social anxiety disorder to ask what early adversity touches. Wiegand and colleagues (2021) reported that genome-wide DNA methylation differs in people with social anxiety disorder, and that early life adversity is entangled with that difference. Nieratschker’s group is an epigenetics group, which explains why a neuroimaging team in a psychiatry department keeps asking about childhood. The program treats early adversity as a biological variable that can be written into the genome’s regulatory layer, so there is every reason to expect it to be written into brain function as well, and every reason to suspect that a social anxiety imaging study that ignores it has quietly omitted its most important covariate. This is the lineage of the diathesis-stress tradition carried into the molecular era, and it descends, intellectually, from the long line of work on how maltreatment reshapes the stress-responsive circuitry of the developing brain. The resting-state paper is therefore a culmination of sorts, the point at which a research program built around a well-characterized cohort turns its attention from methylation to the large-scale functional architecture of the brain.

To understand why the design matters, one needs a clear picture of what the authors did. They scanned 120 German-speaking adults of European descent, aged 19 to 48, at a 3 tesla Siemens Prisma scanner using a multiband sequence with a repetition time of 1.5 seconds, collecting 405 volumes across ten minutes of eyes-closed rest. Diagnosis was established with the structured clinical interview for DSM-IV, which identified 49 participants with social anxiety disorder. Early life adversity was indexed with the Childhood Trauma Questionnaire, and a participant counted as high in adversity if they exceeded the published severity cutoff on at least one of its five subscales, which is 15 or more for emotional neglect, 13 or more for emotional abuse, 10 or more for physical abuse or physical neglect, and 8 or more for sexual abuse. Crossing diagnosis with adversity yielded four cells. There were 49 low-adversity controls, 22 high-adversity controls, 30 low-adversity patients, and 19 high-adversity patients. The cells are unbalanced, and the two high-adversity cells are small by the standards of modern connectivity work, a limitation the authors acknowledge indirectly when they note the risk of false positives in small-scale studies. The clinical profile is what one would hope for. Liebowitz scores averaged about 16 and 23 in the two control cells and about 68 and 75 in the two patient cells, so the patient groups were firmly clinical, and adversity did not significantly shift social anxiety severity, as the diagnosis-by-adversity interaction on the Liebowitz scale was essentially zero (F = 0.002). Childhood Trauma Questionnaire totals averaged roughly 29 and 33 in the low-adversity cells and 47 and 56 in the high-adversity cells, and the patients with high adversity were the most heavily maltreated of all.

The analytic strategy deserves attention because its sequencing is the argument. The team first defined fourteen regions of interest from a literature review of areas where resting-state connectivity had been altered in social anxiety, in early adversity, or in both, including the amygdalae, hippocampi, anterior cingulate, angular and supramarginal gyri, right middle frontal gyrus, medial superior frontal gyrus, left precuneus, putamen, and middle temporal gyrus. They ran a region-to-region analysis on those fourteen regions and found no significant diagnosis-by-adversity interaction. They then ran seed-based correlations from the fourteen regions plus two additional seeds taken from the group’s own earlier task study in a subsample, a left dorsolateral prefrontal cluster and a left medial superior frontal cluster that had shown a diagnosis-by-adversity interaction when participants processed fear-related stimuli. The task-derived dorsolateral prefrontal seed produced ten clusters of interaction, concentrated in visual cortex, pre- and postcentral cortex, and inferior parietal cortex. The amygdala, even with small-volume correction, produced nothing. A lesser paper would have stopped there and reported a null for the field’s favorite structure. This team instead turned to a hypothesis-free approach, connectivity multivariate pattern analysis, which compresses each voxel’s whole-brain connectivity profile into a handful of principal components and asks where those profiles differ by the interaction contrast. Three clusters survived cluster-level false discovery rate correction, a left calcarine fissure and precuneus cluster at Montreal Neurological Institute coordinates (−26, −60, 12) with 70 voxels, a subgenual anterior cingulate cluster at (6, 10, −12) with 65 voxels, and a left middle frontal gyrus cluster at (−34, 8, 36) with 42 voxels. Post-hoc seed-based maps from each of those three clusters, and a conjunction across them, delineated a network of 36 regions in which the effect of diagnosis depended on adversity. Six clusters, in visual cortex, pre- and postcentral cortex, and middle and inferior temporal cortex, interacted with all three hubs.

The result that carries the paper is the shape of the interaction, which is a crossover. Across the network, the authors identified five recurring patterns, and what they share is that the direction of the patient-control difference reverses with adversity. Many of the subgenual cingulate connections showed stronger positive coupling in patients than in controls under high adversity, with little difference under low adversity. The calcarine hub showed the mirror problem in a different register, where in the high-adversity patients the normally negative coupling between visual cortex and the rest of the network was weaker than in high-adversity controls, and the absolute-state analysis described the high-adversity patient as showing hyperconnectivity of the subgenual cingulate and an almost complete absence of resting connectivity in visual cortex. Here is the observation that should make a methodologist sit up. The connectivity profile of high-adversity controls resembled that of low-adversity patients. A healthy person who was maltreated as a child, in this network, looks like a socially anxious person who was not. Only the patient who carries both exposures shows the extreme pattern, which the authors describe as a disconnection of the visual system together with a hyperconnected subgenual cingulate. The result survived controlling for state and trait anxiety and for depressive symptoms, with all 36 interaction effects remaining significant (F(1,116) of at least 14.2, all p below .001), which argues that general negative affect is not what drives it. The dimensional analysis refined the picture. When the authors correlated connectivity strength with Childhood Trauma Questionnaire scores separately in patients and controls, the slopes differed in every one of the 36 connections, typically positive in patients and negative in controls, and the subscale breakdown localized the effect. Emotional abuse and emotional neglect each carried 29 of the 36 connections, physical neglect 21, physical abuse none, and sexual abuse one. In this sample, then, the adversity that rewrites the social anxiety signature is the chronic interpersonal kind, the kind in which the primary experience is being devalued or ignored by the people one depends on.

A critical reader has real work to do here. The interaction cells are small, and a cluster-forming threshold of p below .001 followed by cluster-level correction is standard but not conservative for ten-minute scans. The adversity measure is retrospective self-report, which the authors concede may miss true cases and carries cultural and interpretive baggage, and it says nothing about timing or duration of exposure, the very variables that developmental neuroscience would predict to matter most. The seed selection came partly from an earlier analysis of the same cohort, which invites a circularity worry, although the whole-brain multivariate analysis that produced the three hubs does not depend on it. The sample is young, majority female, and homogeneous in ancestry. Heart rate variability, which is lower in social anxiety and shapes the resting BOLD signal, was not modeled. The authors raise their own hypotheses about mechanism with commendable restraint, and I would add that the cross-sectional design cannot distinguish them. Either the neural correlate of social anxiety is itself conditional on adversity, perhaps through epigenetic regulation, or the correlate of social anxiety is constant and adversity's neural sequelae differ between those who go on to develop the disorder and those who do not. These predict different things in longitudinal data, and the authors say so. What the result does not depend on is which story is true. Either way, a group comparison that ignores adversity is averaging across a crossover.

The Nijmegen paper asks a question that sounds like a different literature entirely. Saskia Koch is the first and corresponding author, working at the Donders Institute for Brain, Cognition and Behaviour at Radboud University, and the senior authors include Peter Hagoort, a leading neurolinguist whose language program anchors the Gravitation consortium that funds the work, and Arjen Stolk, now at Dartmouth, whose work concerns how people establish shared understanding in real time. The project is funded by the Dutch Research Council’s Gravitation program through the Language in Interaction consortium, and Koch holds a European Research Council starting grant titled SocialDynamics. That funding tells you what kind of program this is. It treats social cognition as a process that unfolds between minds in time and tries to measure it with stimuli that resemble life. The immediate lineage is the intersubject correlation tradition that Hasson and colleagues (2004) founded when they showed that different people's visual cortices move in lockstep while they watch the same film, and the clinical extension of that idea by Finn and colleagues (2018), who showed that trait paranoia predicts how far a viewer's brain responses diverge from the group while they follow an ambiguous social narrative. Within the Nijmegen group the direct predecessor is Mangnus and colleagues (2024), who ran this exact film and analysis pipeline in autistic adults and reported preserved spontaneous mentalizing alongside elevated alignment, meaning less variability between viewers than in controls. The present paper is the sibling study, a careful transposition of that design to social anxiety, and the autism comparison it ends with is the reason the paper matters beyond its own findings.

Intersubject correlation is a concept this readership will know, but the interpretation the authors lean on repays a moment's care. When a viewer's regional time series correlates strongly with the average of everyone else, the region is being driven by the stimulus in a way that is shared. When it correlates weakly, the region is being driven by something that differs between viewers, which in a higher-order association area is usually the viewer’s own interpretation, history, and expectation. Variability here is not the same as noise, because noise would reduce alignment in every region, early sensory cortex included. A pattern of preserved or enhanced alignment in sensory cortex alongside reduced alignment in association cortex is the signature of a system that receives the same input as everyone else and does something idiosyncratic with it. The authors paired this with an event-related analysis of mentalizing. The film, the Pixar short Partly Cloudy, runs five minutes and forty-five seconds and tells of a stork and a cloud through cooperation, rejection, injury, and reconciliation, entirely without language. It has been validated as a theory-of-mind localizer by Jacoby and colleagues (2016). Four mental-state scenes totaling 44 seconds were contrasted against seven physical-pain scenes totaling 26 seconds and three socially neutral control scenes totaling 24 seconds.

The sample was 86 adults, recruited through Radboud University’s research database, social media, and campus postings, none on psychotropic medication. Forty-three scored 30 or above on the Liebowitz Social Anxiety Scale, a validated cutoff, and 43 scored below it, matched on sex (27 and 25 women), age (26.3 and 26.1 years), and verbal and nonverbal intelligence. The group means on the Liebowitz scale were 51.4 and 15.2. The authors acclimated participants in a mock scanner to keep state anxiety low, told them simply to watch, and recorded heart rate and pupil diameter throughout, then handed them an unannounced written plot summary after the scan. This design choice is the paper’s conceptual advantage. Most of the mentalizing literature in social anxiety uses tasks that are explicitly evaluative, such as the trust game, the eyes test, and judged performance, and as the authors note, a task that makes a socially anxious person anxious cannot say whether altered mentalizing is a trait of the disorder or a consequence of being evaluated. A cartoon is about as unevaluative as a social stimulus can get.

The mentalizing result was focal. Across all participants the canonical network lit up for mental-state scenes, with the right and left temporoparietal junction at (46, −52, 22) and (−44, −60, 22), the precuneus at (0, −54, 42), and the medial prefrontal cortex at (6, 50, 32), all at a familywise-corrected p below .001. Within that network the high-anxiety group showed reduced activation in the left posterior superior temporal sulcus, in both contrasts, at (−44, −62, 18) for mental greater than control (t = 4.41, corrected p = .003) and at (−50, −64, 14) for mental greater than pain (t = 4.58, corrected p = .009). Bayesian analyses then did something that many imaging papers neglect to do. They estimated evidence for the absence of group differences in the other nodes, and found moderate support for equivalence in the right temporoparietal junction (BF01 = 4.08), weaker support in precuneus (1.22) and medial prefrontal cortex (1.42). That last pair deserves a flag, since Bayes factors of 1.2 and 1.4 are barely informative, so the claim of preserved engagement is firm for the right temporoparietal junction and merely suggestive elsewhere. The behavioral side was stranger. Participants with higher anxiety used fewer negatively valenced emotion words in their plot summaries (proportion 0.65 against 0.85, p = .012), the opposite of the negativity bias the authors had predicted, with no group difference in mental-state vocabulary or overall emotion language. The control physiology came out clean. Mean heart rate was 65.5 and 64.1 beats per minute, pupil dilation tracked event type with pain scenes largest and no group or group-by-event effect (Bayes factors for the absence of group effects above six), and neither the heart-rate nor the pupil time series was more or less synchronized across the anxious viewers.

The intersubject analysis is where the paper earns its place in a frontier edition. Dynamic alignment, computed against a leave-one-out group average in sliding thirty-second windows, revealed three spatiotemporal clusters. Two showed reduced alignment in the high-anxiety group, one across the first two-thirds of the film (cluster statistic 8585, p = .003) spanning superior parietal cortex and bilateral temporal cortex, and one in the final third (4497, p = .02) spanning supplementary motor, medial prefrontal at (−6, 50, 8), and precentral cortex. A third cluster showed increased alignment in the anxious group across much of the film (−8478, p = .002), involving occipital, inferior frontal, and superior temporal cortex. None of the three overlapped the mentalizing network by more than twenty percent, and individual alignment values in the clusters were unrelated to the left posterior superior temporal sulcus effect among anxious participants (all p above .20). So there are two independent findings, a focal reduction in mentalizing-related activation in one region and a diffuse, hierarchy-spanning redistribution of alignment, one with tighter stimulus-locking at the sensory end and looser stimulus-locking at the association end. The authors read it through Mesulam’s hierarchy, with socially anxious viewers receiving the film similarly and integrating it idiosyncratically.

The cross-study comparison is the most provocative paragraph in the paper and also the most exposed. The cluster of reduced alignment in the first two-thirds of the film overlapped by roughly sixty percent, spanning supramarginal, inferior temporal, anterior midcingulate, and precuneus cortex, with a cluster previously reported in the autism sample, yet the sign was reversed, with socially anxious viewers more idiosyncratic and autistic viewers more stereotyped than their respective controls. The two groups were matched on demographics and on social anxiety severity and differed in autism traits. If the reading holds, similar self-reported distress and overlapping anatomy conceal opposite computational styles, a nice demonstration of why symptom-level overlap between conditions has so often misled researchers into proposing common mechanisms. The caveat is that this was an exploratory post hoc comparison across two studies, not a within-sample test, and that the autism sample differed in recruitment and potentially in scanning conditions. The authors present it as a hypothesis, and it should be treated as one. The other limitations are the usual ones for a dimensional design. Groups were defined by a self-report cutoff, not a clinical interview, comorbidity went unassessed, the mental and pain conditions are not fully matched for emotional content, and the plot summary captures retrospective rather than online interpretation.

Set the two papers beside each other and a pattern appears that neither states. Both studies find that the usual group-level question, whether the socially anxious brain differs from the control brain, is underspecified. In Tübingen the underspecification is about a measured moderator, childhood adversity, and the evidence is a sign reversal across a thirty-six-region network. In Nijmegen it is about the viewer, and the evidence is that the anxious group is internally less uniform than controls in association cortex while being more uniform in sensory cortex. The two forms of heterogeneity are not the same thing, and the difference is instructive. The Tübingen heterogeneity is between-subgroup and has a nameable cause. The Nijmegen heterogeneity is within-group and has no named cause at all. It is entirely possible, and the data would be consistent with it, that part of what makes socially anxious viewers drift apart from one another in medial prefrontal and parietal cortex is precisely the kind of history the Tübingen team measured. A viewer who was emotionally neglected as a child and a viewer who was not may bring different priors to the stork and the cloud, and the interpretive divergence the Nijmegen team observed might decompose into subgroups that move in different directions. If so, the reduced alignment in the Dutch sample would be the shadow of the crossover in the German one, cast onto the movie.

The two papers also illuminate each other’s blind spots. The Tübingen design cannot see dynamics. A ten-minute eyes-closed scan with no stimulus gives the brain no occasion to reveal how it interprets anything, so its sign-reversing network remains a description of an idle state, and one is left to infer, speculatively, that the idle state predisposes some particular style of processing. The Nijmegen design cannot see history. It recorded no adversity, no developmental timeline, and no clinical interview, so its idiosyncrasy finding cannot be attributed to anything. The Tübingen network includes the visual cortex, the precuneus, and prefrontal hubs, regions that also appear among the intersubject clusters, and the left middle frontal gyrus appears in the German paper as a link between resting and task-evoked effects. A natural experiment, though not one either team has run, would ask whether the subgenual and calcarine connectivity states measured at rest predict how far a person departs from the group during a movie. There is also a direct tension to name. The Tübingen reading of the high-adversity patient is hyperconnection of a medial affective hub and disconnection of sensory cortex, which sounds like a system sealed off from its input. The Nijmegen reading of the anxious viewer is increased stimulus-locking in sensory and inferior frontal cortex, which sounds like a system faithfully receiving its input. Those are not obviously compatible, and they may describe different subgroups, or different states, or the difference between a brain at rest and a brain under naturalistic drive.

Both teams also converge on a methodological point that deserves to be stated more bluntly than either does. The Tübingen authors argue that adversity should be treated as a required covariate in social anxiety imaging and that region-of-interest masks should be published, since anatomical atlas regions fit poorly to the clusters that actually carry the effects. The Nijmegen result suggests a companion rule, that group means of evoked activation or of connectivity are the wrong summary statistic when the construct of interest is idiosyncratic interpretation, and that a measure of dispersion, such as intersubject correlation, belongs in the primary analysis. A field that learned both lessons would stop asking whether the amygdala is hyper- or hypoactive in the average patient and start asking which patients, with which histories, depart from the group in which directions. That is a harder science, since it needs larger samples with deeper phenotyping and some form of longitudinal anchoring, and the German consortium design is the right vehicle for it if adversity is added to its protocol.

Which leaves the question that sits underneath both papers and that neither can answer. If the neural correlate of social anxiety is conditional on a history of emotional maltreatment, and if socially anxious viewers interpret the same social scene more idiosyncratically than their peers, is the divergence something adversity installs, something that predisposes people to the disorder before adversity acts on them, or the downstream mark of living with the disorder for years? Each answer implies a different clinical future. If adversity installs it, prevention targets the home. If the predisposition comes first, the high-adversity control who looks like a low-adversity patient is a person on a knife edge, and it matters enormously what tips them. If it is a scar of the disorder, treatment ought to be able to erase it. The only study that could decide it follows children with measured adversity and measured interpretive style through adolescence, scanning them with something as unevaluative as a cartoon and as sensitive as a connectivity analysis, and waiting to see who develops the disorder. Nobody has yet run it.
