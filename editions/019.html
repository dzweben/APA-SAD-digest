The Social Brain Dispatch

The Structure Nobody Seeded

In 2023 a consortium that pooled nearly four thousand brains from ten countries found the amygdala largely innocent in social anxiety disorder and pointed instead at the putamen, a structure the circuit literature almost never seeds. In 2024 an Australian team split the amygdala into its four cytoarchitectonic subregions to rescue the circuit story, found no group difference anywhere, and left the field to decide whether it has been refining the map of the wrong territory.

Edition No. 19 · Frontier Neuroscience · October 8, 2026

Every circuit paper this Dispatch has read in its first eighteen editions has begun from the same place. The amygdala is the seed, the prefrontal cortex is the regulator, the face or the self-referential belief is the probe, and the question is whether the coupling between the first two runs too hot or too cold. That consensus has produced a great deal of careful work and almost no agreement about direction, which the field usually blames on small samples, heterogeneous paradigms, and scanner differences. This edition pairs two recent papers that attack the problem of agreement from opposite ends, and the pairing is uncomfortable for the circuit tradition in a specific way. One is the largest structural neuroimaging study of social anxiety disorder ever assembled, nearly four thousand brains from ten countries, and it found the amygdala mostly innocent and pointed at a structure the circuit literature rarely seeds. The other is a deliberately fine-grained resting-state study that split the amygdala into its cytoarchitectonic subregions, hunting for the specificity that coarse seeds were supposed to be hiding, and found that at the level of group diagnosis there was nothing to report. Read together they ask whether the field has been refining its map of the wrong territory.

The stakes are practical, and they are about what a candidate mechanism is allowed to be. If social anxiety disorder is a disorder of an overactive threat detector, the right treatments dampen the detector or strengthen its regulation, and the right developmental question is what makes the detector sensitive early. If instead the replicable anatomical signal sits in the dorsal striatum and pallidum, then the disorder is partly a disorder of how the brain values, learns from, and habitually responds to social outcomes, and a different set of experiments follows. Edition 17 gave a taste of this alternative, in the striatal failure to update on unexpected peer kindness that Jarcho and colleagues (2015) reported. That paper was a functional finding in a small adolescent sample. The question here is whether the structural record, assembled at a scale no single laboratory could manage, says anything comparable.

The first paper belongs to the ENIGMA consortium, and it helps to understand what that institution is before meeting its authors. ENIGMA, the Enhancing Neuro Imaging Genetics through Meta-Analysis network, was founded around 2009 by Paul Thompson, whose laboratory, now at the University of Southern California’s Stevens Neuroimaging and Informatics Institute, had spent years developing computational anatomy and had the realization that imaging genetics would never reach adequate power inside single sites. The consortium’s wager was that harmonized processing pipelines run locally, with only derived volumes shared, could pool hundreds of sites into a single analysis without the political and legal cost of moving raw scans. ENIGMA working groups now exist for schizophrenia, major depression, bipolar disorder, OCD, PTSD, and others, and each has produced the same sobering lesson in different words, that case-control differences in brain structure are real, reproducible, and small. The ENIGMA-Anxiety Working Group extends that model to the anxiety disorders, and the social anxiety paper, published in Molecular Psychiatry in 2023 under first author Nynke Groenewold of the University of Cape Town (Groenewold et al., 2023), is its study of a single diagnosis.

The senior authorship tells you how the project was held together. Dan Stein, of the University of Cape Town, is among the most influential psychiatrists in the anxiety field, a clinician and epidemiologist whose cross-national work on the prevalence of social anxiety disorder (Stein et al., 2017) this Dispatch met in edition eight. Nic van der Wee and Janna Bas-Hoogendam sit at Leiden University Medical Center, where Bas-Hoogendam’s research has centered on the neurobiology of social anxiety across family generations, a design built on the premise that heritable endophenotypes show up in unaffected relatives long before diagnosis. Daniel Pine, of the Emotion and Development Branch at the National Institute of Mental Health, brings the developmental lens, and the paper’s most interesting results turn on development. This is a team whose members have spent their careers on exactly the heterogeneities, age, onset, comorbidity, medication, that the paper sets out to model. They were the right people to ask why earlier small studies disagreed, because the answer they suspected lay in the mix of participants.

The data come from 37 samples across ten countries and five continents, 1,115 patients with social anxiety disorder and 2,775 healthy controls. Diagnoses were made with the interview each sample’s own investigators had used, a heterogeneous set that includes the SCID, MINI, CIDI, DAWBA, and the K-SADS for younger participants. Patients with schizophrenia-spectrum disorders, bipolar disorder, or autism spectrum disorder were excluded, as were controls with major lifetime psychiatric diagnoses or psychotropic use at scan. Every site ran T1-weighted images, acquired at 1.5 or 3 tesla, through FreeSurfer following ENIGMA’s standard protocol, and the seven subcortical regions per hemisphere, the amygdala, hippocampus, caudate, putamen, pallidum, nucleus accumbens, and thalamus, were visually checked at each site. The model was a linear mixed-effects regression with a random intercept for scanner site and fixed effects for sex, age, age squared, their interactions, and total intracranial volume, with family-wise error correction across the fourteen regional tests. Adolescence was defined as ages 8 to 21 and adulthood as 22 to 69, and early onset was defined as before age 13, the sample median.

In the full sample the answer was a modest and surprising one. Patients had smaller putamen volumes bilaterally, with a left effect of d = −0.077 (FWE-corrected p = .037) and a right effect of d = −0.104 (p = .001), and the left putamen showed a diagnosis-by-age interaction (r = −0.034, p = .045). The amygdala, the hippocampus, and the thalamus showed no main effect. Then the stratified analyses did most of the work. In adults, drawn from 30 samples with 668 patients and 1,984 controls, the putamen effect strengthened to d = −0.141 on the left and −0.158 on the right, both below .001 after correction, and a larger pallidum appeared (left d = 0.129, p = .006; right d = 0.099, p = .046). In adolescents, from 29 samples with 437 patients and 788 controls, nothing survived correction. The clinical moderators sharpened the picture further. Patients with a comorbid anxiety disorder had a smaller left amygdala (d = −0.145, p = .017), an effect that failed to survive within either age group separately. Patients with early onset had a smaller right hippocampus (d = −0.194, p = .009), larger in adults (d = −0.326, p < .001), while patients with later onset carried the striatal signature in its strongest form, with bilateral putamen effects around d = −0.31 to −0.34, a smaller left accumbens, and a larger pallidum. Unmedicated patients had a smaller right putamen (d = −0.100, p = .007), and medicated patients showed no corrected differences at all. Severity of social anxiety, trait anxiety, and depression did not associate with any volume once corrected.

The authors are careful about what these numbers are, and the reader should be as well. An effect of d = −0.1 means the distributions of putamen volume in patients and controls overlap almost completely, and the paper itself places these magnitudes alongside the effect sizes ENIGMA has found in OCD, PTSD, and depression and well below those in schizophrenia. The pallidum finding may be partly an artifact of segmentation, since the authors acknowledge it could be an extension of the putamen signal given how the two nuclei abut in an automated parcellation. The sensitivity analyses are reassuring in most respects but not all, since the left putamen effect fell to d = −0.074 and lost significance when the sample was restricted to patients meeting current diagnostic criteria. The design is cross-sectional and partly retrospective, medicated and remitted patients are few, and field strength, sequence, and FreeSurfer version still vary across sites despite the harmonization. What the study achieves is a calibrated map of where a case-control difference can and cannot be found at an N no single site can approach. It shows that the amygdala and hippocampus are not reliably smaller in the pooled disorder, it reveals that the apparent inconsistencies in prior reports are at least partly explained by who was scanned, and it demonstrates that age and onset structure the finding itself and cannot be treated as nuisance covariates.

The second paper comes from a very different scientific culture, a single-site Australian study in which every participant was scanned on the same Siemens Tim Trio and the question was one of resolution. Simone Mizzi, with Mangor Pedersen of Auckland University of Technology, Susan Rossell of Swinburne University of Technology, Peter Rendell, Gill Terrett, Markus Heinrichs of the University of Freiburg, and senior author Izelle Labuschagne of the Healthy Brain and Mind Research Centre at Australian Catholic University, published it in Translational Psychiatry in 2024 (Mizzi et al., 2024). The lineage is worth sketching. Heinrichs is one of the best-known figures in the human oxytocin literature, and the Australian group around Labuschagne has long worked at the junction of social anxiety, amygdala function, and pharmacology. Mizzi’s own earlier contribution, a 2021 systematic review in Molecular Psychiatry of resting-state studies in social anxiety, is the direct ancestor of this paper. That review, which Mizzi et al. (2024) cite as their starting point, found amygdala-frontal connectivity to be the most commonly altered finding, in nine of eighteen studies, with directions that disagreed. A reviewer who has counted that disagreement has an obvious next idea, which is that the amygdala is not one thing and that pooling its nuclei into one seed is what scrambles the sign.

The sample was 138 community volunteers, reduced to 135 after three were excluded for mean framewise displacement above 0.5 millimeters, giving 42 people with social anxiety disorder and 93 controls. Eligible participants were 18 to 55, right-handed, and free of psychotropic medication, substance abuse including smoking, neurological illness, and head injury with significant loss of consciousness. Social anxiety disorder was established with the MINI 6.0.0 and a Liebowitz Social Anxiety Scale score of at least 30, and had to be the primary diagnosis, with five patients carrying secondary diagnoses of generalized anxiety, PTSD, or OCD or ADHD. The groups matched on age (27.6 versus 26.1) and sex, and differed hugely on the Social Interaction Anxiety Scale, 51.6 against 18.3. Each person lay for just over eight and a half minutes at rest with a multiband sequence at TR 1020 ms and 2 millimeter isotropic voxels. The data went through fMRIPrep, eight-parameter nuisance regression, a 0.01 to 0.08 Hz band-pass, and 8 millimeter smoothing, and the seeds were spheres placed in the four cytoarchitectonic amygdala subregions, the amygdalostriatal, basolateral, centromedial, and superficial, together with NeuroSynth-derived seeds in the whole amygdala, precuneus, subgenual cingulate, ventromedial prefrontal cortex, and temporoparietal junction, eighteen seeds in all counting both hemispheres.

The group comparison, run with permutation-based threshold-free cluster enhancement in PALM with age, sex, and motion as covariates, returned nothing. No seed produced a connectivity difference between patients and controls that survived correction, with or without motion in the model. The strongest uncorrected peak, right basolateral amygdala to left cerebellum lobule IV–V, would have been a hard result to build a theory on in any case. The authors then switched to a dimensional analysis across all 135 participants, relating connectivity to Social Interaction Anxiety Scale score with Spearman partial correlations and a permutation procedure that corrects across voxels. Here associations appeared at p < .001, in a handful of places. Left amygdala connectivity with right supramarginal gyrus rose with severity (ρ = 0.39, peak near 44, −44, 36), and the same association appeared for the left superficial subregion alone (ρ = 0.36), with two further left amygdala links to cerebellar targets. The precuneus, seeded in each hemisphere, showed weaker coupling with a perigenual anterior cingulate region at 4, 38, −10 as severity rose (ρ = −0.39 and −0.40). Amygdalostriatal, basolateral, and centromedial seeds produced no symptom associations, and none of the amygdala-prefrontal links that organized the previous decade of circuit research appeared.

The authors interpret the superficial amygdala result as heightened emotional surveillance of self-relevant social information, and the precuneus-cingulate result as weakened regulation of negative self-referential emotion, one that fits the 2014 model of Brühl and colleagues, as Mizzi et al. (2024) characterize it, while the positive amygdala-parietal sign runs against it. They read the absent amygdala-prefrontal effect as a consequence of the absence of threat during rest, and they frame the whole pattern as support for dimensional accounts of the disorder in the Research Domain Criteria tradition. These are reasonable readings and they are readings, and a critical reader owes the study some pressure. The first pressure point is power. With 42 patients and 93 controls, the smallest group difference this design could detect with eighty percent power is roughly half a standard deviation, so the null group result is silent on exactly the d ≈ 0.1 to 0.3 range where the ENIGMA paper found real effects. The second is the relation between the dimensional and categorical analyses. The Social Interaction Anxiety Scale distribution here is almost bimodal, because patients averaged more than thirty points above controls, and a correlation across the pooled sample can therefore be driven largely by the group contrast it was meant to replace, which makes the pairing of a null group test with a positive dimensional test harder to interpret than the authors allow. The third is subregion specificity. Peak coordinates for the superficial and centromedial seeds sit within roughly six millimeters of each other, the sphere radius is 3 millimeters, and the data were smoothed at 8 millimeters, so the claim that only the superficial subregion carries the effect rests on one significant association beside several nonsignificant ones, with no formal test that they differ. The authors flag the smoothing and the small head-motion confound themselves, since controls moved more than patients, and they note the absence of wakefulness monitoring and physiological recording.

Now consider what the two studies reveal about each other. The convergence is that neither finds the amygdala behaving as a monolithic marker. ENIGMA finds its volume unremarkable in the pooled disorder and reduced only in the subset with comorbid anxiety. Mizzi finds its connectivity unremarkable at the group level and informative only dimensionally and only for one nucleus. Both studies leave the strong circuit story, an amygdala that differs in patients, looking underpowered at best and misspecified at worst. There is also a quiet convergence in how both papers behave once participants are partitioned. The large study needs age, onset, comorbidity, and medication to extract its signal, and the small study needs a dimensional model rather than a diagnostic contrast, which are two ways of saying that the diagnostic label is too coarse a unit for neural inference.

The tension lies in what each study could and could not see. The ENIGMA result concerns the putamen and pallidum, and Mizzi’s eighteen seeds contain no striatal seed at all, even though the amygdalostriatal subregion bears the structure’s name. A resting-state connectivity analysis anchored in amygdala, precuneus, cingulate, vmPFC, and temporoparietal junction was never positioned to discover an effect centered in the dorsal striatum, so its null is silent on the circuit the large study implicates. In the other direction, the structural study can say nothing about function. A smaller putamen in adults with later-onset disorder could reflect altered reward learning, a history of avoidance, a consequence of chronic stress, or a marker of vulnerability present before onset, and cross-sectional volume cannot arbitrate. The medication result complicates every reading. The putamen effect was found in unmedicated patients and not in medicated ones, which could mean treatment normalizes the structure, or could simply reflect the small number of medicated patients and the power loss that follows. Mizzi’s design, medication-free by exclusion, is closer to the population in which ENIGMA saw its signal, which is the best argument for a direct test of the striatum in just such a sample.

The developmental dimension raises the stakes, and it is where the pairing teaches the most. ENIGMA found no corrected volumetric difference in adolescents, a group in which the disorder typically begins, and a putamen effect that strengthens with age and is most pronounced in those with later onset. That pattern is awkward for any account in which the neural signature of social anxiety is a fixed early substrate, whether a hyperreactive amygdala or a temperamental inheritance of the kind Kagan and colleagues (1988) described. It fits better a trajectory in which structure and behavior shape each other across years, so that the adult putamen is partly a record of what a socially anxious life has done. The early-onset hippocampal effect points in the same direction through a different door, as a possible fingerprint of longer illness duration. The authors say so directly, and they ask for longitudinal work from adolescence into adulthood. Mizzi’s adult-only, cross-sectional sample cannot speak to any of this, which is to say that the functional literature on resting connectivity, built mostly on adults, has inherited a structural picture it never tested. The adolescent striatal finding of Jarcho and colleagues from edition 17 now looks less like an anomaly and more like a lead that the circuit tradition has been slow to follow.

There is also a methodological lesson about what counts as frontier here. The two papers invert the usual pairing of scale and resolution. The big study gets its power by flattening anatomy to seven volumes per hemisphere, so that a putamen effect of one-tenth of a standard deviation can be certified but cannot be located within the nucleus or tied to any process. The fine study gets its anatomical resolution by dividing the amygdala into four, at the cost of an N where only effects about as large as the diagnosis itself could be detected. The field wants both at once, and the nearest approach so far is the consortium model extended to functional and multivariate data, in which harmonized connectivity or task maps from many sites are pooled the way volumes have been. Edition 12’s multivoxel classification work gestured at what such pooled multivariate maps could one day deliver.

The unresolved question that matters most is whether the putamen and pallidum signature is a cause, a consequence, or a correlate of the way social anxiety unfolds, and whether it relates to the amygdala-prefrontal circuitry at all. Answering it would take something neither paper attempted, a prospective cohort scanned in early adolescence before or near onset, with striatal function and structure measured alongside amygdala and prefrontal connectivity and followed into adulthood with medication and avoidance behavior recorded as they change. If the striatal volume difference appears only after years of illness, the circuit tradition’s amygdala-first story stays largely intact and the striatum is a scar. If it is present at the age of onset and predicts who develops the disorder, the field will have to explain why a decade of fear-circuit imaging ran past the place where its most replicable anatomical signal was sitting.
