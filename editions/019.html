The Social Brain Dispatch

The Map Without an Amygdala

Two papers from the last eighteen months, a seven-study resting-state meta-analysis out of Shandong and a forty-one-student EEG microstate study out of Shenzhen and Chengdu, redraw the socially anxious brain around a quiet left insula, a hushed premotor cortex, and a scalp state that will not let go of the self. Neither contains an amygdala finding, and neither can see one, which makes the new frontier as much a portrait of its instruments as of the disorder.

Edition No. 19 · Frontier Neuroscience · October 22, 2026

Open the figures of almost any social anxiety neuroimaging paper from the first two decades of this field and the amygdala is the first thing the eye finds, a small bright almond in a coronal slice, hot or cold depending on the contrast. The Dispatch has spent eighteen editions inside that picture. Edition one read the Etkin and Wager (2007) synthesis that made amygdala and insula hyperreactivity the field’s default expectation, edition ten read the angry-face studies that established it, and edition eighteen went back to the lesion patient and the rat circuit to ask where the almond’s authority comes from. This edition reads two papers that were published in the last eighteen months and that, between them, contain no amygdala finding at all. One is a meta-analysis of resting-state fMRI that locates the disorder in a left insula, a supplementary motor area, a superior parietal lobule, and a cerebellar lobule. The other is an electroencephalography study that does not locate anything, because it measures when large-scale scalp topographies hold and switch rather than where tissue is active. Frontier work is supposed to tell a field where it is going. These two papers say something more uncomfortable about how much of the new map is being drawn on evidence that cannot yet bear the weight of the drawing.

The stakes are practical. Social anxiety disorder is the one anxiety disorder in which the task-evoked literature has been most reproducible and the translational yield has been least, and the reason is plausibly that task-evoked contrasts (angry face minus neutral face, anticipated speech minus rest) measure a stimulus-bound slice of a condition that is lived continuously. A person with generalized social fear is not scanned at the moment of the podium. They carry the disorder into the corridor, the elevator, and the quiet hours before the meeting. If there is a signature of that standing state, it should be visible without any stimulus at all, in the spontaneous activity of the brain at rest. That is the premise both papers inherit, and it is a serious one, since it is the premise on which every biomarker ambition in clinical neuroimaging depends. It is also the premise that Langhammer and colleagues (2024), read in edition five, tested at scale with a multicenter resting-state connectivity sample and found disappointingly weak diagnosis-specific effects. A reader coming off that edition should treat the two new papers as an argument about whether the small-sample resting-state literature can survive contact with the large-sample one.

The meta-analysis comes first because it is the older idea wearing new software. Its senior and corresponding author is Cong Zhou, and the first author is Sen Li, with the work done at Jining Medical University in Shandong, China, alongside nine co-authors (Li et al., 2026). It is worth being plain about what a reader can and cannot know about the people here. These are not the principal investigators of a famous social anxiety program, and the paper does not tell us where within the discipline Zhou’s own earlier work sits. What the paper does tell us is lineage by citation. It leans on the first generation of resting-state studies of the disorder, two from Qiu and colleagues (2011, 2015) that used the same patient cohort, one from W. Zhang and colleagues (2015), two from M. Yuan (2016, 2018), one from C. Yuan (2018), and one from He (2024), and it reads their findings through the interpretive frame that Clark and McManus (2002) built for self-focused attention. In other words this team is a synthesizer standing at the end of a long chain of small single-site Chinese resting-state studies, and the asset it brings is a statistical method rather than a scanner. That method is seed-based d mapping with permutation of subject images, abbreviated SDM-PSI, an approach developed in the Albajes-Eizagirre and Radua line of work at Barcelona and King’s College London to repair the two chronic weaknesses of coordinate-based meta-analysis, which are that it discards effect magnitude and that it cannot say anything about the reliability of a cluster beyond the fact that studies reported it.

Some of that machinery needs saying aloud, because the interpretation hangs on it. Coordinate-based meta-analysis takes the peak coordinates that primary studies report, plus their statistics, and reconstructs an effect-size map for each study by assuming a spatial kernel around each peak. Older methods, activation likelihood estimation among them, ask whether peaks from independent studies cluster more than chance. SDM instead recreates a signed map of standardized effect, so that increases and decreases within the same study are combined rather than counted as separate vote tallies, and the PSI variant uses permutation of subject images to generate a null that respects the primary studies’ own multiple-comparison structure. The cost is that its output is only as good as the peaks entered. In this case the peaks number 92 across seven datasets, 194 patients with social anxiety disorder and 241 healthy controls, which is a modest mountain of coordinates built on a small hill of people (Li et al., 2026).

The seven datasets are described in a table that tells a more candid story than the abstract. The studies measure local spontaneous activity with one of two metrics, regional homogeneity (ReHo), which indexes local synchrony of the time series among neighboring voxels, or the amplitude of low-frequency fluctuation (ALFF), which indexes the power of the slow signal itself. Mean patient age across the datasets runs from the early twenties to the late twenties, with illness duration, when reported, between 3.8 and 9.1 years. Severity on the Liebowitz Social Anxiety Scale averaged from 53.9 in the Qiu cohort up to 78.9 in the second Yuan study, which means the pooled patients straddle the conventional threshold for generalized subtype. Medication status is mixed. The two Qiu studies and the first Yuan study were drug-naive, W. Zhang included twelve patients on anxiolytics, the second Yuan study included four on antidepressants, C. Yuan included ten patients with comorbid depression, and the He sample, the largest recent contributor with 41 patients against 82 controls, carries a mixed profile that the table itself leaves partly illegible. And the Qiu 2011 and 2015 samples are the same twenty patients analyzed with two different metrics, a fact the authors acknowledge, dealing with it through an exclusion analysis whose results the main text does not report (Li et al., 2026). Effectively the 194 patients contain 20 who are counted twice.

The findings are five clusters. Patients showed higher spontaneous activity than controls in a left superior parietal cluster centered at MNI (−14, −70, 40), spanning 811 voxels with a peak SDM-Z of 2.254 and detected in all seven jackknife iterations, in a right cerebellar cluster in crus I at (18, −80, −24), 778 voxels, Z of 1.723, detected in six of seven, and in a right supramarginal cluster at (64, −38, 36), 219 voxels, Z of 1.884, seven of seven. They showed lower activity in a left insular cluster at (−42, 2, 6), 555 voxels, Z of −1.941, six of seven, and in a right supplementary motor area cluster at (4, −6, 60), 339 voxels, Z of −1.939, seven of seven. Heterogeneity was below 50 percent for every cluster and Egger’s tests showed no funnel asymmetry. A meta-regression found that left insular activity fell with the mean age of the sample, peak at (−36, −10, 6) with an SDM value of −3.497, and that right SMA activity fell with symptom severity measured by the LSAS, peak at (2, −10, 62) with a value of −2.044 (Li et al., 2026).

Read as a pattern, this is an arresting reversal of the task literature. Etkin and Wager (2007) found the insula more active in social anxiety during emotional processing, with the amygdala beside it, and Koban and colleagues (2023), read in edition two, found anterior insula and frontal operculum tracking the biased uptake of negative social feedback. The resting insula here is quieter in patients. The reconciliation is not obvious and the authors do not force one. A reasonable reading is that a region that fails to settle into a strong spontaneous rhythm is a region that has less dynamic range to spend, and that its task hyperactivation is partly an effect of a lowered baseline against which the same stimulus produces a larger relative step. That is a hypothesis about contrast and baseline that the field should have settled long ago and mostly has not, and I would give it better odds than the authors’ own interoception story, which says that diminished insular activity impairs the processing of bodily signals and so increases self-consciousness (Li et al., 2026). Their interpretation of the supplementary motor area is likewise a stretch of the available evidence. They propose that diminished SMA activity reflects disturbed action selection and might contribute to social avoidance, but nothing in a seven-study resting-state synthesis tests an action-selection process. The finding that the SMA effect scales with symptom severity is the most interesting number in the paper, and the one with the weakest explanation.

The weaknesses are on the surface, and a scholar who reads this literature at the methods level will find them quickly. The voxel threshold is p below 0.005 with a ten-voxel extent and a peak Z above 1, a liberal convention that SDM adopted because it correlates with corrected results in simulation but that remains uncorrected in the strict sense. The meta-regression threshold is stated as 0.0005 in the methods and as 0.005 in a table footnote. Table 1 reports neither scanner nor diagnostic instrument. Seven studies is below the number at which random-effects meta-analytic estimates of between-study variance are stable, which makes a pooled I-squared under 50 percent less reassuring than it sounds. The authors call theirs the first systematic quantitative synthesis of resting-state local activity in the disorder, and the careful reader will note that a coordinate-based aggregation of seven datasets, two of them overlapping, is a long way from what an ENIGMA-scale consortium would call a synthesis. And the report is not preregistered. The clusters are hypotheses that earned their jackknife, and the compensation story the authors tell, in which parietal and cerebellar overactivity makes up for insular and prefrontal underactivity, is a narrative with the logical shape of an explanation and no test attached.

The second paper approaches the same standing state from the opposite physical direction. Its team is Huoyin Zhang, Binyu Peng, Yutong Liu, Yu Xi, and Yi Lei, the last of these the corresponding author, with affiliations at the School of Psychology of Shenzhen University and the Institute of Brain and Psychological Science at Sichuan Normal University in Chengdu (Zhang et al., 2025). As with the meta-analysis, the paper gives a reader little biography to work with, no lab name, no earlier program the authors cite as their own, and a search for the group’s other work returns only this article. What it does reveal is the intellectual company it keeps. The introduction positions the study against fMRI findings of altered default mode network connectivity with subcortical, perceptual, and attention networks, against Hao and colleagues’ 2025 report of reduced class D microstate function and reduced transitions into D in generalized anxiety disorder, and against Al Zoubi and colleagues’ (2019) demonstration that transition probabilities among microstates differ in mood and anxiety disorders. It is a microstate paper written by people who want a spatial theory of anxiety delivered in the temporal domain, and the question they ask is a good one. If fMRI sees networks that fluctuate on a scale of seconds, what happens to the scalp field on a scale of tens of milliseconds, and does the pattern of what holds and what hands off to what differ in people who fear evaluation?

A reader fluent in connectivity needs the microstate vocabulary only briefly. Resting EEG, filtered here to 2 to 20 Hz, is reduced to the moments of peak global field power, the instants when the whole-scalp signal is strongest and its topography is most stable. Those topographies are clustered, and the clustering reliably yields a small set of canonical maps, four in the classical solution, labeled A through D, each of which persists for roughly sixty to a hundred milliseconds before the system jumps to another. One then fits the maps back onto the full recording and summarizes each by duration, occurrence per second, and coverage of the total time, and also by the matrix of transition probabilities from each state to every other. The theoretical content comes from mapping the maps to networks. Here is where the field is genuinely divided and where this paper makes a choice it does not defend. The authors, following Tarailis and colleagues (2024), read A as auditory and visual processing and arousal, B as self-related visual processing and autobiographical memory, C as the processing of personally significant information and self-reference, and D as executive function. The influential simultaneous EEG-fMRI work of Britz and colleagues (2010), however, mapped C to a cingulate and anterior insular network, the salience network, and D to the frontoparietal attention network, and a later line of work has argued that C reflects posterior default mode regions. The same topography thus supports an inference about self-reference or an inference about salience detection depending on the paper, and the argument of the study moves with the choice.

The sample is 41 students after four exclusions for head movement or artifacts out of 45 recruited, drawn from a screening of 1,858 questionnaires distributed online of which 1,445 were valid (Zhang et al., 2025). Participants were split at the sample median of 62 on the Liebowitz Social Anxiety Scale, producing a high group of 23 (19 women, mean age 19.87, mean LSAS 82.17) and a low group of 18 (13 women, mean age 19.28, mean LSAS 27.83). All were right-handed with normal or corrected vision and reported no history of mental illness. Nobody in this study was diagnosed with anything. This is an analog design, which means that the extremely high LSAS in the high group, well past the scale’s usual cutoffs for generalized social anxiety disorder, is being read as a stand-in for clinical status in a population in which self-report was the only instrument. EEG was recorded for five minutes with eyes closed on a 64-channel system at 1000 Hz, with ICA cleaning and an average reference, and the clustering used a fixed four-class solution in Cartool with a 30 ms smoothing window. The fixed solution matters, and the authors concede as much, because the paper also describes its approach as data-driven, which a preset class count is not.

The results are large. The four maps explained 67.74 percent of global variance in the high group and 58.22 percent in the low group, t(39) = 4.623 with a Cohen’s d of 1.402. Microstate C lasted 83.64 ms in the high group against 62.23 ms in the low group (d = 1.721), occurred 5.67 times per second against 3.44 (d = 1.913), and covered 29.10 percent of the recording against 13.20 percent (d = 1.983). Microstate D showed the opposite pattern, with a duration of 81.88 ms against 109.67 ms (d = −1.087) and coverage of 28.31 percent against 43.40 percent (d = −1.028), though its rate of occurrence did not differ. After Bonferroni correction across twelve transition types, transitions from A to C, C to A, C to B, and B to C were more probable in the high group and transitions A to D and D to A more probable in the low group. In the low group only, B-to-C transitions correlated negatively with total LSAS (r = −0.572) and with avoidance (r = −0.495), correlations that used an uncorrected threshold in an n of 18 (Zhang et al., 2025).

The authors read this as an antagonism between a self-referential state, C, that is overrepresented in high social anxiety and an executive state, D, that is underrepresented, and they say this is the first demonstration of such a pattern, which is a claim that outruns the data. A single resting recording in eyes-closed students shows a trade in time share between two canonical maps. It does not show that the underlying networks antagonize each other, because with a fixed four-state solution every state’s coverage is constrained to sum with the others to one, so more time in C must be less time in some other state by construction. The pattern also contains a quieter signal that the authors leave unremarked. Higher global explained variance in the high group means four fixed templates fit those brains better, which could indicate more stereotyped, less diverse spontaneous dynamics or could reflect nothing more than a signal-to-noise difference, and the study cannot say which. There is also an internal inconsistency in the table of transition probabilities, where the B-to-C row lists the low group at 0.087 and the high group at 0.049 while the t statistic of 4.774 and the text say the high group is higher. The interpretation of low-group B-to-C flexibility as protective depends on that direction.

Put the two papers side by side and the first thing to notice is that they were built to answer the same question with instruments that cannot disagree with each other, because they cannot be compared. The meta-analysis resolves space at the scale of centimeters, averages over minutes, and reports a difference between diagnosed adults and controls drawn from clinical series. The EEG study resolves time at the scale of tens of milliseconds, cannot localize better than a sheet of scalp, and compares university students split on a self-report scale. They converge at one remove. Both characterize the disorder as a property of a standing state rather than a response to a face. Both find that the standing state is disorganized in the direction of heightened self-directed processing, the parietal increases in one and the C dominance in the other, with a depressed element that the authors attribute to attention or control, the insula and SMA in one and microstate D in the other. And both reach for Clark and Wells’ cognitive account, and the Rapee and Heimberg model read in edition fifteen, as the explanation that makes the pattern make sense, an act of interpretation that precedes the evidence rather than follows from it.

Where they are in tension is more instructive. If the Britz mapping of C is right, microstate C is a salience network state, which is anchored on the anterior insula, and the high group in the EEG study spends more than twice as much of its resting time in the very state whose hemodynamic correlate is quieter in the meta-analysis. If the alternative mapping is right, and C reflects posterior default mode activity, the EEG finding fits the parietal increases but now says nothing about the insula at all. The two papers therefore constitute an unusually clean test of a problem the microstate field has been avoiding, which is that its central interpretive step, from topography to network, is contested, and every paper that ignores the contest inherits an interpretation by default. The meta-analysis, for its part, has a blind spot that the EEG study illuminates by contrast. Its measures, ReHo and ALFF, average over a ten-minute run and cannot distinguish a region that is persistently somewhat low from a region that alternates between long high states and long low ones. A microstate sequence is exactly the sort of evidence that could tell these apart. A lowered mean insular amplitude and a different dwell structure of a network state can be the same observation described at two time scales or two different observations that happen to share a sign, and the field currently has no analysis that adjudicates between the possibilities. And both papers share a blind spot that neither can see from inside, which is that they have almost no overlap in the subject variable that matters most, namely clinical status established by structured interview, medication state, and comorbid anxiety and depression, all of which the EEG study did not control and most of which the meta-analysis imported from heterogeneous primary studies.

What the pair finally demonstrates is the state of the frontier. The amygdala has been left off the map without being refuted, because neither a resting local-activity metric nor a scalp field can see it well, and the new picture of the disorder as a distributed rearrangement of parietal, insular, premotor, and cerebellar function is partly a picture of what the new instruments happen to be sensitive to. A frontier that is defined by the reach of its tools needs the large-sample, harmonized, preregistered work to tell it which of its coordinates are real. Which leaves a question that matters for everyone who builds a career on resting-state markers of social fear. If the right SMA really does track symptom severity, and the left insula really does lose amplitude with age, are those effects properties of the disorder, of the medications and comorbidities that accumulate alongside it, or of the particular scanner rooms and recruitment pipelines in which seven small studies happened to find them, and what would a design look like that could tell the three apart?
