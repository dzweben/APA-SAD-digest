The Social Brain Dispatch

A Threshold With No Fixed Address

Jerome Kagan spent a decade building the case for a discrete, threshold-governed temperament behind childhood shyness. Thirty-two years and a hundred and twenty-six thousand genomes later, the heritability he predicted turns out to be real, larger even than he guessed, and to belong to nothing as specific as social anxiety at all.

Edition No. 6 · Temperament, Genetics, Heritability, and Behavioral Inhibition · July 16, 2026

Behavioral inhibition is the single most reliable prospective sign anyone has found that a two-year-old will grow into an anxious adult, and it is also, on the numbers alone, a spectacularly weak one. Pooled across seven studies and roughly nine hundred children, being classified as behaviorally inhibited in early life raises the odds of a later diagnosis of social anxiety disorder by something like sevenfold, a signal strong enough that Clauss and Blackford, writing the definitive meta-analysis of the construct in 2012, called it one of the largest known risk factors for the disorder anywhere in the literature. And yet, in that same pooled data, only a little over four in ten inhibited children ever become anxious at all. Whatever behavioral inhibition is measuring, it is neither destiny nor noise, it sits in the uncomfortable middle where a trait can be real, biologically anchored, and still leave the majority of the people who carry it alone. That middle ground is where the stakes of this edition live. If inhibition names a discrete temperamental type with a specific, tractable biological substrate, something a clinician could in principle intervene on directly, then the minority who go on to develop the disorder are the ones in whom a known biological switch got tripped, and the search for what trips it is a search worth funding at the level of a targeted circuit. If instead inhibition is a surface behavioral readout of something far more diffuse, a general and heavily shared liability toward negative emotionality that gets sculpted into a specific clinical presentation by parenting, culture, and chance, then the whole framing of a biological basis of shyness is asking the wrong kind of question, and the real work lies downstream, in the moderators that decide which children the liability finds. This edition goes further back than any other so far, to the paper that gave behavioral inhibition its name and its founding biological claim, and further forward than any other, into a genomic dataset built at a scale its original author could not have imagined, to ask whether the trait he spent a decade characterizing turns out to have anything as specific as an address in the genome.

Jerome Kagan was, by the time Science published Biological Bases of Childhood Shyness in 1988, one of the most consequential developmental psychologists of the postwar era, a Harvard professor since the mid-1960s who had spent two decades arguing, against a prevailing tide of strict behaviorist environmentalism, that children arrive already carrying constitutional dispositions a purely learning-based psychology could not explain. His conviction traces to a specific field experience rather than a laboratory one, fieldwork in San Marcos, Guatemala, in 1971 and 1972, where he observed profound and durable differences among infants raised in conditions of extreme early isolation, differences he came to believe reflected biological variation the environment had not put there. That conviction hardened across Birth to Maturity, his 1962 longitudinal collaboration with Howard Moss on continuity from childhood into adulthood, and The Nature of the Child in 1984, his book-length case for biologically prepared temperamental dispositions, both of which established him as the discipline’s leading voice for taking constitution seriously without lapsing into the crude hereditarianism the field had spent a generation trying to live down. The construct he built with J. Steven Reznick, by then at Yale after training at the University of Colorado Boulder and running Harvard’s own Infant Study before he left, and Nancy Snidman, the Harvard research associate who ran the physiological side of the program for the better part of two decades, did not descend from nowhere. It is a direct, more rigorously instrumented heir to Alexander Thomas and Stella Chess’s New York Longitudinal Study, the parent-interview-based temperament taxonomy that had identified an approach-withdrawal dimension and a slow-to-warm-up cluster back in the 1950s. Kagan’s innovation was methodological as much as theoretical, refusing to rest a temperament construct on parental report alone and instead building standardized laboratory protocols, an unfamiliar examiner, unfamiliar toys, a talking robot, brief maternal separation, paired from the outset with peripheral physiological recording, so that inhibition would be a category anchored in an inferred nervous-system substrate rather than a purely descriptive label. The 1988 Science paper was not an opening move. It arrived as the compressed, high-visibility announcement of a research program Kagan, Reznick, and Snidman, working with Cynthia Garcia-Coll, had already been running for six years, through Garcia-Coll, Kagan, and Reznick’s 1984 paper introducing behavioral inhibition in Child Development, a companion 1984 paper on inhibition to the unfamiliar, and above all the far longer 1987 Child Development report, The Physiology and Psychology of Behavioral Inhibition in Children, which used the identical two cohorts and carries most of the granular numbers the Science paper compresses into six columns. Read this way, the 1988 paper is less a discovery than a verdict, the moment Kagan judged the case for a biologically grounded temperamental category strong enough to bring before the broadest possible scientific audience, and it became, with more than twelve hundred citations since, the founding document of behavioral inhibition as a named construct in developmental psychopathology.

The study’s design is a study in the discipline of extreme-group selection. From a screened pool of more than four hundred Caucasian, middle- and working-class children, Kagan’s team retained only the sixty at each of two ages, twenty-one months in one cohort and thirty-one months in the other, who showed consistent restraint or consistent spontaneity across a whole battery of unfamiliar incentives, discarding everyone in the ambiguous middle rather than trying to model a continuous population. That choice buys statistical power and interpretive clarity at the cost of external validity, and Kagan defended it on both empirical and philosophical grounds, arguing in the paper itself, with an explicit nod to Bohr’s complementarity principle, that some phenomena are more profitably described in discrete categorical terms than continuous ones, and that discrete subgrouping by physiological pattern produced cleaner five-and-a-half-year outcomes than continuous regression models did. The behavioral index itself broadened with age, from latency to approach an unfamiliar adult and proximity-seeking to the mother at twenty-one months, to behavior with an unfamiliar peer at four and five and a half years, to independent, blind classroom observation by age five, a design decision that let Kagan report a genuinely external validation, inhibited twenty-one-month-olds were rated as socially isolated by teachers who had never seen the original lab classification, at a correlation of .34. The behavioral construct itself showed real but partial continuity, a correlation of .52 between the twenty-one-month classification and the aggregate index at five and a half years, .67 between the four-year and five-and-a-half-year waves, and, tellingly, an asymmetry Kagan flagged rather than buried, roughly forty percent of originally inhibited children had become measurably less inhibited by five and a half, against fewer than ten percent of uninhibited children drifting the other way, a pattern he attributed to the active work American parents do coaxing timid children out of visible shyness. Consistency, in other words, was the exception this paper was explaining, not the baseline it assumed.

The paper’s real ambition, though, was never behavioral stability on its own, it was to root that stability in a nervous-system substrate, and the physiological battery Kagan, Reznick, and Snidman assembled remains, more than three decades on, an unusually comprehensive attempt to do exactly that. Inhibited children ran higher and less variable heart rates at every wave, a correlation with the behavioral index that strengthened from around .44 in the earliest assessments to .58 for heart rate and .64 for heart-rate variability between four and five and a half years, alongside a spectral shift toward sympathetic dominance under cognitive challenge. They carried larger pupils across a battery of memory and inference tasks, two out of three inhibited children sustaining that dilation across the whole series against about a third of uninhibited children. Under conditions designed to induce speech under stress, they showed measurably less variability in vocal pitch period, a proxy for laryngeal muscle tension, correlating at −.43 with the original classification. Urinary norepinephrine, essentially uninformative at twenty-one months, became a significant correlate by age four and again at five and a half, the biological signature sharpening as the behavioral profile itself consolidated. And cortisol, sampled both at home across three mornings and before and after a ninety-minute laboratory session at five and a half years, proved the single most powerful individual discriminator in the whole battery, correctly classifying seventy-eight percent of the original cohort by laboratory value alone. Kagan folded all of this into an explicit circuit-level theory, that inhibited and uninhibited children differ in the tonic threshold of excitability in limbic structures centered on the amygdala and hypothalamus, a lower threshold cascading through three distinct stress circuits, the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal axis driving cortisol, the reticular activating system acting on laryngeal muscle via the nucleus ambiguus, and the sympathetic nervous system driving heart rate, pupil, and norepinephrine, all three under direct hypothalamic command. He reached outside human developmental psychology entirely to support it, to Robert Adamec and Cynthia Stark-Adamec’s work contrasting defensive and nondefensive domestic cats, where defensive animals showed stronger amygdala-to-ventromedial-hypothalamus evoked responses, and to the locus coeruleus literature on central norepinephrine as the physiological seed of a temperamentally low threshold for treating the ordinarily neutral as threatening. It is a genuinely elegant theory, and it is worth being honest about where the paper’s own data already complicate it. The eight physiological measures Kagan reported turned out, in his own numbers, to be a loose bundle rather than one coherent signal, the median pairwise correlation among them, apart from the mechanically linked heart rate and heart-rate variability pair, sat around .10, and only about a third of inhibited children showed elevated activity across all three of the stress circuits Kagan named. About half of the inhibited children in the sample, in fact, had unremarkable cortisol. The composite physiological index correlated a genuinely strong .70 with the original behavioral classification, but that composite was doing real interpretive work smoothing over a looseness in the underlying biology that the single-threshold language of the paper’s title tends to obscure. Even at the level of eight hand-selected physiological variables in a laboratory Kagan controlled completely, the biological basis of shyness was already, on inspection, a loose bundle of partially independent systems rather than one dial with one setting.

Thalia Eley did not set out to test Kagan’s threshold theory, and that distance is part of what makes her 2020 paper, coauthored with Kirstin Purves as first author and Gerome Breen as senior statistical architect, such a useful counterweight rather than a direct rebuttal. Eley trained at Cambridge and then at University College London’s Institute of Child Health, where her doctoral work on the twin-based aetiology of childhood anxiety and depression placed her from the start inside the behavioral-genetics tradition rather than the laboratory-observational one Kagan represented, a tradition she has spent her career at King’s College London’s Social, Genetic and Developmental Psychiatry Centre, alongside long-time collaborator Robert Plomin, building into one of the field’s largest live research infrastructures. Her signature project, Genesis 12-19, a twin study of adolescent emotional development, gave her the empirical grounding for a career-long argument that genetic risk for anxiety and depression is better understood not as a fixed liability but as differential sensitivity to environment, a framework she and her collaborators have named therapygenetics, the study of genetic moderators of who actually responds to psychological treatment. That argument, and the infrastructure needed to test it at scale, is what led her and Breen, an Irish-trained psychiatric geneticist whose own path ran through industrial biochemistry and doctoral work on schizophrenia genetics in Aberdeen before he became Academic Director of King’s Genomics, to co-found the GLAD Study, an online-recruited, genotyped, recontactable cohort of tens of thousands of people with lifetime depression or anxiety built specifically to make studies like this one possible. Purves, an early-career genetic epidemiologist inside Eley and Breen’s group, is the paper’s first author and operational lead, and the 2020 study sits at a genuine pivot point in this arc, the moment Eley’s twin-study-honed intuitions about shared genetic liability across anxiety, depression, and neuroticism finally get tested against molecular, individual-level genetic data at a scale twin designs alone could never reach.

The methodological leap this paper represents over anything Kagan could have run is worth teaching explicitly, because it is not simply more data, it is a different kind of evidence about heritability altogether. Twin studies, the tool Eley’s own early career depended on, estimate heritability by comparing trait similarity in monozygotic versus dizygotic twins and can produce large, robust heritability estimates without ever identifying a single gene, a strategy that has long put shyness and behavioral inhibition among the most heritable personality dimensions psychology measures. Genome-wide association studies do something structurally different, genotyping hundreds of thousands of common single-nucleotide variants across tens of thousands of individuals and testing each one, one at a time, for association with a phenotype, then aggregating that genome-wide signal two further ways, through linkage disequilibrium score regression, which estimates what fraction of trait variance is explained collectively by all measured common variants, the SNP-based heritability, and through polygenic scoring, which asks how much of that aggregate signal can actually predict an individual’s status out of sample. The gap between these two numbers, aggregate SNP-heritability against individual predictive power, is where the missing-heritability problem lives, and it is exactly the gap this paper puts a number on for anxiety at real scale for the first time. Purves, Coleman, Meier, and colleagues built their primary sample from a hundred twenty six thousand four hundred forty three European-ancestry UK Biobank participants aged forty six to eighty who completed an online mental health questionnaire, defining a primary Lifetime Anxiety Disorder phenotype, twenty five thousand four hundred fifty three cases against fifty eight thousand one hundred thirteen controls, from self-reported professional diagnosis of one of five core disorders, generalized anxiety disorder, social phobia, panic disorder, agoraphobia, and specific phobia, or from meeting DSM-IV criteria on a structured short-form interview, alongside a secondary Current Anxiety Symptoms phenotype defined by a score of ten or above on the seven-item GAD scale against the same control set. They then meta-analyzed the UK Biobank result against two independent cohorts, the ANGST consortium and Denmark’s iPSYCH register, the latter using clinician rather than self-report diagnosis, a useful check on whether the UK Biobank signal was an artifact of self-report, bringing the combined meta-analytic sample to a hundred fourteen thousand nineteen people.

The headline number is a genuine surprise relative to everything that came before it. SNP-based heritability on the liability scale came in at twenty six percent for Lifetime Anxiety Disorder and thirty one percent for Current Anxiety Symptoms, roughly double the seven to fourteen percent estimates the handful of earlier, far smaller anxiety genome-wide studies had produced, a jump the authors attribute mostly to the raw statistical power UK Biobank’s scale finally supplied rather than to anything different about the phenotype itself. That heritability, though, refused to concentrate. Only five loci reached genome-wide significance in the primary analysis, an intergenic signal on chromosome 9p23 already implicated in neuroticism and depression, a variant near NTRK2 on 9q21.33, the gene encoding the TrkB receptor through which brain-derived neurotrophic factor acts on synaptic plasticity, a signal in TMEM106B on 7q21.1 that gene-based analysis ranked as the single strongest association in the whole dataset, an intergenic locus on 5q15, and one near MYH15 on 3q11.2, each individually modest, p-values clustered around 10−8 to 10−12, nothing close to the large single-gene effects a threshold theory built around one circuit would predict. Genetic correlation, computed through linkage disequilibrium score regression against other genome-wide datasets, told a still more dissolving story. Lifetime Anxiety Disorder correlated with Current Anxiety Symptoms in the very same sample at .93, essentially the same underlying signal measured twice, but it also correlated at .74 with independent case-control status in the ANGST cohort, at .73 with neuroticism measured in a wholly separate slice of UK Biobank, and, most consequentially for anyone trying to defend a disorder-specific genetic architecture, at .78 with major depressive disorder in the Psychiatric Genomics Consortium’s data, alongside significant positive correlations with schizophrenia, bipolar disorder, insomnia, and attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder. And when the authors tried to predict individual case status out of sample using a polygenic score built from their own genome-wide signal, cross-validated across ten splits of the data, it explained about half of one percent of the variance in who actually had a diagnosis, a figure the authors present matter-of-factly as the expected consequence of high polygenicity but one that lands, against a twenty six percent aggregate heritability estimate, as a fairly stark statement about how far molecular psychiatry still is from anything resembling individual prediction. One limitation deserves to be named plainly rather than glossed over. Social phobia is one of five diagnoses folded into the composite Lifetime Anxiety Disorder phenotype, and the paper never once tests it as its own outcome, meaning this is evidence about the shared genetic architecture of anxiety disorders as a class, strong and real evidence, and not evidence, in either direction, about anything specific to social anxiety disorder’s own genetic makeup.

Set beside each other, Kagan and Purves agree on more than their methods would suggest. Both find heritability that is large and real, Kagan’s twin-adjacent behavioral tradition having long put shyness among personality’s most heritable dimensions, Purves and colleagues now putting a molecular floor under that claim at twenty six to thirty one percent of variance carried by common genetic variants alone. Where they diverge is not on whether biology matters, it is on how concentrated that biology turns out to be, and each paper’s blind spot is legible only in light of the other’s data. Kagan’s theory wanted, and needed, a small number of interpretable, circuit-level mechanisms, a limbic threshold expressed through three named stress circuits, because a theory that could point to the amygdala and the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal axis was a theory that gave clinicians and neuroscientists something to intervene on. But his own physiological battery had already shown the cracks in that unity, eight measures correlating with each other at a median of roughly .10, only a third of inhibited children lighting up across all three named circuits at once, half showing entirely unremarkable cortisol. Purves and colleagues’ genomic result is that same looseness playing out at a different level of biological organization and a very different scale, five weak loci instead of eight loose physiological measures, a heritability estimate that refuses to sit inside any interpretable handful of genes, spread instead across what the authors themselves describe as many hundreds or thousands of variants of individually negligible effect. The molecular genetics did not discover polygenicity so much as it gave Kagan’s own already-loose physiological bundle its natural explanation, a temperament built not on one circuit with one threshold but on a genuinely diffuse liability distribution that different children’s physiology samples from in partially independent ways. But Purves and colleagues’ paper has its own blind spot, and it is the mirror image of Kagan’s overreach, a genetic architecture so thoroughly shared across anxiety, depression, neuroticism, even schizophrenia and insomnia, correlating at .93 with its own secondary phenotype and .78 with major depression, that the paper cannot, even in principle as designed, locate anything specific to social anxiety at all. It absorbs social phobia into a five-disorder composite for statistical power and never looks back. That is precisely the kind of behaviorally specific, developmentally anchored, prospectively measured signal Kagan’s entire research program was built to detect and that a questionnaire-based, cross-sectional, midlife UK Biobank sample structurally cannot supply. The reconciling move, tellingly, sits inside Eley’s own broader body of work rather than in this paper itself. Her therapygenetics framework and her twin studies of gene-by-parenting interaction argue that genetic liability for anxiety functions less like a fixed amount of risk and more like differential sensitivity, a plasticity factor that makes some children’s outcomes more contingent on environment, parenting, and intervention than others’. That framework converges, from a completely different direction, on exactly what Nathan Fox’s detection and dual control model concluded after three decades of following Kagan’s own inhibited children into adulthood, that the minority of behaviorally inhibited children who develop social anxiety disorder are distinguished not by a purer dose of some single biological threshold but by the type of cognitive control, parenting environment, and attentional bias layered on top of an underlying, diffusely distributed sensitivity. Kagan spent a career looking for the trait’s address. Eley’s data suggest the trait was never going to have one, because what gets inherited is not a location, it is a probability distribution over how reactive a nervous system will be, and what happens next depends on everything that meets it.

What nobody has actually built, thirty two years after the first paper and six after the second, is the study that could adjudicate between these two blind spots directly rather than leaving them to triangulate across entirely different samples, methods, and eras. That would mean taking Kagan’s own laboratory protocol, the unfamiliar examiner, the unfamiliar peer, the physiological battery of heart rate, cortisol, and pupil dilation, and running it in a cohort large enough and genotyped deeply enough to ask whether behaviorally classified inhibition indexes a partially separable slice of the polygenic liability Purves and colleagues mapped, a cluster of variants doing something specifically temperamental rather than generally internalizing, or whether it is simply the earliest behavioral readout available of the same diffuse, undifferentiated liability that eventually gets diagnosed as generalized anxiety in one child, depression in another, and social phobia in a third, depending on which regulatory pathway and which parenting environment happens to meet it. Until someone runs a Kagan-style behavioral and physiological protocol inside a UK Biobank-scale genotyped sample, behavioral inhibition will remain exactly what this edition has shown it to be from both directions at once, a real, heritable, and clinically consequential temperament with no fixed genetic address, and whether that address simply has not been found yet or does not exist at all is the single most consequential thing the field’s two most careful measurements of the same phenomenon have so far failed to agree on.
