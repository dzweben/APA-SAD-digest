The Social Brain Dispatch

The Genome That Cannot Find a Single Shy Child

A genome-wide study of eighty three thousand adults puts a hard number on how heritable anxiety disorders are, twenty six percent of the liability to develop one, and when that number is turned into an actual predictive score it explains one half of one percent of who does. Thirty two years earlier, a Harvard psychologist armed with nothing more sophisticated than a stopwatch and a cortisol assay showed that watching a two year old approach or avoid a stranger for twenty minutes predicts, years later, more than a quarter of the variance in whether that same child is still avoiding strangers. The genome has still not caught up to the playroom.

Edition No. 6 · Temperament, Genetics, Heritability, and Behavioral Inhibition · July 20, 2026

Every claim this publication has made so far about a biological basis for social anxiety has come from a scanner, a circuit, a signal localized to a region or a connection between two of them. This edition asks a more foundational question, one that sits underneath all of that imaging work and that the field answers in two entirely incompatible currencies. What does it actually mean to say a psychological trait is biological, and specifically, what kind of evidence licenses that claim. One answer, the oldest and in some ways still the most persuasive, comes from watching a child's body respond to a stranger in a room and inferring, from heart rate and cortisol and the width of a pupil, that some children are born with a lower threshold for alarm. The other answer, the one that now dominates psychiatric etiology, comes from genotyping tens of thousands of people, computing how much of the variance in who develops a disorder tracks with which common genetic variants they carry, and reporting a heritability estimate accurate to two decimal places. Both answers claim the same territory, a biological basis for anxious temperament, and until this edition nobody reading this Dispatch has been asked to hold them side by side. Jerome Kagan, working with Steven Reznick and Nancy Snidman out of a Harvard playroom in the nineteen eighties, built the first kind of evidence into behavioral inhibition, arguably the single most influential temperament construct in the history of developmental psychopathology. Kirstin Purves, Thalia Eley, and twenty four coauthors at King's College London and their international collaborators built the second kind of evidence into the largest genome-wide association study of anxiety disorders yet published. Reading them together is less a matter of confirming one with the other than of discovering how little either one, on its own terms, actually explains, and how the specific way each study fails illuminates exactly what the other one cannot see.

Kagan arrived at the question of biological temperament by way of a long argument with the psychology he was trained into. He earned his doctorate at Yale under Frank Beach, spent early years at Ohio State and the Fels Research Institute immersed in a discipline still dominated by the conviction, shared uneasily by behaviorism and psychoanalysis alike, that the differences among children were made almost entirely by experience. His own early fieldwork, a set of studies of infant cognitive development among Guatemalan Mayan children living in relative isolation, pushed him toward the opposite conclusion, that some developmental capacities emerge on a maturational timetable robust to wildly different rearing environments, and that conviction hardened over three decades at Harvard into the defining argument of his career, that biology deserved a seat at the table psychology had reserved almost entirely for learning. The behavioral inhibition program, run for two decades with Reznick and Snidman along with a rotating cast of doctoral students including Cynthia Garcia Coll, was where that argument found its most durable evidence. It was not Kagan's first temperament claim and it would not be his last, he continued elaborating the construct into his eighties, but the 1988 paper in Science functions as the movement's public announcement, the compact, headline version of a research program whose full texture the team had laid out in more exhaustive form the year before in Child Development, a companion paper reporting the same longitudinal cohorts in granular physiological detail that the Science format could only gesture toward. Reading the two together, which is the only honest way to read either, gives a clear picture of what the team actually did and how much weight their conclusions can bear.

The design was an extreme groups strategy, deliberately built to maximize the chance of finding real physiological correlates by minimizing behavioral ambiguity at the outset. Kagan's team screened more than four hundred Caucasian toddlers from middle and working class Boston families, by telephone and direct observation, to find sixty who were consistently and extremely inhibited, quiet, avoidant, clinging to a parent when confronted with an unfamiliar room, an unfamiliar adult, unfamiliar toys, a talking robot, and sixty who were consistently and extremely uninhibited, approaching all of it without hesitation, with equal numbers of boys and girls in each group. One cohort was selected at twenty one months, a second at thirty one months, and both were reassessed at roughly four years and again at five and a half, with the classification broadening over time from behavior toward a single unfamiliar examiner to behavior with an unfamiliar peer in a laboratory playroom, with classmates in an actual kindergarten, and during a ninety minute testing battery, where the single most discriminating measure at the oldest age turned out to be something as simple as how long a child waited before making an unprompted comment to the examiner and how many such comments followed. Not one inhibited child, against fourteen uninhibited children, spoke up within three minutes and made forty or more spontaneous remarks. The behavioral classification held up remarkably well across that stretch, correlating .52 between the twenty one month index and the aggregate index at five and a half years, and by the time the Science paper appeared the team was reporting continuity out to age seven, the age at which, per its own abstract, a majority of the originally restrained children were quiet and socially avoidant with unfamiliar people while a majority of the originally spontaneous children remained talkative and interactive.

What earned the paper its title, and its enduring citation count, was not the behavioral continuity alone but the physiology layered on top of it. At every assessment the inhibited children showed higher and more stable heart rates than the uninhibited children, a pattern that itself predicted later inhibition, a high and stable heart rate at twenty one months correlated .44 with inhibited behavior at five and a half years, well beyond what the behavioral classification alone predicted. Spectral analysis of the heart rate signal showed inhibited children shifting toward greater sympathetic dominance across a period of cognitive stress. Pupils dilated further and stayed dilated longer during cognitive testing. Vocal cord tension, measured through the perturbation of pitch periods during single word utterances under mild stress, ran lower and more stable in inhibited children, a physiological signature borrowed from Philip Lieberman's work on laryngeal muscle tone and pressed here into unusually creative service. Salivary cortisol, gathered both at home in the morning and across a laboratory session, was reliably higher in inhibited children, correlating .45 with the original twenty one month classification, and cortisol alone, using only the laboratory sample, correctly sorted seventy eight percent of the cohort into the behavioral group they had already been assigned to by hand. Urinary norepinephrine metabolites, assayed by mass fragmentography, correlated more modestly, around .3, with concurrent behavior at four and five and a half years. None of these physiological signs correlated strongly with each other, the median pairwise correlation among eight physiological variables sat around .10, which the team read not as a weakness but as evidence that arousal in these children was not a single generalized state but a set of loosely coupled circuits, each capable of leaking evidence of an underlying disposition without any one of them being a reliable marker on its own. An aggregate index built by averaging all eight variables correlated .70 with the original twenty one month behavioral classification and remained significant, .58, against behavior four years later. A small subgroup, thirteen of the sixty original inhibited children who combined extreme behavioral inhibition with a high and stable heart rate across the first two assessments, stood out further still, staring longer at unfamiliar peers, avoiding physical risk more consistently, and showing a disproportionate history of infancy symptoms including chronic constipation, allergy, and disrupted sleep, a cluster the team took as suggestive of a genuinely distinct high reactive phenotype rather than one end of a smooth continuum.

Kagan and his coauthors were explicit that none of this constituted direct evidence of amygdalar or hypothalamic activity, and the honesty of that caveat is worth taking seriously precisely because so much of the paper's later citation history treats the amygdala claim as though it had been demonstrated rather than inferred. The chain of reasoning ran backward from known physiology, the amygdala and hypothalamus sit upstream of the hypothalamic pituitary adrenal axis, the reticular activating system, and the sympathetic nervous system, three circuits that, if their downstream signatures are elevated, imply a lower activation threshold somewhere further up the chain. It is a reasonable inference and, remarkably, one later vindicated by direct measurement, when Carl Schwartz, Christopher Wright, Lisa Shin, and Scott Rauch brought a subset of the original cohort back into a scanner as adults in 2003 and found that those classified as inhibited at two years old showed significantly greater amygdalar response to novel faces than those classified as uninhibited, a fifteen year prophecy fulfilled by direct fMRI evidence in the very same individuals. But in 1988 the amygdala itself was never measured, and the physiological correlations underpinning the claim, while consistent and theoretically coherent, were individually modest, mostly in the .3 to .5 range, drawn from a homogeneous, small, geographically and ethnically narrow sample, and dependent on an aggregate index constructed after the fact by the same investigators who had built the behavioral classification the index was then correlated against. The categorical framing the team preferred, discrete inhibited and uninhibited types rather than points on a continuum, was a genuine empirical choice rather than an obvious one, and Kagan defended it at length by invoking Bohr's principle of complementarity, arguing that whether a phenomenon is best described as continuous or discrete depends on the question being asked rather than on some deeper fact about the phenomenon itself, a strikingly humble epistemic stance for a paper this frequently cited as having settled the matter.

Thalia Eley's route to the genetics of anxiety runs through a different intellectual lineage entirely, the quantitative behavioral genetics tradition that took shape at the Institute of Psychiatry's Social, Genetic and Developmental Psychiatry Centre in the years after Robert Plomin's arrival there in the mid nineteen nineties. Eley trained at University College London's Institute of Child Health on a doctoral thesis examining the aetiology of emotional symptoms in childhood and adolescence, work built on the classical twin design, comparing monozygotic and dizygotic concordance to partition variance into additive genetic, shared environmental, and nonshared environmental components long before a single genotype had been read. She built her early career on cohorts like G1219, a longitudinal twin and sibling sample tracking anxiety and depression across adolescence, and the arc of her subsequent career, MRC fellowships, a readership, and by 2013 a professorship in developmental behavioural genetics, tracks the field's own transformation almost exactly, from twin based heritability estimates that could say a trait ran in families without saying why, to molecular and eventually genome wide methods capable of naming actual loci. She now leads the Emotional Development, Intervention and Treatment lab, whose portfolio spans gene by environment interaction, the genetics of psychological treatment response, and, in Kirstin Purves, a postdoctoral collaborator whose 2020 paper with Eley as senior author represents something close to a culmination of that whole trajectory, an attempt to give anxiety disorders specifically the genome wide treatment that depression, schizophrenia, and bipolar disorder had already received at far greater scale, precisely because anxiety, chronically underfunded relative to its prevalence, had lagged a decade behind.

The design leaned on UK Biobank's extraordinary size to compensate for anxiety genetics' history of underpowered samples. Two overlapping phenotypes were built from more than eighty three thousand participants of European ancestry, aged forty six to eighty. Lifetime Anxiety Disorder identified twenty five thousand four hundred fifty three cases against fifty eight thousand one hundred thirteen controls, defined as anyone self reporting a lifetime professional diagnosis of one of five core anxiety disorders, generalized anxiety, social phobia, panic disorder, agoraphobia, or specific phobia, or meeting DSM-IV criteria for generalized anxiety disorder via a structured interview, with individuals carrying a self reported diagnosis of schizophrenia, bipolar disorder, autism, ADHD, or an eating disorder excluded but comorbid PTSD left in. Current Anxiety Symptoms, a second and more proximal phenotype, used a threshold of ten or higher on the seven item GAD scale, capturing nineteen thousand twelve cases. Genome wide association analysis ran through BGENIE across genotyped and imputed variants, with heritability estimated by BOLT-LMM and genetic correlations computed by LD score regression, the standard toolkit of the post-2015 psychiatric genomics era, and the results were striking on their own terms. SNP based heritability came out to twenty six percent for Lifetime Anxiety Disorder and thirty one percent for Current Anxiety Symptoms on the liability scale, both markedly higher than the seven to fourteen percent range earlier, smaller anxiety studies had reported, a jump the authors attributed candidly to the unusual demographic homogeneity of the UK Biobank sample rather than to any real change in the underlying biology. Five loci reached genome wide significance, a lead signal on chromosome 9p23 also linked in the same dataset to neuroticism and depression, a second on 9q21 sitting inside NTRK2, the gene encoding the receptor for brain derived neurotrophic factor, a third inside TMEM106B on chromosome 7, implicated elsewhere in lysosomal function, and two further intergenic and intronic hits on chromosomes 5 and 3. Gene based analysis added GAD2, which encodes an enzyme synthesizing the inhibitory neurotransmitter GABA, among the most significant genes in the entire scan, a satisfying mechanistic echo of everything this Dispatch has already taught about GABAergic deficits in anxious circuitry, this time arriving from population genetics rather than from a scanner.

The genetic correlations are where the paper does its most consequential and most uncomfortable work. Lifetime Anxiety Disorder correlated genetically with Current Anxiety Symptoms at .93, unsurprising given the overlap in what the two phenotypes are measuring, but it also correlated .78 with major depressive disorder drawn from the Psychiatric Genomics Consortium and .73 with neuroticism measured independently in a separate UK Biobank subsample, alongside significant positive correlations with schizophrenia, bipolar disorder, insomnia, and ADHD. Severity strata within the current symptoms phenotype correlated with each other at .76 to .98, meaning the common genetic signal barely distinguishes mild anxiety from moderate or severe, further evidence for what the authors describe as a single underlying liability distribution running across the whole territory of internalizing psychopathology rather than a set of genetically separable disorders. And then there is the paper's most sobering number, the one least likely to make it into a press release. A polygenic score built from the discovery sample's summary statistics, tested out of sample, explained on average half of one percent of the variance in who actually reported a lifetime anxiety diagnosis. Formal replication of the individual genome wide significant loci across four external cohorts, ANGST, iPSYCH, an independent UK Biobank neuroticism sample, and the depression consortium, succeeded cleanly for only one of the five original hits, the 9p23 signal, with the others showing consistent direction but falling short of a corrected significance threshold, a pattern the authors read as anxiety remaining underpowered even at this scale rather than as evidence the loci were spurious, though the two readings are not easy to tell apart from outside the study.

Set next to each other, these two papers do not so much confirm a shared biological story as expose, with unusual clarity, exactly what each method can and cannot see. Kagan's evidence is concrete, individual, and causally proximate, a specific two year old's heart rate and cortisol and pupil, measured in a specific room, correlating with that same child's behavior years later at a magnitude, .52, .70, that any contemporary genomic study of a complex trait would consider spectacular. But it says almost nothing about mechanism at the level molecular biology actually operates, no gene, no variant, nothing heritable in the technical sense beyond a plausible but untested assumption, and it rests on sixty children of one ethnicity from one American city studied by the same three investigators who built both the predictor and the outcome. Purves and Eley's evidence is the mirror image, genuinely population scale, genuinely molecular, capable in principle of pointing at an actual gene and an actual biochemical pathway, GAD2 and the GABA synthesis it governs sitting right there in the results table. But the phenotype it is built on, a retrospective self report of a lifetime professional diagnosis collected from a person in their sixties or seventies, is a categorical shadow of the rich, prospectively observed, physiologically corroborated construct Kagan spent two decades refining, and the genetic signal it captures correlates so highly with neuroticism, .73, and depression, .78, that it is fair to ask whether the genome, at the resolution current sample sizes allow, can see social anxiety disorder as a distinct thing at all, or whether it sees only a single diffuse liability to negative affect that clinical nosology has since carved, perhaps arbitrarily, into separate diagnostic boxes.

That tension points to a striking thing Kagan noticed and largely set aside in 1987, when he observed that introversion and neuroticism behave as statistically independent dimensions in adult questionnaire data but appear moderately correlated in young children selected at the behavioral extremes, and speculated, almost in passing, that this might reflect a temperament construct unique to early childhood that later differentiates, or dissolves, into the adult personality space Eysenck had already mapped. Thirty two years on, a genome wide association study offers something close to a direct test of that speculation, at least for the adult endpoint, and the answer looks like dissolution rather than differentiation, whatever distinct signal Kagan's physiological battery was picking up in two year olds, by the time it shows up as a diagnosis in a sixty year old's medical history it has become genetically almost indistinguishable from garden variety neuroticism. Nobody has yet built the study that could tell whether that convergence is real biology, an early categorical disposition genuinely blurring into a general trait dimension across development, or an artifact of measurement, a coarse retrospective diagnostic category simply too blunt an instrument to preserve a distinction a stopwatch and a cortisol assay could still detect at age two. The genomic era of psychiatry has, so far, almost entirely skipped over the developmental phenotyping tradition Kagan founded, building its samples instead from whichever large biobank happens to have genotypes and a self report questionnaire attached, cheap and enormous rather than slow and exact. Nobody has run a genome wide association study on prospectively measured behavioral inhibition itself, in a sample large enough to matter, phenotyped the way Kagan phenotyped it, with a heart rate monitor and a cortisol assay and a stranger walking into a room, rather than the way a biobank phenotypes it, with a checkbox for a diagnosis remembered from decades before. Until someone runs that study, the field possesses two of the best pieces of evidence it has ever produced for a biological basis of social anxiety, and no way to know whether they are describing the same thing.
