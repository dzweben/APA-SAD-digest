  • Edition No. 8 · Cross-Cultural and Cross-National Studies · July 27, 2026

    The Fear With No Stem Question

    A hundred and forty two thousand respondents across twenty eight countries were asked, in structured interviews built to detect social anxiety disorder wherever it occurs, whether they feared being watched, judged, or embarrassed in front of others. Not one of them was asked whether they feared embarrassing someone else, and a much smaller study of Japanese and American students had already shown, two decades earlier, exactly how much that omission was going to cost.

  • Edition No. 7 · Learning, Conditioning, and Fear-Acquisition Mechanisms · July 23, 2026

    The Cue That Never Learned to Mean Safe

    A meta-analysis assembled from fifty six years of conditioning chambers found, in 2005, only a weak and statistically fragile hint that anxious patients fail to learn that a given cue is safe. Ten years and roughly double the patients later, the same finding came back robust, no longer a trend but a confirmed deficit in the machinery that is supposed to tell a threatened nervous system when to stand down.

  • Edition No. 6 · Temperament, Genetics, Heritability, and Behavioral Inhibition · July 20, 2026

    The Genome That Cannot Find a Single Shy Child

    A genome-wide study of eighty three thousand adults puts a hard number on how heritable anxiety disorders are, twenty six percent of the liability to develop one, and when that number is turned into an actual predictive score it explains one half of one percent of who does. Thirty two years earlier, a Harvard psychologist armed with nothing more sophisticated than a stopwatch and a cortisol assay showed that watching a two year old approach or avoid a stranger predicts, years later, more than a quarter of the variance in whether that same child is still avoiding strangers.

  • Edition No. 5 · Frontier Neuroscience · July 13, 2026

    The Study That Lost the Amygdala

    The largest harmonized brain scan of anxiety disorders ever assembled found a signature for social anxiety that never once implicates the amygdala, and a separately run reinforcement learning study, built to catch the biased learning rate this publication has already taught twice, came back with that exact parameter clean.

  • Edition No. 4 · Child and Adolescent Social Processing · July 9, 2026

    The Model That Bet on Itself

    A 2005 theory paper from the National Institute of Mental Health predicted the exact circuit signature that adolescent onset social anxiety should show, and three years later, from the same corridor, someone found it, except the prefrontal cortex was not falling silent the way the theory expected. It was reaching out, and nobody yet knows whether that reach is the system trying to help or the disorder itself.

  • Edition No. 3 · Foundational and Landmark Neuroscience · July 6, 2026

    The Paper Everyone Cites, the Paper Nobody Counts

    Social anxiety neuroscience traces its founding claim about the amygdala to two small, mismatched studies from the late nineteen nineties and early two thousands, and the field’s own flagship synthesis quietly excluded the more famous one.

  • Edition No. 2 · Neural Methods and Computational Accounts · June 25, 2026

    The Arithmetic of Self-Doubt

    The brain in social anxiety is not simply overreactive to threat; it is under-responsive to evidence that the threat passed — and the difference between those two failures determines everything about why the disorder persists.

  • Edition No. 1 · Neural Circuits and Mechanisms · June 17, 2026

    The Circuit That Flinches, and the System That Lags

    The amygdala’s case against social anxiety turns out to be less about how loudly it fires and more about whether anything answers in time.
